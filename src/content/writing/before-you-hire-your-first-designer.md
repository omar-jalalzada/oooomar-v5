I have experienced the first designer role from both sides.

At Kin, I was the co-founder and only designer. I designed the product and brand from scratch. At Sublime Security, I started as a design advisor. A year later, I joined full time as the first design hire. Now I lead the team and make the hiring decisions.

The first question I would ask a founder is simple:

> Will more investment in design meaningfully contribute to the success of the business?

The answer should shape everything that follows.

## Start with timing

Today, almost anyone on your team can turn an idea into a rough design. A founder can shape a concept. A PM can build a prototype. An engineer can put something in front of customers. Early on, that may be enough.

I was asked to hire a designer at Sublime well before I did. I said no. The business problems we had at the time did not depend on a full-time designer, and I could still cover the work myself. The role would have existed without a clear reason.

I see two good signals that the timing is right:

1. You can no longer give the design work the time it needs.
2. Better design can materially improve a business outcome you care about.

If neither is true, wait. Keep learning from customers. Use the tools available to you. Revisit the decision when the cost of your current approach becomes clear.

## Decide how much design you need

Once the need is clear, decide how deeply design should be involved.

If the product experience is central to your advantage, find a design co-founder. Give them real ownership of the company and its direction.

If the work is ongoing and tied to clear outcomes, hire a founding designer. Give them enough context and authority to shape the product with you.

If the opportunity still feels unclear, start with an advisor. A good advisor can identify where design will have the most impact, define the role, and help you avoid hiring against a vague idea of “good design.”

This step matters. A full-time hire is an expensive way to discover what you needed.

## Give strong designers a reason to join

Talented designers have options. An early-stage company carries real risk. Your job is to make that risk worth taking.

I think about three things: people, purpose, and pay.

**People.** Are you someone a strong designer would want to build with? Do you invite healthy disagreement? Does your team value customer insight, collaboration, and craft?

**Purpose.** Is the problem meaningful or genuinely interesting? Can the designer see how their work will shape the company?

**Pay.** Are you willing to compensate them for the risk and responsibility?

Founder reputation helps. Clear answers to these questions help more.

## Evaluate craft and compatibility

The craft bar is moving. AI gives everyone more ability to generate designs from intent. PMs and engineers can often produce a reasonable first pass. A seven-out-of-ten solution is becoming easier to reach.

Your first designer needs to raise that ceiling. I look for curiosity, adaptability, and strong UX and UI fundamentals. Their work should show clarity, cohesion, and attention to detail. They should know how to learn quickly and use new tools well.

Compatibility depends on the business.

A consumer company working on reach or retention may need someone who has moved those metrics before. Their work should show fast iteration, experimentation, and measurable results.

A B2B company in a complex domain may need a systems thinker. At Sublime, that means someone who can learn email security, understand customer mental models, and turn complexity into a clear product experience.

“Founding designer” is too broad to be an evaluation criterion. Be specific about the problems this person will own.

## Write the role before opening it

Before you start a search, write a one-page case for the hire. Imagine you are sending it to your investors.

Answer five questions:

1. Why do we need this person now?
2. Which business outcome should their work improve?
3. Which gaps will they own?
4. Where are we willing to compromise?
5. What would excellent work look like after one year?

Fuzzy answers are a reason to pause. Clear answers become the role definition, the evaluation criteria, and the pitch to candidates.

This is often the first work I do with founders. A few focused conversations can clarify the timing, the profile, and the hiring bar before a role ever gets posted.
