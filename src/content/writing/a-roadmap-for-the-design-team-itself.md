When I started planning our design roadmap for the second half of the year, I began with the business.

Sublime builds security software for teams working in a complex and fast-moving environment. Our product gives them a lot of control. That power only becomes useful when people can understand it and use it with confidence.

This is where design creates an advantage for the business.

At the same time, the way we produce design is changing. AI can turn clear intent into a reasonable first draft. Designers can build interactive prototypes. PMs can explore an idea before writing a full spec. Creative teams can generate a one-pager or campaign direction without starting from a blank canvas.

The tools were moving quickly. A project list would leave the underlying system unchanged.

So I framed the roadmap around a different question:

> What should the design team become capable of over the next six months?

That led us to roadmap the process, judgment, tools, rituals, and expectations behind the work.

## The floor, the slope, and the ceiling

I needed a simple way to explain how design’s role was changing. I landed on three layers.

The **floor** is the first draft. Over time, anyone at Sublime should be able to use our systems and tools to turn clear intent into something concrete.

The **slope** is how that draft improves. It includes the process, feedback, standards, and checks that pull work toward our quality bar.

The **ceiling** is the originating work. Designers solve the hardest problems, define new patterns, and show the company what excellent design can be.

This model gave us a clearer division of responsibility. Designers continue to own the ceiling. We also build the slope and make the floor easier to reach.

That changes where the team spends its time. Repetitive mechanics should become faster. Judgment should become more visible. The knowledge inside a few people’s heads should become something the full team can use.

## Product design needed a new development loop

For product design, we wrote a starting hypothesis for a five-stage process:

1. Frame the problem.
2. Explore meaningful directions.
3. Refine with human judgment.
4. Validate with users, partners, or evidence.
5. Specify what engineering needs.

The important change happens at the beginning. We start with writing and thinking. The designer turns product intent into a brief that names the problem, user, business objective, constraints, and success criteria.

The brief gets reviewed before exploration begins.

AI can then help produce a wider set of directions. The designer curates those directions, applies judgment, and develops an interactive prototype. A second critique evaluates the result against the original brief.

This gives critique two different jobs. Brief critique sharpens the thinking. Output critique sharpens the solution.

We also began documenting the judgment required at each stage. How do we frame a useful design brief? How do we map a user’s mental model? What makes an exploration meaningfully different? How do we evaluate hierarchy, interaction patterns, or information architecture?

Each answer can become a reusable skill for a person or an agent. The goal is to make our standards easier to apply while the work is happening.

## Creative design needed its own application

The same strategy looked different for the creative team.

We started with recurring and predictable work: one-pagers, ads, product images, web pages, blog covers, and diagrams. These are good candidates for systemization because the inputs and outputs follow recognizable patterns.

The team would build each workflow for its own use first. We could test the process, find the weak points, and improve the artifacts before opening it to anyone else.

Our principle was simple: automate the mechanics and keep creative judgment with the team.

That means agents can help with generation, resizing, formatting, or production. Designers still decide whether the idea is right, whether the work represents Sublime well, and whether the quality is good enough to ship.

We also made the dependencies explicit. Content should be approved before creative production begins. Each recurring workflow should collect its process, examples, evaluation criteria, and reusable assets in one place.

Product and creative design have different outputs. Both teams benefit when judgment becomes part of the system.

## I had to change my role too

The roadmap also exposed a problem in my own operating model.

I reviewed most product design work before it was ready for engineering. That helped establish the quality bar. It also made me part of the approval chain.

The next stage is peer review as the default quality gate. My role moves toward strategic review, new precedents, major releases, and situations where a designer wants another perspective.

We are making that transition in batches. First, I model what useful critique looks like. Then brief and output critiques become team-owned. The process runs independently once the capability is visible.

That requires clear expectations. We documented what effective product design looks like across problem understanding, customer knowledge, technical depth, design judgment, planning, communication, collaboration, and strategic contribution.

The expectations matter because autonomy without a shared bar creates inconsistency. A visible bar gives people something concrete to grow toward.

## Treat the process as a hypothesis

We were careful to describe this roadmap as a starting hypothesis.

We are testing whether written briefs improve the first critique. We are testing whether AI expands the solution space. We are testing whether peer review can maintain quality. We are testing whether the five stages fit naturally into the company’s product development process.

Real projects are the test cases. After a few projects move through the full loop, we review what worked, where the process added friction, and what needs to change.

This keeps the transformation grounded. We are moving through useful steps and testing each one in the work.

## Questions I would ask when planning again

If I were starting another design roadmap tomorrow, I would ask:

- Where does design judgment live today?
- Which recurring tasks consume time without requiring much judgment?
- Which decisions still depend on the manager?
- What needs to remain distinctly human?
- What can the team test on real work this quarter?
- Which new capability would improve every project that follows?

The projects still matter. Leadership also means improving the system that produces them.

That became the most important part of our roadmap.
