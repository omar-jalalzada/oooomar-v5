# East Asia, 1900–2026

Slice `east-asia`. Japan, China (Shanghai, the People's Republic, Hong Kong, Taiwan) and Korea.

East Asian graphic design is usually told as a footnote: Japan "absorbed" the Bauhaus and Swiss
modernism and added a little Zen. That account gets the direction of travel wrong. Every
tradition here was working with a problem Western modernism never had to solve: a script of
thousands of characters (or, in Korea, an alphabet packed into syllable blocks), set vertically
as often as horizontally, with a living calligraphic culture behind it. The designers below
imported European ideas as tools for *their* problems, and several of the answers (the 1964
pictograms, Muji's emptiness, cross-cultural identity in Hong Kong, the non-square Hangul face)
went back out and changed the field everywhere.

Six movements: four fixed by the registry and two added because they are genuinely distinct
schools with their own principles, not variations.

- `japanese-modernism`, 1951–1990: the Nippon Design Center generation and Tokyo 1964.
- `japanese-poster-avant-garde`, 1960–1975: Yokoo, Awazu, Sugiura, Ishioka and the Shinjuku
  underground.
- `chinese-modern-design`, 1920–now: Shanghai commercial art and covers, the PRC poster, Hong
  Kong identity, the Taiwanese independents, contemporary Chinese book design.
- `korean-modern-design`, 1966–now: the institutionalisation of Korean design and the
  modernisation of Hangul type.
- `japanese-emptiness` (added), 1980–now: Muji and the design of *karappo*, the empty vessel.
- `superflat` (added), 2000–2012: Murakami's flattening of high and low.

## Japanese Modernism {#japanese-modernism}

### Context

Japanese modern graphic design has a pre-war root and a post-war trunk. The root was Yōnosuke
Natori's Nippon Kōbō (founded 1933), where Hiromu Hara and the young Yūsaku Kamekura learned
photo-typographic layout on the multilingual cultural magazine *Nippon*, and the wartime
propaganda magazine *FRONT* (Tōhōsha, from 1942), which Hara art-directed using Soviet-style
photomontage. The skills were modern; the purpose was imperial propaganda, which is why the
post-war generation treated their own modernism as a fresh start.

The trunk begins with the Japan Advertising Artists Club (Nissenbi, 1951), the first
professional body for graphic designers in Japan, and the *Graphic '55* exhibition at Takashimaya
(1955), where Hara, Kamekura, Takashi Kōno, Yoshio Hayakawa, Ryūichi Yamashiro and others hung
their work beside Paul Rand's. Then three things in quick succession turned a craft into a
national project:

1. **The World Design Conference, Tokyo, 11–16 May 1960.** 227 delegates, 84 of them from abroad
   (Louis Kahn among them). Hara chaired the Japanese committee's publicity, and the young
   Metabolist architects launched their manifesto there. Japan's designers saw themselves
   measured against the world for the first time.
2. **The Nippon Design Center (NDC).** Registered in December 1959 and opened in March 1960,
   funded by eight companies (Asahi Beer, Asahi Kasei, Toshiba, Fuji Steel, Nippon Kōkan, Nomura
   Securities, Nippon Kōgaku (Nikon) and Toyota) as a *shared* in-house design department. Kamekura,
   Hara, Yamashiro and Ikkō Tanaka led it; Kazumasa Nagai and the young Tadanori Yokoo were staff.
3. **The Tokyo 1964 Olympics.** Critic Masaru Katsumi art-directed a unified design programme:
   Kamekura's emblem (chosen unanimously from a six-designer invited competition in 1960, with
   lettering by Hara), four official posters, and the first systematic Olympic pictograms. The
   twenty sport pictograms were drawn by Yoshirō Yamashita of NDC in 1963, and 39 facility
   pictograms followed in 1964 from a team of around thirty designers under Katsumi, Kiyoshi
   Awazu and Yokoo among them.

The technology was offset lithography and high-speed photography. Kamekura's sprinter poster
is the first Olympic poster built on a photograph rather than an illustration. The economics
were the high-growth era: corporations needed identities and campaigns at a scale the old
"commercial art" (*shōgyō bijutsu*) studios couldn't provide.

### Principles

1. **One mark carries the whole idea.** Reduce a nation, an event or a company to a single
   geometric statement big enough to fill a poster. *Example:* Kamekura's Tokyo 1964 emblem
   (1960), a red disc above gold Olympic rings and the word TOKYO 1964, nothing else. The disc is
   at once the sun, the Hinomaru and a modern abstract circle.
2. **Photography as proof of modernity.** Use the camera's objectivity, and its technical
   difficulty, as the message. *Example:* Kamekura's second Olympic poster (1962). Osamu Hayasaki
   shot about 80 frames at 1/1000s with a telephoto lens and used strobe lighting on a black
   ground; one frame of sprinters leaving the blocks became the image.
3. **Tradition as geometry, not decoration.** Abstract historical Japanese forms (Rinpa colour
   fields, family crests, Noh masks) into flat geometric elements that work on the international
   modernist grid. *Example:* Ikkō Tanaka, *Nihon Buyō* poster (1981), a geisha's face assembled
   from flat circles, squares and bands of colour.
4. **Design as infrastructure.** Design is a national system serving the public, not just a
   client service. *Example:* the Tokyo 1964 pictograms (1963–64), wordless signs for visitors who
   couldn't read Japanese, given to the public domain afterwards and the direct ancestors of
   Otl Aicher's Munich 1972 set.
5. **The designer as institution-builder.** The movement built its own organisations so that
   design could negotiate with industry as an equal. *Example:* NDC (1960), the Japan Graphic
   Designers Association (JAGDA, 1978, with Kamekura as first president), and Kamekura's magazine
   *Creation* (1989–1993).

### Key works

- *Nippon* magazine, Nippon Kōbō (Natori, Hara, Kamekura), from 1934.
- *FRONT* magazine, Tōhōsha, art direction Hiromu Hara, 1942–1945.
- Nikon F camera nameplate and logotype, Yūsaku Kamekura, 1959.
- Tokyo 1964 Olympic emblem, Kamekura (lettering Hara), 1960; posters 1961–1964.
- Tokyo 1964 sport pictograms, Yoshirō Yamashita / NDC under Masaru Katsumi, 1963.
- Sapporo 1972 Winter Olympics emblem, Kazumasa Nagai, 1966.
- Expo '70 official poster, Shigeo Fukuda, 1967.
- *Victory 1945* poster, Shigeo Fukuda, 1975.
- *Nihon Buyō* poster, Ikkō Tanaka, 1981.
- *Hiroshima Appeals* poster *Burning Butterflies*, Kamekura, 1983 (first of the JAGDA series).
- NTT "Dynamic Loop" identity, Kamekura, 1985.

### Visual vocabulary

Large flat primary forms, especially the circle, on white or black grounds. A strong preference
for red, gold, black and white, the colours of lacquer and the flag. Helvetica and geometric
sans for Latin text (the 1964 posters set TOKYO 1964 in it). Japanese text in carefully drawn
Gothic (*kaku-gothic*) or Mincho, mostly kept small and subordinate to the image. Photography
used for its objectivity and freeze-frame speed. Compositions are symmetrical more often than
Swiss work is: one central figure, generous margins. Fukuda adds the visual pun, figure-ground
reversal, the impossible object.

### Lineage

From: the Bauhaus (Kamekura studied at Renshichirō Kawakita's Institute of New Architecture and
Industrial Arts, 1935–37, set up to teach Bauhaus principles), the New Typography (Hara's
"our new typography"), Constructivist photomontage (*FRONT*), Cassandre and Art Deco (Kamekura's
first idol), American modernism (Paul Rand in *Graphic '55*) and Swiss style.

Into: Munich 1972's pictogram system, the global corporate-identity practice of the 1970s and
1980s, the Japanese poster avant-garde (which defined itself *against* it), and
`japanese-emptiness` (Tanaka's Muji, Kenya Hara at NDC).

### Quotes

No verified quote is recorded here for the movement as a whole. Kamekura's widely circulated
remark about the 1964 disc being "the sun" rather than the Hinomaru appears in secondary
sources without a primary citation, so it is paraphrased here and not quoted.

### Remix today

Take the discipline of the single statement: one form, one colour relationship, one idea, and
everything else cut. Take the pictograms too. Tokyo's real legacy is the idea that an event
identity is a *public information system* first and a brand second. For a 2026 designer
building a design system, NDC is also a model worth studying: a shared studio funded by
several companies so that none of them had to settle for mediocre in-house work.

### Hiromu Hara {#hiromu-hara}

**1903–1986. Typographer, book designer, educator.** The bridge from pre-war European typography
to the post-war profession. Hara was trained in printing at the Tokyo Prefectural School of
Arts and Crafts (graduated 1921) and taught there through the 1930s. He championed the New
Typography as "our new typography" (*bokutachi no shin-kappanjutsu*), co-founded Nippon Kōbō with
Natori (1933), art-directed *FRONT* (1942–45), co-founded NDC (1960), and drew the lettering for
the Tokyo 1964 emblem and for "EXPO'70" (1966). He designed nearly 3,000 publications, which
earned him the critic Masaru Katsumi's title "emperor of book design", and from 1959 to 1972 he
developed papers with the paper merchant Takeo (Pandora, Sable, Flocken).

**Principles**

- **Typography that serves the client, not the author.** Hara built designs logically, in place
  of a signature style. *Example:* the *Shōwa Bungaku Zenshū* collected-literature series for
  Kadokawa (1952–55), a uniform, legible system across dozens of volumes.
- **Paper is part of the design.** The substrate is designed, not chosen from stock. *Example:*
  the Takeo paper series he developed from 1959, which won the 1961 Mainichi Industrial Design
  Prize.
- **Modern means, any message.** *FRONT* (1942) shows the skill of the New Typography put to
  wartime propaganda. It is the uncomfortable lesson in his career: the form doesn't carry the
  ethics.

**Key works:** *FRONT*, 1942–45 (magazine); *Shōwa Bungaku Zenshū*, 1952–55 (books); Tokyo 1964
emblem lettering, 1960 (identity); EXPO'70 lettering, 1966 (identity).

### Yusaku Kamekura {#yusaku-kamekura}

**1915–1997. Graphic designer, art director.** Nicknamed "Boss" for his stature. He studied at
Kawakita's Bauhaus-inspired institute (1935–37), worked under Natori on *Nippon* from 1938,
co-founded Nissenbi (1951) and NDC (1960), was JAGDA's first president (1978), and edited
*Creation* (1989–93).

**Principles**

- **Geometry plus light.** Pure geometric forms made to glow through colour gradation and
  photography. *Example:* the Nikon logotype and Nikon F nameplate (1959).
- **Photograph the decisive instant.** *Example:* the Tokyo 1964 sprinter poster (1962), the
  first Olympic poster built on a photograph.
- **The mark as a monument.** Identities designed to outlast the companies' own strategies.
  *Example:* NTT's Dynamic Loop (1985), still in use.
- **Catalogue the world's marks.** He studied identity as a discipline. *Example:* *Trademarks
  and Symbols of the World* (1965), with a preface by Paul Rand.

**Key works:** Nikon F logo, 1959 (identity); Tokyo 1964 emblem, 1960 (identity); Tokyo 1964
poster no. 2, 1962 (poster); TDK logo, 1967 (identity); *Burning Butterflies*, Hiroshima
Appeals, 1983 (poster); NTT, 1985 (identity).

### Ikko Tanaka {#ikko-tanaka}

**1930–2002. Graphic designer, art director.** Trained in Kyoto (Kyoto City University of Arts,
1950), worked at Sankei Shimbun in Osaka, moved to Tokyo in 1957, joined NDC in 1960, opened his
own studio in 1963 and became creative director of the Seibu (later Saison) group from 1975.
He was the first art director of Muji (1980), with Kazuko Koike and Takashi Sugimoto.

**Principles**

- **Rinpa, rebuilt from geometry.** The flat colour and gold grounds of the 17th-century Rinpa
  school, reconstructed from circles, squares and bands. *Example:* *Nihon Buyō* poster (1981).
- **Letters as images.** Characters treated as picture elements and layered in colour. *Example:*
  his series of typographic posters for the Morisawa type foundry.
- **Design as cultural programming.** The art director as curator of a whole culture of shops,
  theatres and museums. *Example:* the Seibu Museum of Art (1975–86) and the Saison group's
  identity, including the Loft mark (1987).
- **The no-brand brand.** *Example:* Muji (1980), with brown kraft paper, clear cellophane and red
  type in place of a logo.

**Key works:** Issey Miyake brand mark, 1979 (identity); *Japan Style*, V&A, 1980 (exhibition
and book); Muji, 1980 (identity); *Nihon Buyō*, 1981 (poster); Expo '85 Tsukuba symbol, 1985
(identity); Ginza Graphic Gallery symbol, 1986 (identity).

### Kazumasa Nagai {#kazumasa-nagai}

**1929–2026. Graphic designer, printmaker.** He left sculpture at Tokyo University of the Arts
for health reasons, designed advertising for Daiwa Spinning from 1951, was a founding member of
NDC (1960) and its president from 1975 to 1986, and succeeded Kamekura as JAGDA president
(1994). He died on 23 February 2026, aged 96.

**Principles**

- **Abstraction as cosmology.** In his early career, line-engraved geometric fields suggest
  energy and space in place of objects. *Example:* the Sapporo 1972 Winter Olympics emblem
  (1966), a snow crystal, the rising sun and the rings.
- **Turn from abstraction to living creatures.** From the late 1980s, hand-drawn animals with
  staring eyes rendered in fine ornamental line. *Example:* the *Life* poster series (from 1995)
  and *Save Nature* (1995).
- **The quiet corporate mark.** *Example:* Asahi Breweries symbol (1986) and the Mitsubishi UFJ
  Financial Group symbol (2004).

**Key works:** Sapporo 1972 emblem, 1966 (identity); Asahi Breweries, 1986 (identity); JR Group
mark (supervision), 1986 (identity); *Life* series, 1995 (poster).

### Shigeo Fukuda {#shigeo-fukuda}

**1932–2009. Graphic designer, sculptor.** Called "the Japanese Escher". He graduated from Tokyo
University of the Arts (1956), went freelance in 1958, won the 1967 competition for the Expo '70
poster and drew the fair's pictograms for lost children, and won the grand prize at the 1975
Warsaw poster competition for *Victory 1945*. He was JAGDA's third president (2000).

**Principles**

- **One visual twist does all the work.** Reduce the argument to a single impossible image.
  *Example:* *Victory 1945* (1975), a shell flying back into the barrel of the cannon that fired
  it.
- **Figure–ground ambiguity.** Two readings in one silhouette. *Example:* *Happy Earth Day*
  (1982), an axe whose handle sprouts a living twig.
- **Illusion leaves the page.** The same idea built in three dimensions. *Example:*
  *Lunch with a Helmet On* (1987), cutlery welded so that its shadow is a motorcycle.

**Key works:** Expo '70 poster, 1967 (poster); *Victory 1945*, 1975 (poster); *Amnesty
International*, 1980 (poster); *Lunch with a Helmet On*, 1987 (other).

## Japanese Poster Avant-garde {#japanese-poster-avant-garde}

### Context

The NDC consensus was barely formed before a younger generation broke it. The 1960 renewal of
the US–Japan Security Treaty (Anpo) had brought hundreds of thousands into the streets; the
same year saw the World Design Conference celebrate a corporate, internationalist design.
Through the 1960s Tokyo's underground (Shūji Terayama's Tenjō Sajiki theatre (1967), Tatsumi
Hijikata's *ankoku butō*, Nagisa Ōshima's films, the Sōgetsu Art Center's concerts with John
Cage and David Tudor) needed posters, and its designers had no interest in looking Swiss.

The founding gesture is Tadanori Yokoo's self-portrait poster for the *Persona* exhibition at
Matsuya Ginza (1965), a modernist designer staging his own suicide. The movement drew on
everything modernism had thrown out: ukiyo-e, Meiji-era advertising, the Rising Sun, manga,
pin-ups, Push Pin Studio's revived Victoriana, American psychedelia. Its medium was silkscreen
(cheap, vivid, run in small editions for theatres) and offset with fluorescent and metallic
inks. Its venues were coffee-shop walls in Shinjuku.

The avant-garde is also where the *book* and the *magazine* became a serious design medium in
Japan, through Kōhei Sugiura. It ends around 1975: the political energy faded, Yokoo turned to
painting (he declared himself a painter after MoMA's 1980 Picasso retrospective), and Eiko
Ishioka's Parco campaigns carried its eroticism and theatricality into advertising.

### Principles

1. **Pastiche as critique.** Collage the national symbols modernism suppressed (Rising Sun,
   Mount Fuji, Hokusai's wave) so that they undercut themselves. *Example:* Yokoo, *Tadanori
   Yokoo* (1965). The Rising Sun dominates; a Shinkansen and an atomic cloud break through Fuji;
   a baby photo of the designer sits under a hanged man.
2. **The vernacular is the avant-garde.** Folk motifs, festival printing and the rural are
   brought into the city as a political act. *Example:* Kiyoshi Awazu's posters for Shingeki
   theatre and his film art direction for Masahiro Shinoda's *Double Suicide* (1969).
3. **Maximum density.** Fill the sheet; let colour vibrate; refuse white space as ideology.
   *Example:* Yokoo's poster *A la Maison de M. Civeçawa* (1966) for Hijikata's butō company,
   with Shibusawa, Hijikata and Kazuo Ōno threaded through roses, the Rising Sun and Hokusai's
   waves.
4. **The page as a field of energy.** Information design and book design as fields of
   vibration and pattern rather than neutral containers. *Example:* Kōhei Sugiura's covers for
   *Ongaku Geijutsu* (1960–63), generated from sequences in Messiaen's *Mode de valeurs et
   d'intensités* and twelve-tone rows, reprinted from the same plates in shifting colours.
5. **Political engagement through affect.** Posters aim at the gut rather than at information.
   *Example:* Awazu, *Umi o Kaese* (*Give Back Our Oceans*, 1955), a fisherman's face cut by
   barbed wire, made in solidarity with fishermen excluded from Kujūkuri Beach by the US
   occupation forces.

### Key works

- *Umi o Kaese*, Kiyoshi Awazu, 1955.
- *Metabolism 1960* manifesto booklet, design Kiyoshi Awazu, 1960.
- *Ongaku Geijutsu* covers, Kōhei Sugiura, 1960–1963.
- *Tadanori Yokoo* (*Having reached a climax at the age of 29, I was dead*), Yokoo, 1965.
- *A la Maison de M. Civeçawa*, Yokoo, 1966.
- *Experimental Music of Japan '69* record jacket, Sugiura, 1969.
- Textile Pavilion, Expo '70, design Yokoo, 1970.
- Parco advertising campaigns, art direction Eiko Ishioka, 1970s (Mainichi Industrial Design
  Prize 1975).
- *Den Shingon-in Ryōkai Mandara* (book), Sugiura, 1977.

### Visual vocabulary

Silkscreen flats in fluorescent pink, acid green, gold and silver. Hand-drawn outlines borrowed
from ukiyo-e and Meiji print, collaged with halftone photographs. Radiating sun-ray grounds.
Type is hand-lettered or set in heavy Mincho, often vertical, frequently stacked in boxes.
English slogans are used as a foreign texture ("Made in Japan"). In Sugiura's work: dense
vertical Mincho settings, diagrams warped by time instead of distance, mandalic symmetry.

### Lineage

From: Push Pin Studio (Yokoo's early work shows Glaser and Chwast), Dada collage, Ben Shahn
and Herbert Bayer (Awazu's self-taught education), ukiyo-e and Meiji advertising, and the Ulm
School (Sugiura taught there 1964–67 and came back convinced of the difference of Asian
thought). Against: NDC modernism.

Into: Western psychedelic and postmodern work (MoMA collected Yokoo in 1967 and gave him a solo
show in 1972), Ishioka's Hollywood and Broadway career, Chinese and Korean book design through
Sugiura's students (Lu Jingren above all), and `superflat`, which recycled the same flattening
of high and low thirty years later.

### Quotes

- The text on Yokoo's 1965 self-portrait poster reads, in English: "Made in Japan / Having
  reached a climax at the age of 29, I was dead." (Tadanori Yokoo, *Tadanori Yokoo*, poster,
  1965; collection MoMA.)

### Remix today

The lesson is permission: your own visual culture, including its embarrassing and kitsch parts,
is material, not a liability to be designed away. The specific techniques transfer: collage of
national symbols, maximum density, fluorescent and metallic spot colour. Sugiura's
"self-proliferation" (one plate pattern re-run through rotations and colourways to make a whole
series) is generative design done with printing plates, and maps directly onto a parametric
cover system.

### Tadanori Yokoo {#tadanori-yokoo}

**Born 1936. Graphic designer, painter.** From Nishiwaki, Hyōgo. He worked at the Kobe Shimbun,
moved to Tokyo in 1960 and joined NDC, worked on the 1964 Olympic pictograms, co-founded Tenjō
Sajiki with Terayama (1967), and had a MoMA solo show in 1972. He became a painter in the 1980s.
The Yokoo Tadanori Museum of Contemporary Art opened in Kobe in 2012.

**Principles**

- **The self as subject.** The designer's own biography is the poster's content. *Example:*
  *Tadanori Yokoo* (1965).
- **Kitsch and the sacred side by side.** Pin-ups, Buddhist imagery and the Rising Sun in one
  plane, given equal weight. *Example:* *A la Maison de M. Civeçawa* (1966).
- **Restless restyling.** He refuses a fixed style; his 2019 self-curated retrospective, *Jiga
  Jison* ("self-indulgence, self-harm"), hung deliberately incompatible works together.

**Key works:** *Tadanori Yokoo*, 1965 (poster); *A la Maison de M. Civeçawa*, 1966 (poster);
Expo '70 Textile Pavilion, 1970 (building).

### Kiyoshi Awazu {#kiyoshi-awazu}

**1929–2009. Graphic designer, filmmaker.** Self-taught. He worked in Nikkatsu's publicity
department (1954–58), won the 1955 Nissenbi prize with *Umi o Kaese*, joined and designed for
the Metabolist group (1960), taught at Musashino Art University, produced exhibits for Expo '70
and Tsukuba '85, and was first chairman of the Printing Museum, Tokyo (2000–05).

**Principles**

- **Design that sides with the dispossessed.** *Example:* *Umi o Kaese* (1955).
- **Reproduction as method.** Recurring motifs (fingerprints, turtles, Sada Abe) reprinted and
  transformed across decades, treating graphic design as an art of duplication.
- **Graphics at architectural scale.** Supergraphics wrapping a building. *Example:* the
  exterior of Minoru Takeyama's Nibankan building, Shinjuku (1970), which appeared on the cover
  of Charles Jencks's *The Language of Post-Modern Architecture* (1977).

**Key works:** *Umi o Kaese*, 1955 (poster); *Metabolism 1960*, 1960 (book); *Double Suicide*
art direction, 1969 (film); *A Diversity of Birds*, Hiroshima Appeals, 1984 (poster).

### Kohei Sugiura {#kohei-sugiura}

**Born 1932. Graphic designer, book designer, researcher of Asian iconography.** He trained as an
architect at Tokyo University of the Arts (1955), won the 1955 Nissenbi prize for an LP jacket,
was a panellist at the 1960 World Design Conference and a finalist in the 1964 emblem
competition, taught at Ulm (1964–67) and at Kobe Design University (1987–2002), and has directed
its Research Institute for Asian Design since 2010.

**Principles**

- **Self-proliferating design.** One generative pattern, re-run in rotations and colours, produces a
  series. *Example:* *Ongaku Geijutsu* covers (1960–63).
- **The cover is a face.** The cover answers the contents. *Example:* his magazine covers for
  *SD* and *Toshi Jūtaku*, from the late 1960s.
- **Maps of experience.** Diagrams deformed by time rather than distance. *Example:* his
  Time-Distance Maps, which established the diagram as a branch of Japanese visual
  communication.
- **The book as a three-dimensional building.** Design begins from the body text, set
  vertically in Mincho, and works out to the binding. *Example:* *Den Shingon-in Ryōkai Mandara*
  (Heibonsha, 1977), which won the Leipzig special honour award (1982).

**Key works:** *Ongaku Geijutsu* covers, 1960–63 (magazine); *Experimental Music of Japan '69*,
1969 (other); *Den Shingon-in Ryōkai Mandara*, 1977 (book); *Mandala* exhibition, 1980
(exhibition).

### Eiko Ishioka {#eiko-ishioka}

**1938–2012. Art director, graphic and costume designer.** She graduated from Tokyo University
of the Arts and joined Shiseido's advertising division (1961), went independent in 1970 and
art-directed Parco through the 1970s, then moved to New York in the 1980s. Her later work:
production design for Paul Schrader's *Mishima* (1985, Cannes artistic contribution prize), the
cover of Miles Davis's *Tutu* (1986, Grammy for best album package), an Academy Award for the
costumes of *Bram Stoker's Dracula* (1992), and costumes for the Beijing 2008 opening ceremony.

**Principles**

- **Advertise an attitude, not a product.** Parco's campaigns sold a stance (female independence,
  cosmopolitanism, eroticism) and showed almost no merchandise. *Example:* Parco campaigns of the
  1970s, Mainichi prize 1975.
- **Total art direction.** Poster, film, set and costume as one designed world. *Example:*
  *Mishima: A Life in Four Chapters* (1985).
- **Uncompromising originality.** Her motto, the title of her 2020–21 retrospective at the
  Museum of Contemporary Art Tokyo, asked whether blood, sweat and tears could be designed.

**Key works:** Parco campaigns, 1975 (poster); *Mishima*, 1985 (film); *Tutu*, 1986 (other);
*Bram Stoker's Dracula* costumes, 1992 (film).

## Chinese Modern Design {#chinese-modern-design}

### Context

"Chinese modern design" covers four distinct settings under one registry id. Each had its own
politics, market and printing technology, and the four are only now being written up as one
history (M+ in Hong Kong, opened in November 2021, is the first museum to collect across all of
them).

**Shanghai, 1910s–1937.** The treaty port was China's printing and publishing capital. Two
graphic cultures grew there. The first was the *yuefenpai* (calendar poster): a commercial
poster for cigarettes, medicines and insurance, painted in a "rubbed charcoal and watercolour"
technique credited to Zheng Mantuo (from 1914) and perfected in the studio of Hang Zhiying, its
subjects modern women in *qipao*. It peaked in the 1920s and 1930s, financed above all by
British American Tobacco and the Nanyang Brothers. The second was the modernist book cover
and pictorial magazine, centred on the New Culture writers around Lu Xun. Lu Xun commissioned
Tao Yuanqing (1893–1929) for covers such as *Panghuang* (*Wandering*, 1926). Qian Juntao, art
editor of the Kaiming Bookstore from 1927, was so prolific he was nicknamed "Qian Covers".
Zhang Guangyu's *Shanghai Sketch* (*Shanghai Manhua*, 1928–30) and *Modern Sketch* (*Shidai
Manhua*, 1934–37) fused Art Deco, Constructivism, Mexican muralism and Chinese folk art. The
Japanese invasion of 1937 ended this world.

**The People's Republic, 1949–1980s.** Mao called on painters to serve the revolution. The
*xuanchuanhua* (propaganda poster) at first mixed *nianhua* (New Year print) traditions with
political content: many Shanghai calendar painters simply changed subject. It moved towards Soviet
socialist realism, then back to a "Chinese style" during the Great Leap Forward, and peaked in the
Cultural Revolution (1966–76). Liu Chunhua's painting *Chairman Mao Goes to Anyuan* (1967) was
reproduced on the order of 900 million times. Production was centralised in houses like the
Shanghai People's Fine Arts Publishing House. On 1 January 1956 every newspaper in the PRC
switched from vertical to horizontal setting, a typographic revolution by decree.

**Hong Kong, 1960s–1997.** The colony's design scene had a modernist founder figure in Henry
Steiner (arrived 1961) and a generation of Chinese designers trained in evening classes by
Wucius Wong and Chung Pui-ching at the Chinese University's extramural department, Kan
Tai-keung among them. Hong Kong design became the art of being *between*: Western corporate
identity and typography meeting Chinese symbolism and ink. The 1997 handover gave it a
deadline and a subject.

**Taiwan and the contemporary mainland, 1970s–now.** Taiwan's design scene grew around
groups such as the Amoeba Design Association (Wang Hsing-kung joined it in 1974) and,
from the 2000s, independents working in record packaging, publishing and politics, Aaron Nieh
the best known. On the mainland the reform era reopened contact with Japan and Hong Kong. Lu
Jingren, trained with Sugiura, rebuilt Chinese *book design* (*shuji sheji*) as a discipline
distinct from mere cover decoration (*zhuangzhen*); Shenzhen, next to Hong Kong, became the
mainland's design city.

### Principles

1. **Commerce as modernity's first language.** In China, modern visual culture arrived through
   advertising before it arrived through art schools. *Example:* Shanghai *yuefenpai* calendar
   posters of the 1920s–30s, modern women, products and a calendar on one sheet, hung at home for
   a year.
2. **The cover as manifesto.** The book jacket was where the New Culture movement looked
   modern, with abstract motifs, hand-drawn characters and asymmetry. *Example:* Tao Yuanqing's
   cover for Lu Xun's *Panghuang* (1926), three abstracted figures under a sun, lettering drawn to
   match.
3. **Satire through the graphic line.** Caricature and photomontage as political commentary
   under censorship. *Example:* Zhang Guangyu's covers for *Shanghai Sketch* (from 1928) and his
   *Journey to the West* caricature cycle (1945), a myth used to lampoon the wartime
   government.
4. **Image as mass mobilisation.** One authorised image, reproduced without limit, with
   saturated red, heroic upturned faces and radiant light. *Example:* *Chairman Mao Goes to Anyuan*
   (1967).
5. **Cross-cultural synthesis.** Identity built by translating Chinese forms (coins, characters,
   creatures) into modernist geometry for an international audience. *Example:* Kan Tai-keung's
   Bank of China symbol (1980), an ancient coin with a square hole that also reads as 中.

### Key works

- *Panghuang* cover for Lu Xun, Tao Yuanqing, 1926.
- Kaiming Bookstore covers, Qian Juntao, from 1927.
- *Shanghai Sketch* (*Shanghai Manhua*), Zhang Guangyu and the Shanghai Sketch Society,
  1928–1930.
- *Modern Sketch* (*Shidai Manhua*), editor Lu Shaofei, 1934–1937.
- *Journey to the West* caricature cycle, Zhang Guangyu, 1945.
- *Havoc in Heaven* (*Danao Tiangong*), art design Zhang Guangyu, Wan brothers' animation,
  1961–64.
- *Chairman Mao Goes to Anyuan*, Liu Chunhua, 1967 (mass-reproduced poster).
- Bank of China symbol, Kan Tai-keung, 1980.
- HSBC hexagon identity, Henry Steiner, 1983.
- *East Meets West* exhibition, Alan Chan, Tokyo, 1991.
- *Democracy at 4am* poster campaign, Aaron Nieh, 2014.

### Visual vocabulary

Shanghai: rubbed-watercolour realism for calendar girls; for covers and magazines, flat
woodcut-like silhouettes, Deco geometry, hand-drawn display characters with exaggerated
stroke contrast, and photomontage. PRC: red dominant with yellow, the sun-burst, low
viewpoints, block Song and hand-lettered characters in slogans, and in the Cultural Revolution
woodcut and *dazibao* (big-character poster) roughness. Hong Kong: modernist sans and grids
with one Chinese element per mark (coin, chop, character, creature), ink-wash fields placed
like abstract painting, often bilingual layouts that treat English and Chinese as two equal
voices. Contemporary: extreme typographic play with characters, layered and fragmented
(Nieh), and books where paper, binding and grid are designed as one object (Lu).

### Lineage

From: Art Deco and the commercial poster (*yuefenpai*), Constructivism and German Expressionist
woodcut (via Lu Xun's woodcut movement), Soviet socialist realism, Japanese modernism (Sugiura's
teaching; Fukuda's ties to China and Taiwan), Swiss style and American corporate identity
(Steiner was Paul Rand's student at Yale).

Into: a global language of cross-cultural identity (Steiner's *Cross-Cultural Design*, 1994),
the "East meets West" style of 1990s luxury and hospitality branding, and the contemporary
Chinese book as an art object.

### Quotes

- Henry Steiner, on the outsider's role in cross-cultural design: "Ultimately, it is the context
  of a design which makes it cross-cultural and this is determined by the designer's attitude.
  You don't have to be an outsider but it helps." (Henry Steiner, *Graphic Communications:
  Essays on Design*, Rennie's Mill Press, 2021.)

### Remix today

Two lessons. From Shanghai: commercial work was where modernity was *invented* in China, so a
designer in 2026 shouldn't treat commerce as the lesser brief. From Hong Kong: identity can be
built by translation, one element from each culture, held in tension, not blended to beige.
Steiner's working rule is still the sharpest test of a "global" brand: is each culture
represented by a real, specific form, or by a cliché?

### Zhang Guangyu {#zhang-guangyu}

**1900–1965. Cartoonist, illustrator, art designer.** From Wuxi. He worked in the advertising
departments of Nanyang Brothers Tobacco (1921–25) and British American Tobacco, co-led the
Shanghai Sketch Society (1927), relaunched *Shanghai Sketch* in 1928, and was a central
contributor to *Modern Sketch* (1934–37). After the war he moved to Hong Kong (1946), returned to
Beijing in 1949 to teach at the Central Academy of Arts and Crafts, and was art designer of the
animated feature *Havoc in Heaven*. Neglected after his death, he was revived in the 1990s
through the advocacy of Zhang Ding and Ding Cong.

**Principles**

- **Synthesis without hierarchy.** Bauhaus, Constructivism, Mexican murals, German
  Expressionism and Chinese folk art in one decorative language. *Example:* *Modern Sketch*
  covers (1934–37).
- **Myth as political weapon.** Classical stories retold to satirise the present. *Example:*
  *Journey to the West* caricature cycle (1945), 60 captioned paintings in ten chapters.
- **Design across every medium.** Furniture, covers, stamps, film. *Example:* *Havoc in Heaven*
  (1961–64), its characters drawn like opera masks and folk prints.

**Key works:** *Shanghai Sketch*, 1928 (magazine); *Modern Sketch* covers, 1934 (magazine);
*Journey to the West*, 1945 (book); *Havoc in Heaven*, 1961 (film); *Modern Arts and Crafts*
(*Jindai Gongyi Meishu*) (book; date not verified).

### Qian Juntao {#qian-juntao}

**1907–1998. Book cover designer, seal carver, calligrapher.** Born in Tongxiang, Zhejiang. He
was art editor at the Kaiming Bookstore in Shanghai from 1927 to 1934, credited with covers for
writers of the New Culture circle including Lu Xun, and became editor-in-chief at Wanye
Bookstore (1938). After 1949 he edited at the Shanghai and Beijing music publishing houses and
was vice-president of the Xiling Seal Society.

**Principles**

- **The seal carver's economy.** The compression of a carved seal (a few strokes, a square field)
  brought to the cover. *Example:* Kaiming Bookstore covers (from 1927).
- **Western pattern, Chinese structure.** Art Nouveau and Deco ornament borrowed for borders
  and grounds while the title characters stay hand-drawn and Chinese.
- **Volume as craft.** Hundreds of covers made cover design a recognised craft in China, not a
  printer's afterthought.

**Key works:** Kaiming Bookstore cover designs, 1927 (book); *Long March Seal Album* (*Changzheng
Yinpu*), Shanghai People's Fine Arts (book; date not verified).

### Henry Steiner {#henry-steiner}

**Born 1934. Graphic designer.** Born Hans Steiner in Vienna; his family fled the Anschluss to
New York in 1939. He studied under Paul Rand at Yale (MFA), went to the Sorbonne as a Fulbright
scholar (1958), and in 1961 came to Hong Kong on a nine-month contract to launch *The Asia
Magazine*. He stayed and founded Graphic Communication Ltd. He is called the "father of Hong Kong
graphic design"; M+ gave him a retrospective in 2024.

**Principles**

- **Cross-cultural design: one element from each side.** A Western structure paired with a
  specific Chinese form, never a pastiche. *Example:* the Hongkong Land H mark, which combines
  the character for longevity with a floor plan.
- **Equity over novelty.** Refresh an inherited mark without throwing it away. *Example:* the
  Hong Kong Jockey Club monogram, redrawn when the club dropped "Royal" ahead of 1997.
- **Banknotes as public storytelling.** *Example:* the Standard Chartered Hong Kong notes, which
  have no portraits and use a hierarchy of mythical creatures from aquatic to celestial.

**Key works:** HSBC hexagon, 1983 (identity); *Cross-Cultural Design*, 1994 (book); Hong Kong
Jockey Club, 1996 (identity; year approximate, before the 1997 rename); Standard Chartered 150-dollar
note, 2009 (other).

### Kan Tai-keung {#kan-tai-keung}

**Born 1942. Graphic designer, ink painter.** Born in Panyu, Guangzhou; moved to Hong Kong in
1957 and worked as a tailor for a decade. He studied design in Wucius Wong's extramural classes
at the Chinese University (1966–69) and ink painting with Lui Shou-kwan, rose to design
director at Chung Pui-ching's studio, and co-founded his own firm in 1976 (later Kan & Lau, now
KL&K). He was the first designer named one of Hong Kong's Ten Outstanding Young Persons (1979).

**Principles**

- **Ink as a design element.** Literati ink-wash brought into the modernist poster as a pure
  field of tone. *Example:* his ink-and-typography posters of the 1980s–90s.
- **Archaic form, modern geometry.** *Example:* the Bank of China symbol (1980), a coin with a
  square hole read as 中 and as "round heaven, square earth".
- **The city as a character.** *Example:* the Chongqing city identity (adopted 2006), two
  joyful figures forming 庆, "double celebration".

**Key works:** Bank of China symbol, 1980 (identity); Hong Kong Baptist University emblem, 1994
(identity); Chongqing city identity, 2006 (identity).

### Alan Chan {#alan-chan}

**Born 1950. Graphic and interior designer, brand consultant.** He trained in a ten-month evening
course at First Institute of Art and Design (1969) and spent a decade in multinational agencies
before co-founding his own agency (1980) and founding Alan Chan Design Company (1986). He was the
first Chinese designer given solo shows at Tokyo's Creation Gallery G8 (1991) and Ginza Graphic
Gallery (2002), and joined AGI in 2014.

**Principles**

- **"East meets West" (*dongqing xiyun*).** Chinese decorative vocabulary, from tea culture to
  old Shanghai posters, set in Western layouts for consumer brands. *Example:* *East Meets West*
  exhibition, Tokyo (1991).
- **Nostalgia as a brand asset.** Shanghai-era graphics reissued as products and packaging, which
  fed the 1990s fashion for Old Shanghai.
- **The designer as collector.** His work comes out of a collection of antiques and ephemera; his
  own 27 Gallery shows design-led art.

**Key works:** *East Meets West*, Creation Gallery G8, 1991 (exhibition); *East Meets West*, Ginza
Graphic Gallery, 2002 (exhibition); *Art of Living*, Hong Kong Heritage Museum, 2003
(exhibition).

### Lu Jingren {#lu-jingren}

**Born 1947. Book designer, illustrator, educator.** Born in Shanghai. He worked at China Youth
Publishing House and studied with Kōhei Sugiura in Japan, then founded his own studio in
Beijing. He is credited with moving Chinese practice from *zhuangzhen* (binding and decoration)
to *shuji sheji* (book design as a whole object).

**Principles**

- **The book is designed from the inside out.** Text, grid, paper and binding are one
  structure, Sugiura's method transplanted to Chinese.
- **Material as meaning.** Wood, thread, rice paper and traditional binding used as structural
  argument, not decoration. *Example:* *Zhu Xi Bangshu Qianziwen* (*Zhu Xi's Thousand Character
  Classic in Large Script*), commonly dated 1999.
- **Teaching the field.** Workshops and writing that produced a generation of mainland book
  designers.

**Key works:** *Zhu Xi Bangshu Qianziwen*, 1999 (book; year from secondary sources, not
independently verified).

### Aaron Nieh {#aaron-nieh}

**Born 1977. Graphic designer.** Born in Taichung; studied at National Taiwan University of
Science and Technology. He made his name with hundreds of Mandopop album packages (three Golden
Melody Awards for packaging) and in 2013 became the first Taiwanese member of AGI.

**Principles**

- **Characters as malleable material.** Chinese characters sliced, layered and distorted until
  they are image and text at once. *Example:* the main visual for the 50th Golden Horse Awards
  (2013).
- **Design as civic action.** Crowdfunded political design. *Example:* *Democracy at 4am* (2014),
  during the Sunflower Movement, funded to run in *Apple Daily* and *The New York Times*.
- **Political identity as cultural identity.** *Example:* Tsai Ing-wen's 2016 presidential
  campaign identity.

**Key works:** 50th Golden Horse Awards visual, 2013 (identity); *Democracy at 4am*, 2014
(poster); Tsai Ing-wen campaign, 2016 (identity).

## Korean Modern Design {#korean-modern-design}

### Context

Korean graphic design was shaped by two facts. Colonial rule (1910–45) and the Korean War
(1950–53) meant a profession built almost from scratch in the 1960s. And the national script,
Hangul, is an alphabet promulgated in 1446 whose letters are assembled into square syllable
blocks, so a Hangul typeface needs thousands of block designs, a problem shared with Chinese and
Japanese, but built from just 24 letters.

The institutions came first. In 1966 the government launched the Korean Commercial and
Industrial Art Exhibition (Sanggong Mijeon), which separated design from the crafts section of
the national art exhibition. In 1972 Cho Young-jae, Yang Seung-choon, Kim Kyo-man and
colleagues founded the Korea Society of Graphic Design (later KSVD). Corporate identity arrived
with the OB Beer label and mark (1972–74), developed by Seoul National University professors and
shown in Cho Young-jae's 1976 *DECOMAS* exhibition, which set off a CI boom among Korean firms.
The 1988 Seoul Olympics (emblem by Yang Seung-choon, 1983; design committee chaired by Cho) were
Korea's 1964.

Typography modernised in parallel. Choi Jeong-ho's metal-type myeongjo and gothic designs of the
1950s–60s became the base of nearly every later Hangul text face. Phototypesetting arrived in the
1970s. In 1976 Cho Young-jae proposed a *tal-nemo* (non-square) Hangul module for typewriters,
in which letters keep fixed shapes in place of distorting to fill a square. Ahn Sang-soo's
Ahn typeface (1985) made it a design manifesto. Horizontal setting, normal in schoolbooks since
1948, reached the press when *The Hankyoreh* launched horizontally in 1988; the other national
dailies followed in the 1990s.

### Principles

1. **Liberate the letter from the square.** Let each jamo (letter) keep one shape so the
   alphabet's logic shows. *Example:* the Ahn typeface (1985); final consonants hang below the
   centre of the syllable and the line has a ragged lower edge.
2. **The type is the national identity.** The script is the most Korean thing a designer has.
   *Example:* the Ahn typeface used in the opening and closing ceremonies of PyeongChang 2018.
3. **Identity as modernisation.** Corporate identity introduced as a tool of the export economy.
   *Example:* the OB Beer identity (1974) and Cho Young-jae's *DECOMAS* exhibition (1976).
4. **Lyrical illustration as a national voice.** A gentle, simplified illustrative style as a
   Korean counterweight to Swiss objectivity. *Example:* Kim Kyo-man's culture posters for the 1986
   Asian Games and 1988 Olympics.
5. **Design as alternative education.** A design culture built outside the official academy.
   *Example:* Ahn Sang-soo's Paju Typography Institute (PaTI).

### Key works

- Myeongjo and gothic metal types for Samhwa Printing and Dong-A Publishing, Choi Jeong-ho,
  1950s–60s.
- OB Beer identity, Cho Young-jae, 1974.
- *DECOMAS* exhibition, Cho Young-jae, Shinsegae Gallery, 1976.
- Seoul 1988 Olympic emblem, Yang Seung-choon, 1983.
- Ahn typeface, Ahn Sang-soo, 1985.
- Seoul 1988 Olympic official poster, Cho Young-jae (computer graphics with Etsuo Genda), 1980s.
- *bogoseo/bogoseo* magazine, Ahn Sang-soo, 1988.

### Visual vocabulary

Hangul set as image: syllable blocks broken apart, jamo enlarged as geometric shapes (circle,
line, short stroke), non-square faces with ragged baselines. Primary colours drawn from
*obangsaek* (the five traditional colours: blue, red, yellow, white, black). Swiss grids and
asymmetric layouts (Ahn translated Emil Ruder's *Typographie* into Korean, 2001). In
illustration, Kim Kyo-man's soft flat forms and folk themes.

### Lineage

From: Swiss style and the New Typography (Ahn's translations of Ruder and Tschichold), Japanese
modernism (KSVD–JAGDA exchanges from the 1970s; Cho's 1979 membership of the Japanese Society
for the Science of Design), American corporate identity, and Korean folk tradition.

Into: contemporary Korean type foundries and the free-font culture (the Arita typeface for
AmorePacific, developed with Ahn), the Seoul indie design scene, and Paju Book City's publishing.

### Quotes

No verified direct quote is recorded here.

### Remix today

Hangul's modernisers solved a problem every multilingual system meets: when a script doesn't fit
the frame the technology assumes, change the frame, not the script. For anyone designing a
variable or multi-script type system in 2026, the non-square Hangul experiments (Cho, Ahn and
their successors) are a case study in letting the writing system's own logic set the grid.

### Choi Jeong-ho {#choi-jeong-ho}

**1916–1988. Type designer.** Born in Yeongdong, North Chungcheong. He drew metal and phototype
faces for Samhwa Printing, Dong-A Publishing and most Korean printers, including Samhwa-che,
Bojinjae Gothic, Dong-A Publishing-che, display gothic and myeongjo, and extra-light gothic. Korean
sources describe modern Hangul myeongjo as resting on the types he made in the 1960s.

**Principles**

- **The text face is the infrastructure of a literate state.** Two families, myeongjo (serif)
  and gothic (sans), designed for reading at scale.
- **Balance inside the square.** Each syllable block drawn so that its optical colour matches its
  neighbours across thousands of combinations.

**Key works:** Myeongjo body type, 1960s (typeface; exact year not verified); Dong-A Publishing
typeface (typeface).

### Cho Young-jae {#cho-young-jae}

**1935–2019. Graphic designer, educator.** Professor at Seoul National University (1965–2000). He
founded Cho Young-jae Design Lab (now CDR) in 1974, introduced corporate identity to Korea,
founded and four times presided over KSVD, chaired the Seoul 1988 Olympic design committee
(1982–88), and was among the first two inductees of the Korean Designers' Hall of Fame (2012).

**Principles**

- **Design as thinking work.** The designer as strategist and manager, not decorator.
  *Example:* *DECOMAS* exhibition (1976), which explained CI to Korean industry.
- **The non-square module.** Hangul letters with fixed shapes so that machines can set it
  beautifully. *Example:* his 1976 paper on structure for Hangul mechanisation, published in the
  first issue of SNU's journal *Johyeong*.
- **Every Olympics uses the newest technique.** *Example:* the Seoul 1988 official poster, built
  with computer graphics.

**Key works:** OB Beer identity, 1974 (identity); *DECOMAS*, 1976 (exhibition); Seoul 1988
official poster, 1980s (poster).

### Ahn Sang-soo {#ahn-sang-soo}

**Born 1952. Typographer, graphic designer, educator.** From Chungju. He studied at Hongik
University, designed at Geumseong (now LG Electronics) from 1977, and art-directed the magazines
*Madang* and *Meot* (1981–83). In 1985 he founded Ahn Graphics and released the Ahn typeface. He
opened Korea's first internet café near Hongik (1988), was ICOGRADA vice-president (1997–2001),
won the Leipzig Gutenberg Prize (2007), and founded the Paju Typography Institute (PaTI).

**Principles**

- **Unlock the square.** Fixed-shape jamo; the first variable-width Hangul face. *Example:* Ahn
  typeface (1985), bundled as a default in the Hangul word processor (computer version 1991).
- **Typography as poetry.** Letters as a visual score, informed by his doctoral study of the
  modernist poet Yi Sang's typographic poems (Hanyang University, 1995).
- **Play as pedagogy.** Making by hand, outside the official academy. *Example:* PaTI.

**Key works:** Ahn typeface, 1985 (typeface); *bogoseo/bogoseo*, 1988 (magazine); Korean
translation of Emil Ruder's *Typographie*, 2001 (book).

## Japanese Emptiness {#japanese-emptiness}

### Context

In December 1980 the supermarket chain Seiyu launched forty products under the name *Mujirushi
Ryōhin*, "no-brand quality goods". The idea came from Seiji Tsutsumi, head of the Saison group,
who was reading Baudrillard on consumer society. It was developed by Ikkō Tanaka, copywriter
Kazuko Koike and interior designer Takashi Sugimoto. Its launch slogan, by Koike, was *wake atte,
yasui*: "lower priced for a reason." The first store opened in Aoyama in 1983; London followed
in 1991.

Muji began as anti-branding in the bubble economy. After the bubble burst, and when Kenya Hara
took over its art direction in 2001 with Naoto Fukasawa on the advisory board, it became the
model of a different design philosophy: *emptiness*. In this view the product or poster is an
empty vessel (*karappo*) that each person fills with their own meaning, rather than a
message pushed at them. Hara wrote the theory down in *Designing Design* (Japanese 2003, English
2007) and *White* (Japanese 2008, English 2010). His 2003 Muji *Horizon* campaign is its
canonical image.

It deserves its own movement and not a footnote to Japanese modernism. Its principles invert
NDC's: NDC made a strong statement with one mark; emptiness offers deliberately *no* statement.
It spread through the 2000s into the global minimalism of tech products, wayfinding (Masaaki
Hiromura, Tanaka's former assistant) and contemporary Japanese branding (Kashiwa Sato).

### Principles

1. **The empty vessel.** Design offers a space for meaning instead of a message. *Example:*
   Kenya Hara's Muji *Horizon* poster (2003), a figure standing on the flat horizon of the
   Uyuni salt flats in Bolivia, with only the Muji wordmark.
2. **Subtract the brand.** Remove logos, ornament and colour so that the material and the price
   become the message. *Example:* Muji's launch packaging (1980), kraft paper, cellophane and red
   type.
3. **White as potential.** White isn't absence but the state before content. *Example:* Hara's
   book *White* (2008).
4. **Re-design the ordinary.** Look again at everyday objects instead of inventing new ones.
   *Example:* Hara's *Re-Design: Daily Products of the 21st Century* exhibition (2000), with
   Shigeru Ban's square-cored toilet roll.
5. **Clarity through organisation.** In branding, the designer's job is to tidy a client's
   identity down to its essential form. *Example:* Kashiwa Sato's Uniqlo identity for the SoHo
   New York flagship (2006), a red square with katakana and Latin wordmarks.

### Key works

- Muji, Ikkō Tanaka, Kazuko Koike, Takashi Sugimoto, 1980.
- Nagano 1998 opening and closing ceremony programmes, Kenya Hara, 1998.
- *Re-Design* exhibition, Kenya Hara, 2000.
- Muji *Horizon* campaign, Kenya Hara, 2003.
- *HAPTIC: Awakening the Senses* exhibition, Kenya Hara, 2004.
- Uniqlo global identity (SoHo flagship), Kashiwa Sato, 2006.
- National Art Center, Tokyo identity and signage, Kashiwa Sato, 2007.
- Tokyo 2020 sport pictograms, team led by Masaaki Hiromura, released March 2019.

### Visual vocabulary

Off-white and unbleached grounds, kraft brown, a single dark red or black. Small, quiet type,
often a light Gothic or a Latin grotesque, placed off-centre with very large margins. Photography
of horizons, empty rooms and raw materials. No ornament, no gradient, very little hierarchy. In
Sato's branding variant the reduction is the same, but loud: one primary colour, one square, one
wordmark.

### Lineage

From: Japanese modernism (Tanaka, NDC, where Hara has worked since 1983), tea-ceremony
aesthetics (*wabi*, *ma*), functionalist product design (Dieter Rams's "less, but better" is the
closest Western analogue), and Swiss restraint.

Into: global minimalist retail and tech branding of the 2000s–2010s, flat design (which shares
its subtraction but not its philosophy), and Japan House (Hara as general producer, from 2017).

### Quotes

- Kenya Hara: "Yet emptiness doesn't mean 'nothingness' or 'energy-less'; rather, in many cases,
  it indicates a condition, or *kizen*, which will likely be filled with content in the future."
  (Kenya Hara, *White*, Lars Müller Publishers, 2010.)
- Muji's 1980 launch slogan, by Kazuko Koike: *Wake atte, yasui.* ("Lower priced for a reason.")

### Remix today

Emptiness is the most copied and most misunderstood Japanese idea in contemporary design. The
copy is the look (white, small type, beige). The idea is the *offer*: leave the meaning to the
person using it. In 2026, with generative interfaces that can fill every surface, an
interface that deliberately says less and lets the user bring the meaning is a real
position, not a style.

### Kenya Hara {#kenya-hara}

**Born 1958. Graphic designer, curator, writer.** From Okayama. He graduated from Musashino Art
University (MA 1983), joined NDC the same year and has led it as president since 2014. He has
been Muji's art director since 2001, has taught at Musashino Art University since 2003, and is
general producer of Japan House. He received the Medal with Purple Ribbon in 2024.

**Principles**

- **Emptiness (*karappo*).** Design as a receptacle for meaning. *Example:* Muji *Horizon* (2003).
- **Ex-formation.** Instead of informing, make the familiar unknown again so that people notice
  it. *Example:* his *Ex-formation* seminar with Musashino students, begun in 2004.
- **Haptic design.** Design addressed to the senses before the eye. *Example:* the *HAPTIC*
  exhibition (2004), where designers were asked for objects motivated by touch, not form or
  colour.
- **Re-design.** *Example:* the *Re-Design* exhibition (2000).

**Key works:** Nagano 1998 programmes, 1998 (book); *Re-Design*, 2000 (exhibition); Muji
*Horizon*, 2003 (poster); *Designing Design*, 2003 (book); *White*, 2008 (book); Xiaomi logo,
2021 (identity).

### Kashiwa Sato {#kashiwa-sato}

**Born 1965. Creative director, art director.** From Tokyo. He graduated from Tama Art University,
worked at Hakuhodo, and in 2000 founded the studio SAMURAI. His work includes the Uniqlo global
identity and flagships, Seven-Eleven's branding, Rakuten, the National Art Center, Tokyo, Imabari
Towel, the Fuji Kindergarten (with Tezuka Architects) and the Cup Noodles Museum (2011). The
National Art Center gave him a retrospective in 2021.

**Principles**

- **Organising (*seiri*).** Branding as tidying: find the client's essence, discard the rest.
  *Example:* the Uniqlo identity (2006).
- **Reduction, loudly.** Straight lines, primary colours, one strong square. *Example:* the
  National Art Center, Tokyo identity (2007).
- **Brand as an entire environment.** Logo, store, product, architecture as one direction.
  *Example:* Fuji Kindergarten (2007).

**Key works:** SMAP album artwork, 2000 (other); Uniqlo, 2006 (identity); National Art Center,
Tokyo, 2007 (identity); Cup Noodles Museum, 2011 (exhibition).

## Superflat {#superflat}

### Context

Takashi Murakami (born 1962, trained in *nihonga*, traditional Japanese painting, at Tokyo
University of the Arts) coined *Superflat* for an exhibition at Parco Gallery, Shibuya, in April
2000, and its catalogue. The show travelled to the Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles
(Pacific Design Center, January–March 2001), the Walker Art Center and the Henry Art Gallery. The
argument: Japanese visual culture, from Edo painters like Itō Jakuchū to anime directors like
Hideaki Anno, shares a flat, decorative, depthless picture plane, and post-war Japanese society
is itself "flat", with no real line between high and low, art and merchandise. He completed the
trilogy with *Coloriage* (Fondation Cartier, 2002) and *Little Boy: The Arts of Japan's Exploding
Subculture* (Japan Society, New York, 2005), named after the Hiroshima bomb.

It belongs in a graphic design history because Murakami ran it as a *brand system*. Kaikai Kiki
(incorporated 2001) produces paintings, merchandise and campaigns. The Louis Vuitton
collaboration (begun 2002 at Marc Jacobs's invitation, with the Multicolore monogram) and Kanye
West's *Graduation* cover (2007) put Superflat's graphic language on goods and screens worldwide.
Graphic designers in the Parco show (groovisions) and in anime and fashion were part of it from
the start.

### Principles

1. **Flatness as cultural theory.** No perspective, no depth: line and flat colour as a claim
   about a society, not only a style. *Example:* the *Superflat* exhibition (Parco, 2000).
2. **Collapse high and low.** Paintings, figures, handbags and album covers treated as one
   practice. *Example:* the Louis Vuitton Multicolore monogram (2003), the monogram redrawn in
   33 colours.
3. **The character as a brand.** A mascot that mutates endlessly across media. *Example:* Mr. DOB,
   Murakami's recurring character, from 1993.
4. **Cuteness carries trauma.** *Kawaii* surfaces over the memory of defeat and the bomb.
   *Example:* *Little Boy* (2005).

### Key works

- Mr. DOB character, Takashi Murakami, from 1993.
- *Superflat* exhibition and catalogue, Parco Gallery, 2000; MOCA Los Angeles, 2001.
- Louis Vuitton collaboration, Murakami, from 2002.
- *Little Boy* exhibition and book, Japan Society, New York, 2005.
- Kanye West, *Graduation* album cover and "Good Morning" video, Murakami, 2007.

### Visual vocabulary

Hard black outlines, anime-flat fills in saturated candy colours, gold leaf and silver grounds
borrowed from Rinpa screens, smiling flower patterns repeated as all-over wallpaper, eyes and
mushrooms, character heads. Type is minimal; the image is the logo.

### Lineage

From: the Japanese poster avant-garde (the collage of high and low; Yokoo is the obvious
ancestor), anime and manga, Edo eccentric painters, and American Pop art and psychedelia.

Into: 2000s luxury collaborations, streetwear graphics, the "art-toy" industry, and the
character-led brand worlds that dominate social media.

### Remix today

Superflat's useful idea for designers is the *system*, not the flowers: one character or motif
pushed through every medium, from a gallery painting to a handbag lining, with the boundary
between "work" and "merchandise" erased on purpose. That is how most brand worlds operate in
2026; Murakami did it knowingly, and with a critique built in.

### Takashi Murakami {#takashi-murakami}

**Born 1962. Artist, curator, entrepreneur.** He founded the Hiropon Factory in the 1990s, which
became Kaikai Kiki Co. in 2001. He coined and curated *Superflat* (2000), worked with Louis
Vuitton (from 2002), and designed album covers for Kanye West (*Graduation*, 2007; *Kids See
Ghosts*, 2018).

**Principles**

- **The art enterprise.** The studio as a company with production lines, like an animation house.
  *Example:* Kaikai Kiki (2001).
- **Repackage the subcultural for the market.** Otaku forms presented as high art and sold back as
  goods. *Example:* *Miss ko²* (1996).
- **Curate the context for your own work.** Theory, exhibitions and product together.
  *Example:* *Superflat* (2000) and *Little Boy* (2005).

**Key works:** *Superflat*, 2000 (exhibition); Louis Vuitton Multicolore, 2003 (product); *Little
Boy*, 2005 (exhibition); *Graduation*, 2007 (other).

## Disputed and uncertain dates

- **Nippon Design Center founding:** registered December 1959, publicly launched January and
  officially opened March 1960. Sources cite either year. Used here: 1960 (event).
- **Tokyo 1964 emblem:** chosen in the invited competition of 1960 (Japanese sources); English
  Wikipedia says the logo and poster series were designed in 1961. The first poster used the
  emblem in 1961; the sprinter poster is 1962.
- **HSBC hexagon (Steiner):** usually dated 1983; English Wikipedia gives 1984. Used: 1983.
- **Superflat:** the Parco show and Japanese catalogue are April 2000; the MOCA show is
  January 2001. English Wikipedia ties the theory to the MOCA catalogue of 2000.
- **Eiko Ishioka and Parco:** Japanese sources date her independent studio to 1970; English
  Wikipedia makes her Parco chief art director from 1971.
- **Kamekura and *Nippon*:** English Wikipedia says *Nippon* from 1937; Japanese sources say he
  joined Nippon Kōbō in 1938.
- **Kenya Hara, *Designing Design*:** Japanese edition 2003, English (Lars Müller) 2007. *White*:
  Japanese 2008, English 2010.
- **Kazumasa Nagai:** died 23 February 2026, so he is no longer living.
- **Unverified years, kept out of the JSON or flagged:** Lu Jingren's *Zhu Xi Bangshu Qianziwen*
  (commonly 1999); Choi Jeong-ho's myeongjo (the "1960s" is verified, an exact year is not); Qian
  Juntao's individual cover titles; Henry Steiner's Jockey Club redesign (before 1997).
