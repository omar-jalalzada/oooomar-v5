# 1850–1914: Ornament to reform

Slice `1850-1914`. Lineages: `ornament` (Victorian print through Jugendstil) and `commerce`
(Plakatstil).

Between the Great Exhibition of 1851 and the outbreak of war in 1914, graphic design went from
being something that happened to print to something someone designed. At the start of the
period, machines were producing more type, more colour and more ornament than anyone knew what
to do with. Over the next sixty years a chain of reformers argued about what ornament was *for*.
Owen Jones's answer was rules, Morris's was craft, Mucha and Chéret took ornament into the
street, Vienna took it into geometry, and Berlin took it away almost entirely. By 1914 most of
the moves modernism would later claim as its own were already on the wall: the brand mark, the
house typeface, the reduced object poster, the modular grid, the designed environment.

Three technologies shape every section below. **Chromolithography** made colour cheap.
**Photomechanical reproduction** (the zinc line block, then the halftone) let a drawing or a
photograph go to press without a hand engraver re-cutting it. **Mechanical typesetting**
(Linotype, 1886; Monotype, from 1887) made text cheap and fast, and that set off the
counter-reaction of the private presses.

## Turning points

- **1837 — Chromolithography patented.** Godefroy Engelmann's patent for printing colour from
  multiple stones. The colour poster of the 1880s and 1890s stands on it.
- **1851 — Great Exhibition, London.** The Crystal Palace's overloaded industrial goods became
  the reformers' shared exhibit of what had gone wrong. Owen Jones ran the building's colour
  scheme.
- **1856 — The Grammar of Ornament.** Owen Jones's chromolithographed survey turned world ornament
  into a teachable system.
- **1861 — Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co.** Designers who made what they designed; the practical
  start of Arts and Crafts.
- **1865 — Edmund Evans's colour toy books.** Colour wood-engraving for a mass market, with
  Walter Crane drawing.
- **1867 — Exposition Universelle, Paris.** Japanese prints and crafts on show; Japonisme follows.
- **1874 — Louis Prang's Christmas cards.** Chromolithography in ordinary American homes.
- **1880–1881 — Halftone.** On 4 March 1880 the New York *Daily Graphic* printed *A Scene in
  Shantytown*, an early newspaper halftone. In 1881 Frederic Ives of Philadelphia patented the
  first commercially successful process.
- **1881 — French press law of 29 July.** It liberalised bill-posting in France and opened Paris's
  walls to the poster.
- **1886 — Linotype.** Ottmar Mergenthaler's line-caster went into commercial use at the
  *New York Tribune* in July.
- **1887 — Monotype.** Tolbert Lanston founded the Lanston Monotype Machine Company in
  Philadelphia; the London company followed in 1897. Single-type casting gave mechanical
  composition book quality.
- **1888 — Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society.** It gave the movement its name and a London
  showcase, with Walter Crane as founding president. At its first season Emery Walker's
  lantern-slide lecture on early printing sent Morris toward a press of his own.
- **1891 — Kelmscott Press.**
- **1895 — Maison de l'Art Nouveau.** Siegfried Bing's Paris gallery opened in December and gave
  the style its English and French name.
- **1896 — Jugend, Munich.** Georg Hirth's weekly gave German Art Nouveau its name.
- **1897 — Vienna Secession founded** (3 April).
- **1900 — Exposition Universelle, Paris.** Art Nouveau's public high point; Guimard's Métro
  entrances opened the same year.
- **1903 — Wiener Werkstätte founded.**
- **1904 — Offset lithography on paper.** Ira Washington Rubel's rubber-blanket press, the
  process that would come to dominate commercial print.
- **1907 — Deutscher Werkbund founded; Behrens joins AEG.**
- **1910 — Das Plakat.** The Verein der Plakatfreunde's journal (the society was founded 1905)
  documented Plakatstil as it happened.
- **1914 — Werkbund exhibition, Cologne; First World War.** The Muthesius–van de Velde debate over
  standard types versus artistic individuality, then the war, closed the period.

## Victorian Print {#victorian-print}

**Context.** Britain and the United States, roughly 1840–1900. Industrialisation multiplied the
printed surface: handbills, posters, playbills, labels, catalogues, packaging, illustrated
papers. Three new technologies flooded the market. Machine-cut wood type (Darius Wells's first
known catalogue was 1828, and William Leavenworth added the pantograph in 1834) made huge
display letters cheap. Typefounders produced a stream of display styles: fat faces, Egyptians
(slab serifs), Tuscans, grotesques, outlined, shaded and decorated faces. Chromolithography and
chromoxylography (colour wood-engraving) brought colour to the mass market. Nobody was in charge
of the result. The job printer was compositor, layout artist and client service at once, and
the aesthetic was additive. In the 1870s and 1880s American and British printers developed
"artistic printing", which bent brass rules into curves and set type in fragmented panels, the
most virtuosic and least legible form of the idea.

**Principles.**

1. **Every line a different face.** Hierarchy came from variety and scale rather than from a
   system: each line of a bill was set in the largest face that would fit the measure. *Example:
   Darius Wells's wood type specimens, New York, from 1828, and the letterpress playbills and
   auction bills built from them.*
2. **Ornament as a grammar.** Ornament was surveyed, compared and taught as a language with rules.
   *Example: Owen Jones, The Grammar of Ornament, 1856.*
3. **Colour for the mass market.** Chromolithography and colour wood-engraving made saturated
   colour domestic. *Example: Walter Crane and Edmund Evans, The Baby's Opera, 1877; Louis Prang's
   chromolithographed Christmas cards, from 1874.*
4. **Eclectic revivalism.** Gothic, Egyptian, Moorish and Rococo motifs mix on one surface;
   fidelity to the motif matters more than unity of the whole. *Example: Owen Jones's Alhambra
   volumes, 1836–1845.*

**Key works.** Owen Jones, *Plans, Elevations, Sections and Details of the Alhambra*, 1836–1845;
Owen Jones, *The Grammar of Ornament*, 1856; Walter Crane and Edmund Evans, *The Baby's Opera*,
1877; Louis Prang & Co., Christmas cards, from 1874.

**Visual vocabulary.** Centred, symmetrical layouts where every line fills the measure. Wood type
condensed or expanded to fit. Fat faces, slab serifs and Tuscans with bifurcated serifs in one
setting. Ruled borders, corner ornaments and printers' flowers. Chromolithographic colour,
often eight or more stones, with metallic golds. Wood-engraved vignettes. In artistic printing,
curved brass rules and panels set at angles.

**Lineage.** It came out of the Georgian jobbing trade and the industrial typefoundry. It provoked
the reform movements (Arts and Crafts, private press), which defined themselves against it, and
fed Plakatstil and the twentieth-century advertising poster with its scale and its directness.
It is also the source vocabulary for every later "Victorian revival", from the psychedelic
posters of the 1960s to wood-type nostalgia today.

**Quotes.** Owen Jones, *The Grammar of Ornament* (1856), Proposition 8: "All ornament should be
based upon a geometrical construction."

**Remix today.** Victorian job printing was the first time a design language was driven by the
contents of a type case instead of by an idea, and that is a useful mirror for a field built on
component libraries. The positive lessons are the confidence of scale (one word filling the
sheet edge to edge) and mixed display faces held together by a single centred axis. The warning
is what happens when every component is available and no one decides.

### Owen Jones {#owen-jones}

**Context.** Owen Jones (1809–1874) was a London architect who spent the early 1830s measuring the
Alhambra in Granada and then published it in chromolithography, 1836–1845, pushing the process
further than any British printer had. He was a Superintendent of Works for the Great Exhibition
of 1851 and devised the colour scheme of the Crystal Palace interior. The exhibition's clutter
and his work with Henry Cole on design education led to *The Grammar of Ornament* (1856): one
hundred chromolithographed plates surveying ornament from "savage tribes" (the period's term)
through Egypt, Greece, Islam, India, China and the Renaissance, prefaced by 37 general
propositions.

**Principles.**

1. **Ornament has rules.** Decoration follows stated propositions about geometry, proportion and
   colour, so it can be taught rather than merely copied. *Example: the 37 propositions, The
   Grammar of Ornament, 1856.*
2. **Decorate construction.** Ornament should grow from the structure of the object and clarify
   it. *Example: Proposition 5, 1856.*
3. **Flat for flat surfaces.** Ornament on a flat plane should be flat and conventionalised, not a
   naturalistic illusion. *Example: the stylised leaf and flower plates of the Grammar, 1856.*

**Key works.** *Plans, Elevations, Sections and Details of the Alhambra*, 1836–1845 (book); colour
scheme for the Great Exhibition building, 1851; *The Grammar of Ornament*, 1856 (book).

**Visual vocabulary.** Dense, symmetrical plates in flat chromolithographic colour; strong primary
palettes with gold; geometric interlace; ornament conventionalised into repeat units.

**Lineage.** Fed directly into Morris (who knew the book), the design schools at South Kensington,
and Art Nouveau's stylised plant. His insistence on flat, geometric ornament anticipates
Vienna's grid by fifty years.

**Quotes.** *The Grammar of Ornament* (1856), Proposition 5: "Construction should be decorated.
Decoration should never be purposely constructed."

**Remix today.** Jones wrote a design system's principles page in 1856, then backed every
principle with a hundred examples. Do the same for a pattern or icon language: a dozen short
propositions, each tested against a plate of real cases.

### Walter Crane {#walter-crane}

**Context.** Walter Crane (1845–1915) trained as a wood engraver. From 1863 the printer Edmund
Evans employed him on "yellowback" railway-novel covers, and from 1865 the two made colour toy
books, nursery rhymes and fairy tales printed from a few colour blocks, two or three a year
until 1876. Crane went on to become one of the Arts and Crafts movement's chief theorists and
teachers. He was a founder and the first president of the Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society
(1888) and a committed socialist illustrator.

**Principles.**

1. **The page as one decorative panel.** Picture, border and lettering are composed together as a
   flat whole. *Example: The Baby's Opera, 1877.*
2. **Line first.** Outline is the fundamental element of design; colour and mass follow. *Example:
   Line and Form, 1900.*
3. **Design for the press.** Draw for the limits of reproduction, here a few flat colour blocks,
   instead of fighting them. *Example: the Evans toy books, 1865–1876.*

**Key works.** *The Absurd ABC*, 1874; *The Baby's Opera*, 1877; *The Bases of Design*, 1898;
*Line and Form*, 1900.

**Visual vocabulary.** Strong black outline; flat colour in a few blocks; Japanese-influenced
flat space (visible in his toy books from the early 1870s); hand-lettered text integrated into
borders.

**Lineage.** From Victorian colour printing and Pre-Raphaelite drawing into Arts and Crafts
teaching; a bridge from the toy book to the designed picture book.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Crane is the patron saint of designing *with* the production constraint. When a
format only allows a few colours (a risograph, a two-colour print, an e-ink screen), design
inside it.

## Arts and Crafts {#arts-and-crafts}

**Context.** Britain, 1860s–1910s, spreading to the United States (Roycroft in East Aurora, New
York, from 1895; Gustav Stickley's *The Craftsman*, 1901). The theory came from A. W. N. Pugin's
Gothic revival and John Ruskin's chapter "The Nature of Gothic" in *The Stones of Venice*
(volume 2, 1853), which argued that machine production degrades both the object and the worker.
William Morris put it into practice. In 1861 he founded Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co. with
Dante Gabriel Rossetti, Edward Burne-Jones, Ford Madox Brown, Philip Webb and others to design
and make glass, furniture, textiles and wallpapers. The movement was named in 1887 (the term is
credited to T. J. Cobden-Sanderson) and given a public face by the Arts and Crafts Exhibition
Society from 1888. A. H. Mackmurdo's Century Guild (1882) and its magazine *The Century Guild
Hobby Horse* (1884) took the ideas into graphic design. The reaction was against Victorian
eclecticism, cheap machine ornament and the split between designer and maker.

**Principles.**

1. **The designer makes.** Design and production are one act. *Example: Morris, Marshall, Faulkner
   & Co., 1861, where the partners designed and supervised their own glass, tiles and textiles.*
2. **Truth to materials.** A pattern should show how it was made. *Example: William Morris,
   Strawberry Thief, indigo-discharge block-printed cotton, 1883.*
3. **Nature, stylised and repeated.** Plants and birds flattened into an interlocking repeat.
   *Example: William Morris, Trellis wallpaper, designed 1862, issued 1864.*
4. **Useful and beautiful.** Everyday objects deserve fine-art care. *Example: the first Arts and
   Crafts Exhibition Society show, London, 1888.*
5. **The book as architecture.** Title page, frame and text are one built structure. *Example:
   A. H. Mackmurdo, title page of Wren's City Churches, 1883.*

**Key works.** Philip Webb and William Morris, Red House, Bexleyheath, 1859–1860 (building); Morris,
*Trellis* wallpaper, 1864; Morris, *Strawberry Thief*, 1883; A. H. Mackmurdo, *Wren's City
Churches* title page, 1883 (book); *The Century Guild Hobby Horse*, 1884 (magazine); Walter Crane,
*The Bases of Design*, 1898 (book).

**Visual vocabulary.** Dense, all-over repeats on a hidden diagonal or turnover structure.
Naturalistic but flattened plant and bird motifs. Natural dyes: indigo, madder, weld. Medieval
and Gothic references. Hand-drawn borders and initials. In books, heavy black type and woodcut
decoration. Mackmurdo's title page adds flame-like, asymmetric stems, the first hint of the
whiplash.

**Lineage.** Came from Pugin, Ruskin and the Gothic revival, and from the reform debate the 1851
exhibition set off (Owen Jones, Henry Cole). Fed the private press movement directly; through
Mackmurdo, Art Nouveau; through Glasgow and Hermann Muthesius's survey *Das englische Haus*
(1904–1905), the Vienna Secession, the Wiener Werkstätte and the Deutscher Werkbund; and through
the Werkbund, the Bauhaus (`bauhaus`, avant-garde slice), whose 1919 programme called for the
unity of art and craft.

**Quotes.** William Morris, "The Beauty of Life" (lecture, 1880; published in *Hopes and Fears
for Art*, 1882): "Have nothing in your houses that you do not know to be useful or believe to be
beautiful."

**Remix today.** The Arts and Crafts argument applies directly to anyone designing with
generative tools: when making becomes effortless, value moves to judgement, provenance and
evident care. The practical things to take are the hidden structure of a Morris repeat (design
the armature, then the motif) and the idea that the designer should understand the medium all
the way down.

### William Morris {#william-morris}

**Context.** William Morris (1834–1896): poet, designer, manufacturer, translator and socialist. He
trained briefly as an architect under G. E. Street, then built Red House with Philip Webb
(1859–1860), and furnishing it led to the firm in 1861 (reorganised under his sole control as
Morris & Co. in 1875). He taught himself dyeing, weaving and block printing and revived
indigo-discharge printing. In 1891, after Emery Walker's 1888 lecture on early printing, he founded
the Kelmscott Press in Hammersmith. Over seven years it printed over fifty titles in three
typefaces of his own design. Its masterpiece, *The Works of Geoffrey Chaucer* (1896), with 87
woodcut illustrations after Burne-Jones, was finished months before his death.

**Principles.**

1. **Useful or beautiful.** Every object in a home must pass one of two tests. *Example: "The
   Beauty of Life", 1880.*
2. **Structure under pattern.** Every repeat sits on a hidden armature, a diagonal trellis or a
   turnover, so dense ornament stays readable. *Example: Strawberry Thief, 1883.*
3. **Return to the source.** When a craft has decayed, relearn it from its best historical moment
   by doing it. *Example: Golden Type, 1890, redrawn from Nicolas Jenson's roman of 1470 using
   enlarged photographs.*
4. **The book as a whole.** Type, paper, ink, margins, initials and borders are designed together.
   *Example: the Kelmscott Chaucer, 1896.*

**Key works.** *Trellis* wallpaper, 1864; *Strawberry Thief*, 1883; Golden Type, 1890 (typeface);
Troy and Chaucer types, 1892 (typefaces); *The Story of the Glittering Plain*, 1891 (book); *The
Works of Geoffrey Chaucer*, 1896 (book).

**Visual vocabulary.** Heavy, dark roman and blackletter in tight setting. Wide woodcut borders of
vine and acanthus. Large decorated initials. Red-and-black printing. Handmade paper. In textiles,
dense birds-and-branches repeats in indigo, red and yellow.

**Lineage.** From Ruskin, the Gothic revival and medieval manuscripts. Led to every private press
after him, to the Cranach Presse and Edward Johnston's revival of lettering, and through Walker to
the historical type revivals of the 1920s.

**Quotes.** *A Note by William Morris on His Aims in Founding the Kelmscott Press* (1898): "I began
printing books with the hope of producing some which would have a definite claim to beauty,
while at the same time they should be easy to read and should not dazzle the eye, or trouble the
intellect of the reader by eccentricity of form in the letters."

**Remix today.** Morris is the founder-designer who builds the company that makes the product. His
habit of going back to the strongest source rather than the nearest copy is what made Golden Type
better than the commercial faces around it, and it is still the best method for a revival.

## Private Press Movement {#private-press}

**Context.** Britain first, then the United States and Germany, 1891–1930s. Mechanical
composition and cheap wood-pulp paper had made the trade book thin, grey and badly proportioned.
Morris's Kelmscott Press (1891–1898) answered with a complete reconstruction: new types modelled
on fifteenth-century originals, handmade paper, hand presses, and the double-page spread treated
as the unit. A second wave stripped Morris's ornament and kept his rigour. Emery Walker and T. J.
Cobden-Sanderson founded the Doves Press (1900–1916) with a single roman, the Doves Type, and no
decoration. Others followed: C. H. St John Hornby's Ashendene Press (1895), Charles Ricketts's
Vale Press (1896), C. R. Ashbee's Essex House Press (1898), Daniel Berkeley Updike's Merrymount
Press in Boston (1893), and Harry Graf Kessler's Cranach Presse in Weimar (1913). In 1913, and
then in 1916–1917, after the partnership with Walker broke down, Cobden-Sanderson dropped the
Doves punches, matrices and finally the type itself off Hammersmith Bridge into the Thames, so it
could never be used for anything else.

**Principles.**

1. **The spread is the unit.** Margins graduate from inner to top, outer and bottom so two text
   blocks read as one composition. *Example: Kelmscott Chaucer, 1896.*
2. **Type from the source.** Redraw from the strongest historical model, not from its weakened
   descendants. *Example: Morris, Golden Type, 1890, after Jenson.*
3. **Dense, even colour.** Tight word spacing and robust type make the page a dark, even texture.
   *Example: The Story of the Glittering Plain, Kelmscott, 1891.*
4. **Austerity as luxury.** Ornament goes; proportion, presswork and one fine roman carry the book.
   *Example: the Doves Press Bible, 1903–1905.*

**Key works.** Kelmscott Press, *The Story of the Glittering Plain*, 1891; Kelmscott Press, *The
Works of Geoffrey Chaucer*, 1896; Doves Type, 1900 (typeface); Doves Press, *The English Bible*,
1903–1905 (book), whose opening of Genesis in red capitals running down the margin, by Edward
Johnston, is the period's best-known single page.

**Visual vocabulary.** Venetian roman (Jenson) or gothic types, set solid. Narrow inner and wide
bottom margins. Red used sparingly for initials and headings. At Kelmscott, woodcut borders and
initials; at Doves, none, only calligraphic initials and space.

**Lineage.** Came from Arts and Crafts and from Walker's scholarship of incunabula. Fed the
twentieth-century book: the Monotype revivals of the 1920s, Johnston, Eric Gill and Stanley
Morison, and through them the classical typography that the New Typography (`new-typography`,
avant-garde slice) defined itself against. The Doves Type was digitally revived by Robert Green
in 2013, and he recovered pieces of the original type from the Thames in 2014–2015.

**Quotes.** See William Morris above.

**Remix today.** The private press is the original argument that the medium is part of the
message. Its spread logic (graduated margins, the two pages as one composition) still applies to
any paged or scrolled reading layout, and Doves shows how much a single excellent face and
generous space can carry. A 2026 "private press" might be a single-author website whose
typography is a set of choices made once and kept.

## Japonisme {#japonisme}

**Context.** France and Britain, 1860s–1900s. After Japan reopened to Western trade in the 1850s,
ukiyo-e woodblock prints by Hokusai, Hiroshige and Utamaro reached Paris and London as souvenirs,
packing paper and then collectors' objects. Japanese goods were shown at the London International
Exhibition of 1862 and the Paris Exposition Universelle of 1867. The critic Philippe Burty coined
"Japonisme" in 1872. The dealer Siegfried Bing published *Le Japon artistique* (1888–1891) and
later opened the Maison de l'Art Nouveau. It is included as its own movement here because nearly
every poster principle of the 1890s traces back to it.

**Principles.**

1. **Flat colour, bounded line.** Unmodelled colour held by a confident contour. *Example:
   Toulouse-Lautrec, Divan Japonais, 1893.*
2. **The cropped frame.** Figures cut by the edge and seen from high or oblique angles. *Example:
   Toulouse-Lautrec, Ambassadeurs: Aristide Bruant, 1892.*
3. **Empty ground.** Untouched paper as an active part of the composition. *Example: Aubrey
   Beardsley, The Peacock Skirt, Salome, 1894.*

**Key works.** Siegfried Bing (ed.), *Le Japon artistique*, 1888–1891 (magazine);
Toulouse-Lautrec, *Divan Japonais*, 1893.

**Visual vocabulary.** Asymmetric composition, high horizon or none, diagonal recession through
flat planes, silhouetted figures, pattern against plain fields, vertical formats (from the
*kakemono* hanging scroll and the pillar print).

**Lineage.** Fed Art Nouveau, the Glasgow Style, the Secession, Beardsley and Whistler directly,
and later Plakatstil's flatness. The East Asia slice traces the reverse current, Japanese
modernism (`japanese-modernism`) absorbing the Western avant-garde.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Japonisme is a case study in productive misreading. Western designers took a
mature foreign system's surface (flatness, cropping, emptiness) and turned it into their own
thing. Its compositional moves still work, and the cultural-borrowing question it raises applies
to any remix.

## Art Nouveau {#art-nouveau}

**Context.** International, about 1890–1910, centred on Paris and Brussels with branches in Nancy,
Barcelona (Modernisme), Italy (Stile Liberty), Britain and the United States. It was named after
Bing's gallery, opened in December 1895. Its preconditions were technical and legal: large-stone
colour lithography, perfected commercially by Jules Chéret from the 1860s, and the French press law
of 29 July 1881, which liberalised bill-posting. Paris became a gallery of posters, and by the
mid-1890s they were collected, exhibited (the Salon des Cent) and sold in reduced editions
(*Les Maîtres de l'Affiche*, 1895–1900). The style rejected historical revival and academic
naturalism in favour of line taken from plants, Japanese prints and Celtic and Gothic manuscripts.
It was the first style built for consumer culture: theatre, cabaret, bicycles, cigarette papers,
champagne. Mackmurdo's 1883 title page is usually cited as its first appearance; Guimard's Paris
Métro entrances (1900) are its most public monument.

**Principles.**

1. **The whiplash line.** One sinuous, accelerating curve organises everything (hair, smoke,
   stems, letters). *Example: Alphonse Mucha, Job, 1896.*
2. **The poster as street art.** Large colour lithographs make the wall a gallery and the figure a
   brand ambassador. *Example: Jules Chéret, Bal au Moulin Rouge, 1889.*
3. **Silhouette over shading.** Flat colour and few contours, legible across a boulevard.
   *Example: Toulouse-Lautrec, Moulin Rouge: La Goulue, 1891.*
4. **Lettering as ornament.** Hand-drawn letters share the image's line. *Example: Will Bradley,
   The Chap-Book: The Twins, 1894.*
5. **The halo and the frame.** A circular nimbus or mosaic arch isolates the figure like an icon and
   makes the layout repeatable. *Example: Mucha, Gismonda, 1894.*

**Key works.** A. H. Mackmurdo, *Wren's City Churches*, 1883; Jules Chéret, *Bal au Moulin Rouge*,
1889; Toulouse-Lautrec, *Moulin Rouge: La Goulue*, 1891; Mucha, *Gismonda*, 1894; Beardsley,
*Salome*, 1894; Bradley, *The Twins*, 1894; Henry van de Velde, *Tropon*, 1898; Hector Guimard,
Paris Métro entrances, 1900.

**Visual vocabulary.** Asymmetric, organic composition; the whiplash curve; flat colour areas in
lithographic pastels (ochre, sage, dusty rose) or bold primaries (Chéret); idealised women with
flowing hair; plant forms (lilies, irises, poppies); custom display lettering with swelling,
rounded or plant-like strokes; vertical formats; circular or arched framing devices; in
black-and-white, strong mass against hairline (Beardsley).

**Lineage.** From Arts and Crafts, Japonisme, Celtic and Gothic illumination, and Pre-Raphaelite
drawing. Its geometric wing (Glasgow, Vienna, van de Velde) led toward modernism; its decorative
wing toward Art Deco (`art-deco`, avant-garde slice). Revived wholesale by psychedelic posters
(`psychedelic`, postmodern slice), which borrowed Mucha's frames and invented lettering directly.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Art Nouveau proves that one formal idea, a line with momentum, can carry an entire
visual system across poster, typeface, chair and railway station. Pick one formal gesture and let
it govern type, image and layout together. Mucha's other lesson is commercial: a figure-and-frame
template that can be refilled for any client is a design system.

### Jules Chéret {#jules-cheret}

**Context.** Jules Chéret (1836–1932) had little schooling and was apprenticed to a lithographer at
13. He worked in London from 1859 to 1866, absorbing British colour printing and poster practice,
then opened his own lithographic shop in Paris in 1866. He sold it to Imprimerie Chaix in 1881 and
stayed on as its artistic director. He was the first to make the large colour lithographic poster
an industrial product of real artistic ambition, producing over a thousand designs. His
light-filled, dancing young women became known as *Chérettes*. He received the Légion d'honneur in
1890 for his contribution to the graphic arts.

**Principles.**

1. **Three stones, maximum colour.** Build the image from a few overlapping colour stones,
   typically red, yellow and blue over a black key line. *Example: Bal Valentino, 1869.*
2. **Movement as message.** One figure mid-leap carries the event's energy. *Example: Folies-Bergère:
   La Loïe Fuller, 1893.*
3. **Letters that float.** Hand lettering curves into the image instead of sitting in a band.
   *Example: Bal au Moulin Rouge, 1889.*

**Key works.** *Orphée aux Enfers*, 1858; *Bal Valentino*, 1869; *Bal au Moulin Rouge*, 1889 (for
the cabaret's opening); *Folies-Bergère: La Loïe Fuller*, 1893.

**Visual vocabulary.** Diagonal, upward-spiralling compositions; translucent overlapping colour;
crayon texture on the stone; bright primaries graded into each other; joyful figures in motion;
brush-drawn display lettering.

**Lineage.** From Rococo painting (Tiepolo, Watteau, Fragonard) and British colour printing. Led
directly to Toulouse-Lautrec, Mucha and the entire Paris poster boom.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Chéret is the designer who made the production system the creative edge: he
designed for his three or four stones the way a motion designer designs for a frame rate. His
figures sell a feeling, not a product, which is exactly what most brand campaigns still do.

### Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec {#henri-de-toulouse-lautrec}

**Context.** Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec (1864–1901), an aristocrat from Albi, settled in Montmartre
and recorded its cabarets. He made only about thirty posters, but his first, *Moulin Rouge: La
Goulue* (1891), changed the medium. Where Chéret drew idealised types, Lautrec drew recognisable
people, reduced to graphic signs, using Japanese flat planes and cropping. His posters for the
singer Aristide Bruant and the dancer Jane Avril made performers into brands.

**Principles.**

1. **The silhouette as character.** A person reduced to one flat, unmistakable shape. *Example:
   Ambassadeurs: Aristide Bruant, 1892, the black hat and red scarf.*
2. **Layered planes.** Cropped foreground, subject and flat background build space without
   perspective. *Example: Moulin Rouge: La Goulue, 1891, the silhouetted crowd and the grey
   profile of Valentin le Désossé in front.*
3. **Spatter as tone.** *Crachis*, ink spattered across the stone with a toothbrush, gives texture
   and tone without hatching. *Example: Divan Japonais, 1893.*

**Key works.** *Moulin Rouge: La Goulue*, 1891; *Ambassadeurs: Aristide Bruant*, 1892; *Divan
Japonais*, 1893; *Jane Avril* (Jardin de Paris), 1893.

**Visual vocabulary.** Flat areas of colour without modelling; bold black contour; caricature;
radical cropping; high viewpoints and diagonal floors; spattered texture; loose, hand-written
lettering.

**Lineage.** From Degas, Japanese prints and Chéret. Fed the twentieth-century illustrative
poster, caricature and editorial illustration, and every portrait reduced to an icon.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Lautrec shows how to make a person into a logo without losing the person. For a
founder-led brand, a podcast or a performer, find the one silhouette detail that is theirs and
build the identity from it.

### Eugène Grasset {#eugene-grasset}

**Context.** Eugène Grasset (1845–1917), born in Lausanne, trained in architecture and settled in
Paris in 1871. He began as a furniture and textile designer steeped in medieval art. His
*Histoire des quatre fils Aymon* (1883), with text and colour illustration printed together by
Charles Gillot's photo-relief process, is a landmark of the integrated illustrated book. In the
1890s he made posters and American magazine covers, drew the Grasset typeface (patented by
Georges Peignot in 1897, issued by Peignot & Fils in 1898), and taught ornament. His *La Plante et
ses applications ornementales* (1896) and *Méthode de composition ornementale* (1905) systematised
the style for a generation of students.

**Principles.**

1. **Text and image in one plate.** Illustration, border and text interpenetrate on every page.
   *Example: Histoire des quatre fils Aymon, 1883.*
2. **Contour and flat fill.** A thick, even outline encloses flat colour, a stained-glass logic.
   *Example: Harper's Magazine Christmas cover, 1892.*
3. **Ornament from the plant, by method.** Study a real plant's structure, then derive motifs from
   it step by step. *Example: La Plante et ses applications ornementales, 1896.*

**Key works.** *Histoire des quatre fils Aymon*, 1883 (book); *Harper's Magazine* Christmas cover,
1892; *Salon des Cent: Exposition E. Grasset*, 1894 (poster); Grasset typeface, 1898; *Méthode de
composition ornementale*, 1905 (book).

**Visual vocabulary.** Even black contour; muted flat colour; medieval costume and settings;
plant-based borders; solid, slightly archaic hand lettering.

**Lineage.** From the Gothic revival and Japanese prints. Taught Art Nouveau method in France, and
his early American covers helped trigger the American poster craze of the 1890s.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Grasset is the systematiser: he wrote down the method. Every style someone wants
to scale needs its *Méthode*, a step-by-step from observation to motif.

### Alphonse Mucha {#alphonse-mucha}

**Context.** Alphonse Mucha (1860–1939), from Ivančice in Moravia, was a struggling illustrator in
Paris when, at Christmas 1894, he was asked to design a poster for Sarah Bernhardt in *Gismonda*.
It went up on the boulevards around New Year 1895 and made him famous overnight. Bernhardt put him
under contract, and his posters for her ran to 1899. His decorative panels (*The Seasons*, 1896,
and many series after) and his advertising for Job cigarette papers, Moët & Chandon and Nestlé
made the "style Mucha" synonymous with Art Nouveau. *Documents décoratifs* (1902) published the
system as a pattern book. He later returned to Bohemia and painted *The Slav Epic*.

**Principles.**

1. **The template.** A repeatable layout (figure, circular halo, mosaic arch, lettering band) that
   can be refilled for any client. *Example: Gismonda, 1894, and the Bernhardt posters through
   1899.*
2. **The tall format.** An extreme vertical, near-life-size sheet turns the figure into a standing
   presence. *Example: Gismonda, about two metres tall, 1894.*
3. **A pattern book for the style.** Publish the system itself so others can apply it. *Example:
   Documents décoratifs, 1902.*

**Key works.** *Gismonda*, 1894; *Job*, 1896; *The Seasons*, 1896 (panels); *Documents décoratifs*,
1902 (book); *The Slav Epic*, 1912–1926 (paintings).

**Visual vocabulary.** Idealised women with abundant, coiling hair; circular haloes; Byzantine
mosaic and arch frames; flowers; muted pastel palettes with gold; letterforms with rounded,
interlocking forms in a band at top or bottom.

**Lineage.** From Chéret, Grasset, Czech folk art and Byzantine mosaics. Directly reused by the San
Francisco psychedelic posters of the 1960s (`psychedelic`, postmodern slice) and continually by
fantasy illustration, tattoo and packaging.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Mucha shipped a design system with a template, a component library and a
documentation site (*Documents décoratifs*). The lesson is to design the frame once, well enough
that the content can change every time.

### Aubrey Beardsley {#aubrey-beardsley}

**Context.** Aubrey Beardsley (1872–1898) had a working career of about six years before
tuberculosis killed him at 25. His first commission, Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* for J. M. Dent
(1893–1894), imitated Kelmscott at a fraction of the cost by drawing for the new photomechanical
line block instead of hand-cut wood. His drawings for the English edition of Oscar Wilde's
*Salome* (1894), his covers as art editor of *The Yellow Book* (1894–1895) and his work for *The
Savoy* (1896) made black-and-white illustration as graphic, and as provocative, as anything in
the decade. He lost the *Yellow Book* post after Wilde's arrest in April 1895.

**Principles.**

1. **Black and white, nothing between.** Pure black against pure white, no grey, built to survive
   cheap line-block reproduction. *Example: The Peacock Skirt, Salome, 1894.*
2. **Line against mass.** Hairline detail set against huge flat blacks, each amplifying the other.
   *Example: The Climax, Salome, 1894.*
3. **The cover as a brand.** A bold, flat cover image gives a periodical a recognisable face.
   *Example: The Yellow Book, volume 1, April 1894.*

**Key works.** *Le Morte d'Arthur*, 1893–1894 (book); *Salome*, 1894 (book); *The Yellow Book*,
1894 (magazine); *The Savoy*, 1896 (magazine).

**Visual vocabulary.** Absolute black-and-white; hairline contours; large flat black masses;
peacock-feather and dotted-line ornament; elongated, decadent figures; asymmetric, Japanese-style
empty fields.

**Lineage.** From Japanese prints, Burne-Jones, Greek vase painting and Whistler. Fed Bradley and
American Art Nouveau, Glasgow, Vienna (Ver Sacrum published his work), Jugendstil illustration
and later comics and editorial illustration.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Beardsley designed for the cheapest reproduction available and made the
constraint the style. Two-tone, high-contrast work is still the most robust image language there
is (favicons, stickers, single-colour print, dark mode), and Beardsley shows how far it can go.

### The Beggarstaffs {#beggarstaffs}

**Context.** The Beggarstaffs were the brothers-in-law James Pryde (1866–1941) and William
Nicholson (1872–1949), who designed posters together in London for only a few years in the 1890s.
They worked from cut paper: they arranged flat shapes and then had the design printed in a few
flat colours. Their reduction (big silhouettes, no outlines where figure and ground share a
colour) was a commercial failure in its day, since several designs were never used, but it
influenced designers for decades. Nicholson went on to the woodcut books *An Alphabet* and *An
Almanac of Twelve Sports* (both 1898).

**Principles.**

1. **Collage of flat shapes.** Compose by cutting and arranging coloured paper. *Example: Hamlet,
   1894.*
2. **Let the eye close the shape.** Leave out contours where figure and ground share a colour.
   *Example: Girl on a Sofa, 1895.*
3. **Two colours and a big shape.** A very few flat colours and one dominant silhouette sized for
   the hoarding. *Example: Rowntree's Elect Cocoa, 1896.*

**Key works.** *Hamlet*, 1894; *Don Quixote* (Lyceum Theatre), 1895; *Harper's Magazine*, 1895;
*Rowntree's Elect Cocoa*, 1896.

**Visual vocabulary.** Large flat silhouettes in black, brown, ochre and red; no outlines; implied
contours; minimal, solid lettering; generous empty paper.

**Lineage.** From Japanese prints, Whistler and Velázquez's dark grounds. Fed Plakatstil directly,
and the reduced British and German poster through the 1920s.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** The Beggarstaffs worked with cut paper, and closure was their method: let the
viewer finish the shape. In motion, icon and identity work, edges that are implied rather than
drawn make the viewer participate, and the image stays light.

### Will Bradley {#will-bradley}

**Context.** Will Bradley (1868–1962), largely self-taught, worked in Chicago printing in the early
1890s. His covers for the trade journal *The Inland Printer* (from 1894) and his posters for
Stone & Kimball's literary magazine *The Chap-Book* (*The Twins*, 1894) adapted Beardsley's line
and Japanese flatness into an American Art Nouveau. The Central Type Foundry cut his lettering as
Bradley type (1894). He then moved toward the Arts and Crafts revival of colonial American printing:
he founded the Wayside Press in Springfield, Massachusetts (1895), published *Bradley: His Book*
(1896), and designed type and ornament for American Type Founders (Wayside Roman, 1900). He later
worked as an art editor and art director in magazines and film.

**Principles.**

1. **Repeat for rhythm.** Duplicate or mirror a figure to turn one drawing into a pattern.
   *Example: The Chap-Book: The Twins, 1894.*
2. **Type and drawing share one weight.** Lettering and image use the same weight of black and fuse
   into a single block. *Example: The Inland Printer covers, 1894–1895.*
3. **Look backward to move forward.** Revive colonial chapbook typography (Caslon, woodcut ornament)
   as a fresh vernacular. *Example: Bradley: His Book, 1896.*

**Key works.** *The Inland Printer* covers, 1894–1895; *The Chap-Book: The Twins*, 1894 (poster);
*Bradley: His Book*, 1896 (magazine); Wayside Roman, 1900 (typeface).

**Visual vocabulary.** Flat colour, often two or three; repeated figures; heavy black lettering
blocks; bold borders; later, Caslon, rules and colonial woodcut ornament.

**Lineage.** From Beardsley, Morris and Grasset. Fed the American poster craze and the American
private press; anticipates the American art director (`american-modernism`, american-modernism
slice).

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Bradley shows the generalist designer at work: printer, type designer, art
director and publisher in one person, switching styles on purpose. His shift from Art Nouveau to
colonial revival is a reminder that the vernacular of your own place is a design resource.

### Henry van de Velde {#henry-van-de-velde}

**Context.** Henry van de Velde (1863–1957), a Belgian painter in Antwerp and Brussels, gave up
painting in the early 1890s for design, under the influence of Morris. He built his own house,
Bloemenwerf, in Uccle (1895), designed interiors for Bing's Maison de l'Art Nouveau (1895), and
moved to Germany around 1899. In Weimar he founded the Kunstgewerbeschule (building 1907), which
merged with the art academy in 1919 to become the Bauhaus. His poster for the food concentrate
Tropon (1898) is pure abstract line. At the 1914 Werkbund congress in Cologne he defended the
designer's individuality against Hermann Muthesius's call for standardised types (*Typisierung*).

**Principles.**

1. **Abstract line as force.** Line carries energy and emotion with no flowers or figures. *Example:
   Tropon, 1898.*
2. **The artist's individuality.** The designer's free invention against standardised types.
   *Example: his counter-theses at the Werkbund congress, Cologne, 1914.*

**Key works.** Villa Bloemenwerf, 1895 (building); *Tropon*, 1898 (poster);
Kunstgewerbeschule Weimar, 1907 (building); Werkbund Theatre, Cologne, 1914 (building).

**Visual vocabulary.** Abstract, dynamic, swelling curves; ochre, orange and dark green; lettering
built from the same lines as the ornament.

**Lineage.** From Morris, the Belgian avant-garde and Art Nouveau. Bridges Art Nouveau and Jugendstil
to the Bauhaus (`bauhaus`, avant-garde slice).

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Van de Velde's side of the 1914 argument, the individual against the standard, is
the argument every design-system team has. He lost it historically, but the case for authored
form inside a standardised system is worth making again.

## Glasgow Style {#glasgow-school}

**Context.** Glasgow, about 1890–1910. Four students at the Glasgow School of Art, Charles Rennie
Mackintosh, Herbert MacNair, and the sisters Margaret and Frances Macdonald, met around 1892 and
worked as "The Four". The school's headmaster Francis Newbery encouraged them. Critics derided their
attenuated, ghostly figures as the "Spook School". Their 1890s posters and Mackintosh's Glasgow
School of Art building (1897–1909) took Art Nouveau in an austere, rectilinear direction: curves
held by grids, colour reduced to white, black, mauve and silver. The Four were better received
abroad than at home. Through *The Studio* magazine and their room at the 8th Secession exhibition
in Vienna (1900), they gave the Viennese a model for a geometric, total design.

**Principles.**

1. **Verticality.** Forms stretched into columns. *Example: Mackintosh, The Scottish Musical
   Review, 1896.*
2. **Curve held by the grid.** Organic motifs boxed by straight lines and squares. *Example: Glasgow
   School of Art, 1897–1909.*
3. **The stylised rose.** One reduced, repeatable motif travels across media as a house mark.
   *Example: Margaret Macdonald, The May Queen, 1900.*
4. **The room as one work.** Lettering, stencils, furniture and cutlery as one environment.
   *Example: Willow Tea Rooms, Room de Luxe, 1903.*

**Key works.** Margaret and Frances Macdonald with Herbert MacNair, Glasgow Institute of the Fine
Arts poster, 1895; Mackintosh, *The Scottish Musical Review*, 1896 (poster); Mackintosh, Glasgow
School of Art, 1897–1909 (building); Mackintosh, Hill House, 1902–1904 (building); Mackintosh and
Macdonald, Willow Tea Rooms, 1903.

**Visual vocabulary.** Tall, narrow formats; symmetrical, emblematic figures; stylised roses and
seed pods; grids of small squares; restrained palettes (white, mauve, pink, silver, black);
elongated, idiosyncratic hand lettering with high crossbars.

**Lineage.** From Arts and Crafts, Celtic ornament, Japonisme and Beardsley. Fed the Vienna Secession
and Wiener Werkstätte directly, and so the geometric turn toward modernism.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Glasgow shows how a hard grid and one soft motif can give a whole identity
system its warmth. The practical lesson is the restricted signature (a single motif and a single
accent colour) applied consistently across every surface.

### Charles Rennie Mackintosh {#charles-rennie-mackintosh}

**Context.** Charles Rennie Mackintosh (1868–1928) apprenticed as an architect from 1884 while
studying at night at the Glasgow School of Art. With Margaret Macdonald (whom he married in 1900),
Frances Macdonald and Herbert MacNair he formed The Four. His 1896 poster for *The Scottish Musical
Review* shows the style at its most graphic. The competition win for the new Glasgow School of
Art (built 1897–1909) made his reputation, and the tea rooms for Catherine Cranston and Hill House
for Walter Blackie carried it into total interiors. Vienna embraced his work in 1900; Glasgow
mostly did not, and his architectural practice largely ended when he left Glasgow in 1914.

**Principles.**

1. **The vertical stretch.** Elongate figures, chair backs and lettering until they read as
   columns. *Example: The Scottish Musical Review, 1896.*
2. **Square and rose.** A grid of small squares paired with one soft organic motif. *Example:
   Willow Tea Rooms, 1903.*
3. **Light and dark rooms.** Environments in white or dark keys, colour kept for tiny accents.
   *Example: Hill House interiors, 1902–1904.*

**Key works.** *The Scottish Musical Review*, 1896 (poster); Glasgow School of Art, 1897–1909
(building); Hill House, 1902–1904 (building); Willow Tea Rooms, 1903 (building and interiors).

**Visual vocabulary.** Extreme verticals; lattice and square grids; stylised roses; white enamel,
dark oak and stained glass; idiosyncratic lettering with high, open forms.

**Lineage.** From Arts and Crafts and Scottish vernacular building. Fed Hoffmann and Moser, the
Wiener Werkstätte and early modernism.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Mackintosh's alphabet of square, lattice and rose is a ready-made identity kit, and
it has been flattened into tourist kitsch. What survives the kitsch is the discipline: one grid,
one motif, one accent colour, designed together across sign, room and object.

### Margaret Macdonald {#margaret-macdonald}

**Context.** Margaret Macdonald (1864–1933), born in Tipton, Staffordshire, studied at the Glasgow
School of Art with her sister Frances. With Frances and Herbert MacNair she designed the poster for
the Glasgow Institute of the Fine Arts (1895), one of the earliest full statements of the Glasgow
Style, and she worked as an equal partner with Mackintosh on the tea rooms and the Turin
exhibition of 1902. Her gesso panels, built from plaster, string, beads and metal leaf, supplied
the figurative centre of their interiors. Her contribution was long underplayed beside her
husband's and has been substantially re-credited since the late twentieth century.

**Principles.**

1. **The emblematic figure.** Attenuated, symmetrical figures used as signs, almost as lettering.
   *Example: Glasgow Institute of the Fine Arts poster, 1895.*
2. **Surface as relief.** Gesso, string and beads build a low relief that catches light.
   *Example: The May Queen, 1900.*

**Key works.** Glasgow Institute of the Fine Arts poster (with Frances Macdonald and Herbert
MacNair), 1895; *The May Queen*, gesso panel for the Ingram Street tea rooms, 1900; *The White Rose
and the Red Rose*, gesso panel, 1902.

**Visual vocabulary.** Elongated, symmetrical female figures; rose and briar; muted greens, pinks and
silver; raised gesso line.

**Lineage.** From the Pre-Raphaelites, Celtic manuscript and Symbolism. Fed Klimt's and the
Secession's flat, figurative ornament.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Macdonald's gesso is graphic design in low relief: line drawn with string. Use it
as a model for print finishes (embossing, foil, varnish) that draw rather than just decorate.

## Vienna Secession {#vienna-secession}

**Context.** Vienna, 1897–1914 (with the Wiener Werkstätte to 1932). On 3 April 1897 a group led by
Gustav Klimt, with Koloman Moser, Josef Hoffmann, Joseph Maria Olbrich and others, left the
conservative Künstlerhaus. They took the name from the Munich Secession of 1892. Within two years
they had a building (Olbrich's Secession, 1898), a magazine (*Ver Sacrum*, 1898–1903) and an
exhibition programme in which every show was itself designed as a work. Glasgow and British Arts
and Crafts were the models. The 14th exhibition (1902), devoted to Beethoven and installed by
Hoffmann around Klimt's *Beethoven Frieze*, was the high point of the Gesamtkunstwerk. In 1903
Hoffmann, Moser and the banker Fritz Waerndorfer founded the Wiener Werkstätte to make the whole
designed environment. In 1905 Klimt's group, including Hoffmann, left the Secession. Over its
first decade Vienna pushed Art Nouveau from the curve toward the square.

**Principles.**

1. **The square module.** The square and the grid become the organising unit at every scale.
   *Example: Josef Hoffmann, perforated-metal Gitterwerk objects, Wiener Werkstätte, c. 1904.*
2. **Lettering as architecture.** Condensed, blocky letters fitted into rectangles become a
   structural field. *Example: Koloman Moser, poster for the 13th Secession exhibition, 1902.*
3. **Flat field, precise figure.** A large flat or empty area punctuated by a small, exactly placed
   element. *Example: Gustav Klimt, poster for the 1st Secession exhibition, 1898.*
4. **Gesamtkunstwerk.** Exhibition, building, magazine and object as one work. *Example: the 14th
   Secession exhibition, 1902.*
5. **The maker's mark as identity.** Monograms for designer, craftsman and workshop on every object.
   *Example: Wiener Werkstätte rose mark and monograms, 1903.*

**Key works.** Klimt, poster for the 1st Secession exhibition, 1898; Olbrich, Secession Building,
1898; *Ver Sacrum*, 1898–1903 (magazine); Moser, poster for the 13th Secession exhibition, 1902;
Klimt, *Beethoven Frieze*, 1902; Hoffmann, Palais Stoclet, Brussels, 1905–1911.

**Visual vocabulary.** Square formats (*Ver Sacrum* is almost square); square-cut, condensed,
geometric display lettering, often in blocks that fill a rectangle; black-and-white chequers and
grids; gold; flat colour fields with a small figurative element; strict symmetry; monograms.

**Lineage.** From Arts and Crafts, Glasgow, Japonisme and Art Nouveau. Fed Art Deco (`art-deco`,
avant-garde slice), the Werkbund, Plakatstil's flat block lettering, and early modernism. The
Secession's "To every age its art" became the founding claim of every avant-garde after it.

**Quotes.** The motto inscribed over the entrance of the Secession Building, written by the critic
Ludwig Hevesi: "Der Zeit ihre Kunst, der Kunst ihre Freiheit" ("To every age its art, to art its
freedom").

**Remix today.** Vienna is the most directly usable style of the period: square formats, condensed
block lettering, black-and-white grids, one precise accent. Its deeper lesson is that the
exhibition, the magazine and the object are all one identity, designed by the same people at the
same time.

### Gustav Klimt {#gustav-klimt}

**Context.** Gustav Klimt (1862–1918) trained at the Vienna Kunstgewerbeschule and made his name as
a decorative painter of public buildings. He was a founder and the first president of the
Secession (1897) and contributed to *Ver Sacrum*. His poster for the 1st Secession exhibition
(1898) showed Theseus killing the Minotaur in a narrow band at the top and Athena at the side, over
a nearly empty sheet. The censor objected to Theseus's nudity, and a second state added a tree
trunk across him. He left the Secession with the Klimt group in 1905.

**Principles.**

1. **The empty middle.** Leave the centre blank and push image and text to the edges; the void is
   the event. *Example: poster for the 1st Secession exhibition, 1898.*
2. **Pattern as space.** Flat ornamental fields of gold, spirals and squares replace perspective
   around naturalistic faces and hands. *Example: Beethoven Frieze, 1902.*

**Key works.** Poster for the 1st Secession exhibition, 1898; *Ver Sacrum* contributions, from
1898; *Beethoven Frieze*, 1902; designs for the *Stoclet Frieze*, Palais Stoclet, c. 1910–1911.

**Visual vocabulary.** Gold leaf; flat ornamental fields; spirals, eyes and rectangles; tall, narrow
formats; naturalistic faces set into abstract pattern.

**Lineage.** From Byzantine mosaic, Japonisme and Symbolism. Fed the Secession's graphic language,
Art Deco luxury and decorative illustration since.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry (the Secession motto is Hevesi's, not Klimt's).

**Remix today.** Klimt's first poster is a lesson in negative space as provocation: it is mostly
empty, and it was censored anyway. Use the void as the strongest element of the layout.

### Koloman Moser {#koloman-moser}

**Context.** Koloman Moser (1868–1918) trained in painting at the Vienna Academy and then at the
Kunstgewerbeschule. A Secession founder, he was the lead designer of *Ver Sacrum* (1898–1903) and
designed exhibition posters, catalogues, pattern portfolios (*Die Quelle*) and, with Hoffmann, the
Wiener Werkstätte (1903). He left the Werkstätte in 1907 and designed Austria's 1908 jubilee stamps
and banknotes, bringing the Secession's language to the state's own graphics.

**Principles.**

1. **Letters as building blocks.** Condensed, rectangular letters lock into a solid field.
   *Example: poster for the 13th Secession exhibition, 1902.*
2. **Pattern by module.** Ornament generated by repeating and rotating one unit on a grid.
   *Example: Die Quelle portfolio, 1901.*
3. **The state as client.** The modern style applied to postage and currency. *Example: Austrian
   jubilee stamps, 1908.*

**Key works.** *Ver Sacrum*, 1898–1903 (magazine design); poster for the 13th Secession exhibition,
1902; Wiener Werkstätte marks and stationery (with Hoffmann), 1903; Austrian jubilee stamps, 1908.

**Visual vocabulary.** Square formats; condensed geometric capitals; chequerboards; rhythmic
modular pattern; stylised figures in profile; restrained palettes of black, white, gold and one
colour.

**Lineage.** From Glasgow and Japanese stencils (*katagami*). Fed Art Deco, the geometric strand of
modern graphic design, and systematic pattern design.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Moser's pattern portfolios are generative design avant la lettre: one module plus
a rule gives an endless surface. Set up a pattern as a unit with its transformation rules, and
let it run across a brand.

### Josef Hoffmann {#josef-hoffmann}

**Context.** Josef Hoffmann (1870–1956), from Brtnice in Moravia, studied architecture under Otto
Wagner, co-founded the Secession (1897), designed its exhibitions (including the 14th, 1902) and
in 1903 founded the Wiener Werkstätte with Moser and Fritz Waerndorfer. His fondness for the
square earned him the nickname "Quadratl-Hoffmann". The Purkersdorf Sanatorium (1904) and the
Palais Stoclet in Brussels (1905–1911) are his total works. The workshop's working programme (1905)
put quality of making ahead of quantity of output.

**Principles.**

1. **The square everywhere.** One module at every scale, from basket to facade. *Example: Sanatorium
   Purkersdorf, 1904.*
2. **Better ten days on one thing.** Quality of making is the brand; slow and excellent over fast and
   mediocre. *Example: the Wiener Werkstätte working programme, 1905 (paraphrased).*
3. **Contour as frame.** Edges outlined in a contrasting band, so form reads as drawing. *Example:
   Palais Stoclet, 1905–1911, white stone edged with dark metal.*

**Key works.** Wiener Werkstätte, 1903; Sanatorium Purkersdorf, 1904 (building); Sitzmaschine,
c. 1905 (product); Palais Stoclet, 1905–1911 (building); Kubus armchair, 1910 (product).

**Visual vocabulary.** Square grids; perforated sheet metal; black-and-white; cubic volumes;
contrasting edge bands; monograms.

**Lineage.** From Wagner, Glasgow and Arts and Crafts. Fed Art Deco and modernist architecture and
product design.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Hoffmann's single module at every scale is the ancestor of the 4px or 8px spacing
grid. Pick one unit and let it decide everything, then outline the edges so the structure is
visible.

## Jugendstil {#jugendstil}

**Context.** Germany, about 1895–1914; named after *Jugend* ("Youth"), the Munich weekly founded by
Georg Hirth in 1896. The Berlin art quarterly *Pan* (1895) and the satirical weekly *Simplicissimus*
(1896, its red bulldog drawn by Thomas Theodor Heine) gave it two further platforms. Jugendstil
started floral, close to French Art Nouveau, and turned abstract and then geometric, especially at
the Darmstadt Artists' Colony (founded 1899 by Grand Duke Ernst Ludwig of Hesse), where Olbrich and
Behrens built a designed community and showed it in 1901 as *Ein Dokument deutscher Kunst*. Germany
was the first country to industrialise the reform: the 1907 Werkbund tied artists to industry, and
Behrens's work for AEG grew out of Jugendstil.

**Principles.**

1. **The magazine as laboratory.** Cover art changed every issue, a running test bed. *Example:
   Jugend, Munich, 1896.*
2. **Typefaces drawn with a brush.** Type adopts the ornament's swelling stroke. *Example: Otto
   Eckmann, Eckmann-Schrift, 1900.*
3. **From curve to abstraction.** Natural forms pushed toward pure line and then geometric
   rigour. *Example: Peter Behrens, Der Kuss, colour woodcut for Pan, 1898.*
4. **The artists' colony.** A designed community as one demonstration. *Example: Darmstadt,
   Ein Dokument deutscher Kunst, 1901.*

**Key works.** *Pan*, 1895 (magazine); *Jugend*, 1896 (magazine); T. T. Heine, *Simplicissimus*
bulldog, 1896; Behrens, *Der Kuss*, 1898; Eckmann, Eckmann-Schrift, 1900 (typeface); Behrens,
Behrens-Schrift, 1902 (typeface).

**Visual vocabulary.** Brush-drawn display type; swans, waves and stylised plants; flat colour
woodcuts; magazine mastheads redrawn per issue; later, geometric frames and Behrens's
square-cut types.

**Lineage.** From Art Nouveau, Arts and Crafts and Japonisme. Fed Plakatstil, Behrens's corporate
design and through the Werkbund and Weimar the Bauhaus (`bauhaus`, avant-garde slice).

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Jugend's cover-per-issue habit is the model for a brand that keeps a fixed masthead
and lets everything else change. And Jugendstil's journey from ornament to geometry within a
decade shows a style can evolve inside one institution if its designers are allowed to.

### Otto Eckmann {#otto-eckmann}

**Context.** Otto Eckmann (1865–1902), from Hamburg, trained as a painter. In 1894 he auctioned
off his paintings to devote himself to applied design. He drew for *Pan* (from 1895) and *Jugend*
(from 1896), did graphic work for AEG from 1900 to 1902, and designed the typefaces Eckmann
(1900) and Fette Eckmann (1902), the most widely used Jugendstil faces. He died of tuberculosis at
36.

**Principles.**

1. **Type from the brush.** Letters written with a broad brush, matching the plant ornament around
   them. *Example: Eckmann-Schrift, 1900.*
2. **Applied over fine.** The cover and the typeface treated as the serious work. *Example: his
   1894 auction of his paintings and turn to Pan and Jugend.*

**Key works.** *Pan* and *Jugend* contributions, 1895–1896; Eckmann-Schrift, 1900 (typeface); Fette
Eckmann, 1902 (typeface).

**Visual vocabulary.** Brush-stroke letters with swelling terminals, mixing roman and blackletter
features; swans and water; flat woodcut colour.

**Lineage.** From Japanese woodcut and Art Nouveau. Fed German display typography and Behrens's
AEG.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Eckmann made type and illustration one gesture. A brand whose custom typeface is
drawn by the same hand, with the same tool, as its illustrations gets coherence for free.

### Peter Behrens {#peter-behrens}

**Context.** Peter Behrens (1868–1940), from Hamburg, started as a painter in the Munich Secession
and made Jugendstil woodcuts (*Der Kuss*, c. 1898, for *Pan*). In 1899 he joined the Darmstadt
Artists' Colony, built his own house there (1901) and designed everything in it, and drew the
Behrens-Schrift (1902) for the Klingspor foundry. In 1907 AEG, Germany's giant electrical company,
retained him as artistic consultant, and he became a founding member of the Werkbund the same
year. For AEG he designed factories (the Turbine Factory, 1909), products (kettles, 1909; clocks;
arc lamps), catalogues, price lists, advertising, typefaces and the trademark. It was the first
time one designer gave a company one visual identity. Walter Gropius, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe
and Le Corbusier all worked in his office around 1908–1911.

**Principles.**

1. **One hand for everything.** The same designer shapes the factory, the kettle, the catalogue,
   the type and the mark. *Example: AEG, from 1907.*
2. **Geometry over ornament.** Naturalistic decoration replaced by grids and geometric marks.
   *Example: the AEG hexagonal honeycomb trademark, 1908.*
3. **Type standards.** A house typeface used consistently. *Example: Behrens-Antiqua, used across
   AEG print, 1908.*
4. **Standard parts, varied products.** A small set of interchangeable components combined into a
   range. *Example: AEG electric kettles, 1909.*

**Key works.** *Der Kuss*, c. 1898 (woodcut); Behrens house, Darmstadt, 1901 (building);
Behrens-Schrift, 1902 (typeface); AEG trademark, 1908 (identity); AEG electric kettles, 1909
(product); AEG Turbine Factory, Berlin, 1909 (building).

**Visual vocabulary.** At AEG: geometric frames, honeycomb hexagons, modular grids, a classicising
roman (Behrens-Antiqua) in disciplined centred layouts, restrained colour, products photographed
or drawn objectively.

**Lineage.** From Jugendstil and Darmstadt, and the Werkbund's industrial ambitions. Fed the Bauhaus
through Gropius, modernist architecture through Mies and Le Corbusier, and corporate identity
(`corporate-identity`, american-modernism slice), which is why that movement is also listed for
him.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Behrens at AEG is the first brand system in the modern sense, and it was built by
one person who designed the buildings as well as the letterhead. The thing to take is the scope:
an identity covers product, place, type and print, and it is most coherent when they come from
one hand.

## Plakatstil {#plakatstil}

**Context.** Berlin and Munich, about 1905–1920. Germany's advertising market was growing fast and
its printers competed for artists. In Berlin, the printing firm Hollerbaum & Schmidt, led by the
advertising man Ernst Growald, signed a stable of young designers, including Lucian Bernhard,
Julius Klinger, Hans Rudi Erdt and Julius Gipkens. Bernhard's entry for a Priester matches
competition (c. 1905–1906) is the founding image: two matches and the word "Priester" on a
dark ground. The *Sachplakat* ("object poster") reduced the poster to the product, its name and a
few flat colours. Munich, led by Ludwig Hohlwein, kept the figure but flattened it into pattern
and light. The Verein der Plakatfreunde (1905) and its journal *Das Plakat* (1910–1921) treated
posters as collectible art. The First World War turned the same language to war loans and
propaganda.

**Principles.**

1. **The object is the message.** Only the product, alone and large; no scene, no figure, no slogan.
   *Example: Lucian Bernhard, Priester, c. 1906.*
2. **One word.** The brand name, in heavy hand-drawn capitals, is the whole text. *Example: Lucian
   Bernhard, Stiller, 1908.*
3. **Flat colour, few colours.** Two to four flat colours on a strong ground, legible in a second
   from a moving tram. *Example: Hans Rudi Erdt, Opel, 1911.*
4. **Pattern and light as texture.** In Munich, the figure dissolves into interlocking flat patches
   of light and shade. *Example: Ludwig Hohlwein, Hermann Scherrer, 1911.*

**Key works.** Bernhard, *Priester*, c. 1906; Bernhard, *Stiller*, 1908; Julius Klinger, *Möhring*
candelabra factory, 1908; *Das Plakat*, from 1910 (magazine); Erdt, *Opel*, 1911; Hohlwein,
*Hermann Scherrer*, 1911; Bernhard, *Bosch*, 1914.

**Visual vocabulary.** A single product, frontal or in profile; heavy, hand-drawn sans-serif or
Fraktur-derived capitals; dark or saturated flat grounds; two to four flat colours; no outlines
or heavy outlines; plenty of empty space. In Munich, patterned textiles and sunlight rendered as
flat shapes.

**Lineage.** From the Beggarstaffs, Jugendstil, the Secession's block lettering, and the
Japanese-print flatness of Art Nouveau. Fed Art Deco and the Swiss object poster
(`swiss-style`, swiss-international slice), which carried the *Sachplakat* forward from the
1920s to the 1950s, as well as American modernism and the logo-plus-product of modern advertising.
Bernhard emigrated to New York in 1923.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Plakatstil is the source of the product-on-a-colour-field ad that fills every
social feed, and its rules still hold: one object, one word, two colours, read in a second. What
modern feeds mostly lack is its lettering. The name is hand-drawn for this product, which is why
it reads as a mark rather than a caption.

### Lucian Bernhard {#lucian-bernhard}

**Context.** Lucian Bernhard (born Emil Kahn, 1883–1972) was largely self-taught. In Berlin he
entered the Priester matches competition with a design that the judges reportedly set aside
until Growald championed it; it became the founding image of the *Sachplakat* (c. 1906).
Hollerbaum & Schmidt published his work for Stiller shoes, Manoli cigarettes and Bosch. He
designed typefaces (Bernhard Antiqua, Bernhard Fraktur) and trademarks, taught poster design in
Berlin, and in 1923 moved to New York, where he continued as a type and poster designer.

**Principles.**

1. **Subtract until it breaks.** Remove everything that is not the product or its name, then check it
   still reads from across the street. *Example: Priester, c. 1906.*
2. **The name as the hero.** The brand name gets as much visual weight as the object. *Example:
   Stiller, 1908.*
3. **Contrasting fields.** Object and lettering on a dark, flat ground with one hot accent.
   *Example: Bosch, 1914.*

**Key works.** *Priester*, c. 1906; *Stiller*, 1908; *Bosch*, 1914.

**Visual vocabulary.** One object, centred or slightly offset; heavy hand-drawn capitals; deep
maroon, black or navy grounds with red, yellow or white accents.

**Lineage.** From the Beggarstaffs and Jugendstil. Founded Plakatstil; fed the Swiss object poster
and American advertising.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Bernhard's test, "does it still work with less?", is the most useful critique
question in identity and ad design. Run it until the next removal breaks the idea, then stop one
step before.

### Ludwig Hohlwein {#ludwig-hohlwein}

**Context.** Ludwig Hohlwein (1874–1949), from Wiesbaden, trained and practised as an architect
before turning to posters in Munich around 1906. Where Berlin stripped the figure out, Hohlwein
kept it and rebuilt it from flat, interlocking patches of light and shadow, often with patterned
textiles, producing thousands of posters for menswear, tobacco, coffee, zoos and events. His
later posters for the Nazi regime are some of the most effective propaganda of the period; that
use of his technique is part of the record and should be taught with it.

**Principles.**

1. **Light as flat shapes.** A figure built from flat zones of light and shade, with volume but no
   modelling. *Example: Hermann Scherrer, 1911.*
2. **Texture against flatness.** A patterned area (check, stripe, stipple) set against plain fields.
   *Example: Jasmatzi, 1908.*

**Key works.** *Jasmatzi*, 1908; *Hermann Scherrer*, 1911; *Tierpark Hellabrunn*, 1912.

**Visual vocabulary.** Strong sunlight and deep shadow as flat shapes; patterned fabrics; warm,
saturated colour; figures cropped by the frame; hand lettering in solid blocks.

**Lineage.** From Plakatstil, the Beggarstaffs and the Munich illustration tradition. Fed
interwar commercial illustration in Germany and beyond, and fascist propaganda.

**Quotes.** None verified for this entry.

**Remix today.** Hohlwein's flat light-and-shadow modelling is a fully transferable technique for
vector illustration and flat 3D rendering. His career is also the period's starkest lesson that a
persuasive style serves whoever commissions it.

## Disputed and uncertain dates

- **Gismonda (Mucha).** Designed and printed in late December 1894, on the walls from about
  1 January 1895. Both years appear; this slice uses 1894.
- **Priester (Bernhard).** Dated 1905 (the competition) or 1906 (publication), often "c. 1906".
  This slice uses 1906.
- **The Beggarstaffs' partnership.** James Pryde's biography gives 1893–1899; William Nicholson's
  gives 1893–1898; Meggs and most poster histories begin with their 1894 posters. This slice uses
  1894–1899.
- **Doves Press Bible.** Five volumes, dated 1902–1904 in some references and 1903–1905 in others.
  This slice uses 1903. The destruction of the Doves Type is also given variously: punches and
  matrices in 1913, the type itself in 1916–1917.
- **Job (Mucha).** Dated 1896 in most poster histories; one widely used Wikipedia listing gives
  1898 (Mucha designed more than one Job poster). This slice uses 1896.
- **The Slav Epic.** Given as 1910–1928 or 1912–1926 (painting) depending on whether conception and
  exhibition are included.
- **Der Kuss (Behrens).** Usually "c. 1898".
- **Golden Type.** Begun by 1889, completed 1890, first used in 1891; this slice uses 1890.
- **Monotype.** The company was founded in 1887, the casting machine patented in its working form
  in 1896, and the London corporation established in 1897. This slice uses 1887 for the event.
- **Grasset typeface.** Patented by Peignot in 1897, issued in 1898.
- **AEG trademark.** Behrens designed several AEG marks between 1907 and 1914; the honeycomb hexagon
  is usually dated 1908.
- **Hermann Scherrer (Hohlwein).** Hohlwein made several posters for the tailor; 1911 is the date
  most often given for the best-known version.
