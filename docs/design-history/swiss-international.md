# Swiss & International modernism, 1945–1975

Slice: `swiss-international`. Movements: Swiss Style, the Ulm School, functionalist product
design, Dutch modernism and Italian corporate modernism. Lineage: `modernism` throughout.

The thirty years after the Second World War are when modernism stopped being a polemic and
became an infrastructure. Before 1945 the New Typography was a minority position argued in
manifestos by Tschichold, Moholy-Nagy and the Bauhaus; by 1975 its descendants were running the
signage of airports, the identities of airlines, the typefaces of every phototypesetter and the
look of the Olympic Games. The engine of that change sat in a handful of small places: two
craft schools in Zürich and Basel, a design college on a hill above Ulm, an electrical goods
company in Frankfurt, a typewriter firm in Ivrea and a multidisciplinary studio in Amsterdam.

What unites them is a belief that design is a *method* rather than a style: that a problem,
analysed honestly, produces its own form; that a grid, a type family or a product system can be
designed once and then generate many correct answers; and that the designer's personality is
something to be disciplined, not displayed. What divides them is equally useful to a designer
today. The Swiss made that method visual and teachable. Ulm made it scientific and political.
Braun made it physical. The Dutch made it playful and technological. The Italians proved it
could be warm, sensual and commercially seductive at the same time.

A note on dates: where sources disagree, the prose says so and the JSON takes the date most
commonly given by the primary institution (the manufacturer, foundry or museum).

## Swiss Style {#swiss-style}

Also called the International Typographic Style. Zürich and Basel, c. 1950–1975, with roots in
the 1920s and a long revival tail.

### Context

Switzerland came out of the war intact: its printing industry, type foundries and schools had
kept working while the rest of Europe's were destroyed. It was trilingual, so any public
message had to work in German, French and Italian at once, which put a premium on neutral,
structured communication. Its craft schools — the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich and the Allgemeine
Gewerbeschule Basel — trained typesetters and lithographers to apprenticeship standards, so the
people designing posters could also set them.

The style grew out of three pre-war sources. The first was the New Typography of Jan Tschichold
(who lived in Basel from 1933) and the Bauhaus, which supplied asymmetry, sans-serif type and the
idea of typography as functional communication. The second was Swiss *concrete art* — Max Bill,
Richard Paul Lohse, Camille Graeser and the Allianz group (from 1937) — which supplied
mathematically ordered composition built from pure colour and geometry. The third was Ernst
Keller's teaching in Zürich from 1918, which supplied the pedagogy: problem first, form second.

It reacted against two things: the illustrative *Sachplakat* and Plakatstil of the pre-war
Swiss poster (charming, painted, built around a single object), and the emotive propaganda of
the 1930s and 1940s, which had shown what persuasive imagery could be made to do. The
objectivity of the Swiss poster was partly an ethical stance.

Technology mattered at both ends. In the 1950s the style was set in metal — above all Berthold's
Akzidenz-Grotesk, a turn-of-the-century grotesque the Swiss rediscovered. In 1957 two new
neo-grotesques appeared in the same year, Neue Haas Grotesk (renamed Helvetica in 1960) and
Univers, the latter conceived for Deberny & Peignot's Lumitype phototypesetting machine. Offset
lithography and good photographic reproduction made "objective photography" practical in place
of illustration.

The movement's manifesto was a magazine: *Neue Grafik / New Graphic Design / Graphisme actuel*,
founded in principle at a meeting in a Zürich restaurant on 15 February 1956 and published
quarterly from 1958 to 1965 by Josef Müller-Brockmann, Richard Paul Lohse, Hans Neuburg and
Carlo Vivarelli. It printed only good examples, never criticism, and set every issue on a strict
grid in Akzidenz-Grotesk in three languages. From 1960 Armin Hofmann's visiting teaching at Yale
carried the Basel version to the United States, and the Basel school's advanced course (from
1968) brought American students the other way.

### Principles

1. **The grid as a constitution.** A modular grid, fixed before any content is placed, governs
   the size and position of every element, so that the page is built rather than arranged.
   *Example:* Müller-Brockmann, *Der Film* poster for the Kunstgewerbemuseum Zürich, 1960 — two
   bands of Akzidenz-Grotesk and a stepped column of small type, all locked to one modular grid.
2. **Objectivity over persuasion.** The designer presents information; they do not cajole. Type
   is neutral, photography is documentary, illustration is avoided. *Example:* Müller-Brockmann,
   *schützt das Kind!* poster for the Automobil Club der Schweiz, 1953 — a photograph of a child
   and a car, a single line of type, no rhetoric.
3. **Asymmetric balance and active white space.** Composition is asymmetric and dynamic, and
   empty space is a structural element, not leftover. *Example:* Emil Ruder's spreads in
   *Typographie*, 1967, where unequal columns and large voids carry as much meaning as the type.
4. **One sans-serif, flush-left, ragged-right.** A single grotesque family in a few weights and
   sizes, set unjustified so word spacing stays even. *Example:* the layouts of *Neue Grafik*,
   from issue 1, 1958, set throughout in Akzidenz-Grotesk.
5. **Form from elementary means.** Posters are built from the simplest possible elements — a
   dot, a line, a letter, a contrast of black and white — explored systematically. *Example:*
   Armin Hofmann, *Giselle* poster for the Basel open-air theatre, 1959 — a blurred photograph
   of a dancer against crisp, enormous sans-serif letters.

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** Akzidenz-Grotesk in the 1950s; Helvetica and Univers from the early 1960s. Lower case
  preferred; large display sizes against small text; strong weight contrast within one family.
  Flush-left, ragged-right. Text and headlines often rotated 90°.
- **Grid:** modular, mathematically proportioned; columns and baseline both governed;
  layouts visibly derived from the grid's fields.
- **Colour:** restrained — black, white and one or two flat primaries (very often red).
- **Imagery:** objective black-and-white photography, frequently cropped hard, enlarged until it
  becomes texture, or reduced to a single sign; geometric abstraction in concert posters.
- **Composition:** asymmetric, dynamic, high contrast of scale; generous white space;
  information hierarchy carried by size, weight and position alone, never by ornament.

### Lineage

From: the New Typography (`new-typography`), the Bauhaus (`bauhaus`), De Stijl (`de-stijl`) and
Constructivism (`constructivism`) via concrete art; the pre-war Swiss poster (`plakatstil`) as
the thing it reacted against. Into: corporate identity in the US (`corporate-identity`,
`american-modernism` — Unimark, IBM, Vignelli's New York subway), the Ulm School
(`ulm-school`), Dutch modernism (`dutch-modernism`), Brazilian and Argentine concretism
(`brazilian-concretism`, `argentine-modernism` — Bill's 1951 São Paulo retrospective was the
spark), Japanese modernism (`japanese-modernism`), and then the backlash: Wolfgang Weingart's
Swiss "New Wave" from Basel in the late 1960s (`new-wave`), the 1990s–2010s revival
(`new-swiss-revival`) and the grid logic of screen design systems (`design-systems`).

### Quotes

- "Die Belange der Grafik werden auf ihren werbenden und ästhetischen Wert hin untersucht."
  ("The concerns of graphic design are examined for their advertising and aesthetic value.") —
  the editors (Lohse, Müller-Brockmann, Neuburg, Vivarelli), introduction to *Neue Grafik* no. 1,
  1958.

### Remix today

The Swiss lesson isn't Helvetica; it's *deciding the structure before the content arrives and
then trusting it*. In 2026 that is exactly the problem of responsive layout, design tokens and
component systems: a grid that holds across a phone, a billboard and a generated variant. Take
the discipline (one family, a strict scale, a modular grid, objective imagery) and pair it with
something the Swiss rationed — motion, data, variable fonts — and the style stops being a pastiche
and becomes a tool.

### Ernst Keller {#ernst-keller}

Swiss, 1891–1968. Graphic designer, typographer, heraldist, sculptor and teacher.

**Context.** Trained as a lithographer in Aarau from 1906, then from 1911 to 1914 at the
Werkstatt für deutsche Wortkunst in Leipzig, where he absorbed German lettering culture. Alfred
Altherr called him to the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich in 1918, and from 1920 he taught applied
graphics there until his retirement in 1956. He is the father figure of the Swiss school rather
than its stylist: his own work stayed partly pictorial, but his students included Josef
Müller-Brockmann and many of the practitioners who defined the 1950s.
Müller-Brockmann succeeded him as professor in 1957.

**Principles.**
1. **The problem determines the form.** Keller taught that a solution should be derived from
   the content and the brief rather than from a house style. *Example:* his graphic design
   course at the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich, established from 1918, which made analysis the
   first step of every exercise.
2. **Lettering as architecture.** Type is treated as a constructed, monumental object.
   *Example:* the lettering and heraldic relief on the Schweizerische Lebensversicherungs- und
   Rentenanstalt building in Zürich, 1938, and the lettering wall for the Swiss National
   Exhibition (Landi), 1939.
3. **Simple geometry, intense colour.** Bold simplified shapes and strong flat colour carry the
   message. *Example:* his typographic posters of the 1920s and 1930s, described by the
   Kunstgewerbemuseum's 1976 retrospective as a "commitment to modernism".

**Key works.** Graphic design class, Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich, from 1918; heraldic designs for
the City of Zürich, from 1925; Rentenanstalt lettering relief, 1938; Landesausstellung
lettering wall, 1939.

**Visual vocabulary.** Constructed capitals, flat colour, simplified heraldic and geometric
motifs; a bridge between Art Nouveau lettering craft and the objective style.

**Lineage.** From the German lettering and *Wortkunst* tradition; into the entire Zürich school
through his students.

**Remix today.** Keller's real invention was a *curriculum*. If you lead a design team, the most
durable thing you can make is not a style but a way of starting every brief — and he is the
case study for that.

### Max Bill {#max-bill}

Swiss, 1908–1994. Architect, painter, sculptor, graphic, product and typeface designer, educator.

**Context.** Trained as a silversmith (1924–27), then studied at the Bauhaus in Dessau
(1927–29) under Kandinsky, Klee and Schlemmer before settling in Zürich. He became the leading
theorist of *konkrete Kunst* — art made from mathematical and geometric relationships with no
reference to nature — and the bridge between Bauhaus pedagogy and post-war Swiss design. He was
a professor at the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich from 1944, organised the touring exhibition *Die
gute Form* in 1949, co-founded the Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm in 1953 with Inge
Aicher-Scholl and Otl Aicher, designed its building, and was its first rector until a split with
the faculty in 1956–57.

**Principles.**
1. **Concrete form.** Design and art alike should be built from pure, measurable relationships
   — proportion, series, progression — not from imitation. *Example:* his poster
   *negerkunst: prähistorische felsbilder südafrikas*, 1931, all lower case, with a rigorous
   geometric structure.
2. **Good form ("die gute Form").** Everyday objects should be judged on whether they unite use,
   material and beauty, and good objects should be shown to the public as an argument. *Example:*
   the *Die gute Form* exhibition for the Swiss Werkbund, 1949.
3. **The multifunctional object.** One simple form should serve many uses. *Example:* the Ulmer
   Hocker (Ulm stool) with Hans Gugelot, 1954 — stool, shelf, lectern, side table and carrying
   tray in three boards.

**Key works.** *negerkunst* poster, 1931; *USA baut* exhibition poster, 1945; *Die gute Form*
exhibition, 1949; HfG Ulm building, 1953–55; Ulmer Hocker, 1954; dials for Junghans wall and
kitchen clocks (1950s) and later wristwatches.

**Visual vocabulary.** Lower-case sans-serif, mathematical spacing, single geometric figures,
flat primaries; in objects, minimal volumes and visible construction.

**Lineage.** From the Bauhaus (`bauhaus`) and De Stijl (`de-stijl`); into Swiss Style, Ulm,
Brazilian concretism (`brazilian-concretism`, via his 1951 São Paulo retrospective) and
Argentine concrete art.

**Remix today.** Bill shows that a rule set can be expressive. A generative identity or a
parametric product line is a concrete artwork: decide the relationships, let the forms follow.

### Josef Müller-Brockmann {#josef-muller-brockmann}

Swiss, 1914–1996. Graphic designer, author, educator.

**Context.** Born in Rapperswil, trained under Keller's circle in Zürich, opened his studio in
1936 (graphics, exhibitions, photography) and joined the Swiss Werkbund in 1937. His early work
was illustrative; around 1950 he moved to pure constructive form. He designed the Tonhalle
concert posters from 1950, became professor at the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich in 1957
(succeeding Keller, until 1960), co-founded *Neue Grafik* in 1958, taught at Ulm from 1963, and
in 1967 became IBM's European design consultant and founded Müller-Brockmann & Co.

**Principles.**
1. **The grid as a mental attitude.** The grid isn't decoration but a way of thinking — the
   will to order, clarify and penetrate to essentials. *Example:* *Grid Systems in Graphic
   Design*, 1981, which codified the modular grids he had been using for thirty years.
2. **Music made visible.** Abstract geometric structures (circles, arcs, intervals, rhythms) can
   express the content of music without illustrating it. *Example:* the Tonhalle "Musica Viva"
   posters, from 1950 into the 1970s, built from concentric rings and stepped bars.
3. **Public information as civic duty.** The poster is a public service, so clarity is an
   ethical demand. *Example:* *weniger Lärm* (less noise), 1960 — a photograph of a man holding
   his head, wrapped in sound-wave arcs, with one line of type.

**Key works.** Tonhalle Zürich concert posters, from 1950; *schützt das Kind!*, 1953; *Der Film*,
1960; *weniger Lärm*, 1960; *Neue Grafik* (co-editor), 1958–65; *The Graphic Artist and his Design
Problems*, 1961; *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, 1981.

**Visual vocabulary.** Akzidenz-Grotesk, often lower case; strict modular grids; black, white and
one colour; objective photography; text set in narrow stepped columns.

**Lineage.** From Keller, Bill, Lohse and the Constructivists; into IBM, Unimark and every
grid-system textbook since.

**Quotes.** "The grid system is an aid, not a guarantee." — *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*,
1981, preface.

**Remix today.** Read *Grid Systems* as a book about systems design, not pages. The same question
— what is the smallest unit, and how do all the larger decisions derive from it — is the
question behind spacing scales and layout tokens.

### Armin Hofmann {#armin-hofmann}

Swiss, 1920–2020. Graphic designer, educator.

**Context.** Born in Winterthur, trained as a lithographer (1938–42), began teaching at the
Allgemeine Gewerbeschule Basel in 1947, where with Emil Ruder he built the Basel school's
graphic design programme, and led its graphics class from 1967. From around 1960 he was a regular
visiting professor at Yale, through which the Basel method entered American education. He
resigned from Yale in 1991 in protest at the programme's postmodern turn. He made more than 100
posters, mostly for Basel cultural institutions.

**Principles.**
1. **Elementary form exercises.** Teach design through the systematic exploration of the most
   basic elements — dot, line, plane, letter — before any real brief. *Example:* *Graphic
   Design Manual: Principles and Practice*, 1965.
2. **Contrast as the engine.** Meaning comes from opposition: large and small, sharp and soft,
   light and dark, static and dynamic. *Example:* *Giselle*, 1959 — a motion-blurred dancer
   against razor-sharp letters.
3. **Reduction to the sign.** A poster should collapse to one memorable form. *Example:* the
   Herman Miller Collection poster, 1962, and *Wilhelm Tell* for the Basel open-air theatre,
   1963.

**Key works.** *Giselle*, 1959; Herman Miller Collection, 1962; *Wilhelm Tell*, 1963; posters for
the Stadttheater, Kunsthalle and Gewerbemuseum Basel, 1950s–80s; *Graphic Design Manual*, 1965.

**Visual vocabulary.** Black and white above all; huge sans-serif letters cropped by the edge;
photographic textures; one shape, one contrast.

**Lineage.** From the Bauhaus Vorkurs tradition; into Yale's graphic design programme and,
through his students, American corporate modernism; also the springboard for Weingart's
reaction at Basel.

**Remix today.** Hofmann's exercises are the best warm-up there is for icon, logo and motion
work: limit yourself to one element and one contrast, and push the variations until the form
says something.

### Emil Ruder {#emil-ruder}

Swiss, 1914–1970. Typographer, educator, writer.

**Context.** Born in Zürich, trained as a typesetter (1929–33), studied in Paris (1938–39), and
taught typography at the Allgemeine Gewerbeschule Basel from 1942. In the merged trade journal
*Typographische Monatsblätter (TM)* from 1952, and in four articles of 1957–59 ("The Plane",
"The Line", "The Word", "Rhythm"), he argued the case for modernism to the printing trade itself.
He co-founded the International Center for the Typographic Arts in New York in 1962 and was an
early champion of Univers, whose many coordinated weights suited his teaching.

**Principles.**
1. **Typography serves communication first.** Legibility and order before expression; form is
   earned by function. *Example:* the opening of *Typographie*, 1967.
2. **Counter-form is form.** The white of the page, the space inside and between letters, is
   designed as deliberately as the black. *Example:* the exercises in *Typographie*, 1967,
   where the same text is set repeatedly to explore rhythm and void.
3. **Rhythm and the word.** A line of type has rhythm like music, built from the shapes and
   intervals of letters, and a whole page can be composed from that rhythm alone. *Example:*
   his *TM* series "Wesentliches", 1957–59.

**Key works.** *TM* articles and covers, from 1946; "Wesentliches" series, 1957–59;
*Typographie: Ein Gestaltungslehrbuch*, 1967 (German, English, French).

**Visual vocabulary.** Univers in many weights; asymmetric spreads; deep white space; text treated
as texture and rhythm; precise optical spacing.

**Lineage.** From the New Typography and Japanese aesthetics (he admired the role of emptiness in
Japanese art); into the Basel school, Weingart (his student and successor at Basel) and the
typographic curriculum of most design schools.

**Quotes.** "Typography has one plain duty before it and that is to convey information in
writing." — *Typographie*, 1967. In *TM* (November 1952) he wrote that part of graphic design's
function was to promote "the good and the beautiful in word and image and to open the way to the
arts".

**Remix today.** Ruder is the patron saint of interface typography: hierarchy from one family,
rhythm from spacing, and white space as a working material. Read him before tuning a type scale.

### Karl Gerstner {#karl-gerstner}

Swiss, 1930–2017. Designer, typographer, advertising executive, artist, author.

**Context.** Apprenticed as a typographer in Fritz Bühler's Basel studio (1944–48), freelanced for
the chemical company Geigy from 1949, and formed the agency Gerstner + Kutter with the writer
Markus Kutter (dated 1958 by some sources, 1959 by others), which became GGK when Paul Gredinger
joined in 1962 — one of the first agencies to bring Swiss rigour to mass advertising. In parallel
he was a serious concrete artist. MoMA gave him a solo show, *designing programs/programming
designs*, in 1973.

**Principles.**
1. **Programmes, not solutions.** Instead of designing one answer, design the rules that generate
   all the valid answers. *Example:* *Designing Programmes*, 1964, with its morphological
   "box" of typographic parameters.
2. **Integral typography.** Type and content are a single unit: the form of the word expresses
   its meaning. *Example:* his typography for Markus Kutter's novel *Schiff nach Europa*, 1957,
   where layout changes with the voice of the text.
3. **Systematic type families.** A type family can be designed as a mathematical system of
   variants. *Example:* the Gerstner Programm typeface, 1964–67, for Berthold's Diatype
   filmsetting.

**Key works.** *kalte Kunst?*, 1957; *Schiff nach Europa* (design), 1957; *die neue Graphik*
(with Kutter), 1959; *Designing Programmes*, 1964; Gerstner Programm, 1964–67; *Kompendium für
Alphabeten* (*Compendium for Literates*), 1972; *The Forms of Color*, 1986.

**Visual vocabulary.** Grids and parametric variation; logotypes generated from a typographic
rule; colour studies; witty, idea-led advertising inside a strict system.

**Lineage.** From concrete art and Swiss Style; directly ancestral to parametric and generative
design, variable fonts and design-system thinking (`design-systems`, `generative-ai-design`).

**Quotes.** "Instead of solutions for problems, programmes for solutions." — *Designing
Programmes*, 1964.

**Remix today.** Gerstner is the most contemporary figure in this slice. Prompting a model,
building a component library and writing a variable font are all "designing programmes". His
morphological box is a usable tool for exploring a brief exhaustively before choosing.

### Adrian Frutiger {#adrian-frutiger}

Swiss, 1928–2015. Type designer.

**Context.** Apprenticed as a compositor, studied at the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich (1949–51) under
Walter Käch and Alfred Willimann, then moved to Paris to work for Deberny & Peignot under Charles
Peignot. His career spans hot metal, phototypesetting and digital type — and he designed for
each technology rather than against it.

**Principles.**
1. **The type family as a system.** A family should be designed all at once as a coordinated set
   of weights and widths, named by a numbering grid rather than adjectives. *Example:* Univers,
   1957, with 21 styles numbered from 39 to 83 on a two-digit system (weight × width).
2. **Legibility at a distance and at speed.** Letters for wayfinding must stay open and
   distinct when seen at an angle and in passing. *Example:* the signage for Charles de Gaulle
   Airport (commissioned 1970), released for print as Frutiger in 1976.
3. **Design for the machine.** Work with the constraints of the reproduction technology.
   *Example:* OCR-B, 1968, a machine-readable typeface that stays humane to human readers.

**Key works.** Président, 1954; Méridien, 1955 (released 1957 in some sources); Univers, 1957;
Apollo, 1964 (Monotype's first typeface for phototypesetting); Serifa, 1967; OCR-B, 1968;
Frutiger, 1976; Avenir, 1988.

**Visual vocabulary.** Neo-grotesque and humanist sans-serifs with open apertures, careful optical
weight; large coordinated families.

**Lineage.** From the Zürich lettering tradition; into almost every wayfinding system and
corporate family since, and the humanist sans of digital interfaces.

**Remix today.** Univers' numbering grid is the ancestor of the variable-font axis. When you
design a type scale or a token set, design the whole matrix at once, as Frutiger did.

### Max Miedinger {#max-miedinger}

Swiss, 1910–1980. Typeface designer.

**Context.** Trained as a typesetter in Zürich (1926–30), took evening classes at the
Kunstgewerbeschule, then worked as a sales representative for the Haas Type Foundry in
Münchenstein near Basel before going freelance. In 1956 Eduard Hoffmann, Haas's director,
commissioned him to draw a new grotesque to win back the Swiss market from Berthold's
Akzidenz-Grotesk. Neue Haas Grotesk was shown at the Graphic 57 exhibition in Lausanne in June
1957; Stempel adapted it for Linotype machine setting and renamed it Helvetica in 1960.

**Principles.**
1. **Refine, don't invent.** Start from an existing, trusted model and remove its quirks.
   *Example:* Neue Haas Grotesk, 1957, which took Akzidenz-Grotesk, Schelter-Grotesk and Haas's
   own Normal-Grotesk as reference points.
2. **Uniformity as voice.** Tight spacing, horizontal terminals and a large x-height produce a
   dense, even, impersonal texture. *Example:* Helvetica's set texture as launched in 1960.

**Key works.** Pro Arte, 1954; Neue Haas Grotesk, 1957; Helvetica (renamed and extended), 1960.

**Visual vocabulary.** Neo-grotesque sans-serif, horizontal stroke endings, tight spacing.

**Lineage.** From the 19th-century grotesques; into corporate identity worldwide, the New York
subway, and the default sans of personal computing.

**Remix today.** Helvetica's success is a lesson in *defaults*: the type everyone reaches for is
a political choice. Choose it on purpose or not at all.

### Siegfried Odermatt {#siegfried-odermatt}

Swiss, 1926–2017. Graphic designer.

**Context.** Largely self-taught; worked in industrial graphics studios, with Hans Falk and as
assistant to the photographer Werner Bischof in the 1940s, and set up on his own in Zürich in
1950. He hired Rosmarie Tissi in 1958, and in 1968 they became equal partners as Odermatt &
Tissi. He and Tissi stood slightly apart from the Zürich orthodoxy: their work used the Swiss
tools with wit, play and surprise, especially in advertising for industrial clients.

**Principles.**
1. **Constructive form with a twist.** Keep the grid and the sans-serif, but break the expected
   order to make an image. *Example:* *Meister der Plakatkunst* poster for the
   Kunstgewerbemuseum Zürich, 1959, which made his name as a renewer of constructive graphics.
2. **Type as the image.** Letters overlap, tilt and scale until the word is the illustration.
   *Example:* his corporate advertising of the 1960s for industrial clients.

**Key works.** *Meister der Plakatkunst*, 1959; Odermatt & Tissi studio, from 1968; Kieler Woche
identity competition win (dated 1992 by the German Wikipedia and 1994 in the festival's own
designer list).

**Visual vocabulary.** Sans-serif type pushed to overlap and rotate; bold flat colour; photographs
used as graphic shapes.

**Lineage.** From the Zürich school; a precursor of the looser, more playful Swiss work that fed
the 1970s–80s.

**Remix today.** Odermatt proves that rigour and humour aren't opposites: keep the system, and
put the joke in the one rule you break.

### Rosmarie Tissi {#rosmarie-tissi}

Swiss, born 1937. Graphic designer, typeface designer.

**Context.** Studied at the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich from 1953, joined Siegfried Odermatt in 1958
and became his equal partner in 1968. One of very few women recognised in the Swiss canon, she
developed a freer, more pictorial constructive style: shapes collide, letters become figures,
colour is bright and flat. Gold medal at the Warsaw International Poster Biennial in 1986;
second prize in the 1989 Swiss banknote competition; Swiss Grand Award for Design in 2018.

**Principles.**
1. **Playful geometry.** Geometric forms are arranged for tension and humour rather than
   equilibrium. *Example:* her posters for the typesetting company Englersatz and for cultural
   clients from the 1980s.
2. **Letterforms as display objects.** A typeface can be a graphic voice in its own right.
   *Example:* Sinaloa, a display typeface for Letraset, 1974.

**Key works.** Odermatt & Tissi studio work, from 1968; Sinaloa, 1974; Swiss banknote competition
design, 1989.

**Visual vocabulary.** Bright flat colour, overlapping geometric and typographic elements,
deliberately "unstable" compositions on an underlying order.

**Lineage.** From Swiss Style; towards the freer post-Swiss work of the 1980s and today's
revivalists.

**Remix today.** Tissi is the model for anyone who wants Swiss clarity without Swiss austerity:
keep the underlying structure and let the shapes misbehave on top of it.

## Ulm School {#ulm-school}

The Hochschule für Gestaltung (HfG) Ulm, 1953–1968.

### Context

The HfG grew out of the Geschwister-Scholl-Stiftung, set up by Inge Scholl in memory of her
siblings Hans and Sophie, executed in 1943 as members of the White Rose resistance. With Otl
Aicher she planned a school that would contribute to the moral and cultural reconstruction of
Germany; John McCloy, the US High Commissioner, gave a million marks in 1952. Max Bill became
first rector (in April 1953; one German source has him as rector from 1952), teaching began on 3
August 1953 in rooms of the Ulm adult education centre, with Josef Albers, Johannes Itten and
Walter Peterhans visiting, and the school moved into Bill's own building on the Kuhberg in
1955.

Ulm began as a "new Bauhaus" and quickly became something else. A conflict between Bill (who
saw design as continuous with art) and a younger faculty led by Tomás Maldonado, Otl Aicher and
Hans Gugelot ended with Bill's departure in 1956–57. The "Ulm Model" that followed integrated
semiotics, ergonomics, sociology, systems theory, operational research and production
engineering into design teaching. Departments: Product Design, Visual Communication,
Industrialised Building, Information (until 1964) and Film (independent from 1962).

Ulm worked through *Entwicklungsgruppen* (development groups) contracted by industry, which
is how it produced Braun's product language and Lufthansa's identity. Internal conflict, state
funding cuts and political pressure closed it in 1968.

### Principles

1. **Design as science, not art.** Design decisions are justified by analysis — of use,
   production, perception and sign — not by taste. *Example:* the Lufthansa corporate identity
   by Otl Aicher's development group E5, 1962, built from a written analysis of the airline's
   communication needs.
2. **The system over the object.** Design families and modular systems rather than single
   pieces. *Example:* Hans Gugelot's M125 modular storage system for Bofinger, developed from
   1950.
3. **Semiotics in practice.** Treat every graphic and product element as a sign with syntactic,
   semantic and pragmatic dimensions. *Example:* Maldonado and Gui Bonsiepe's coding system for
   Olivetti and La Rinascente, 1964–67.
4. **Responsibility.** Design is a social and political act with consequences, not
   commercial styling. *Example:* the school's own founding mission as a democratic
   institution after Nazism, 1953.

### Key works

- Max Bill — HfG building, Ulm, 1953–55; Ulmer Hocker (with Gugelot), 1954.
- Hans Gugelot & Dieter Rams — Braun SK 4 Phonosuper, 1956.
- Otl Aicher & development group E5 — Lufthansa identity, 1962.
- Hans Gugelot & Reinhold Häcker — Kodak Carousel-S slide projector, 1962/63.
- *ulm* journal of the HfG, 1958–68.
- Otl Aicher — visual identity of the 1972 Munich Olympics (after Ulm, staffed with Ulm alumni).

### Visual vocabulary

- **Graphics:** sans-serif (Akzidenz-Grotesk, later Univers and Helvetica), strict grids, a
  limited corporate palette, the systematic design manual.
- **Products:** light grey and white bodies, black controls, flush surfaces, rounded corners
  derived from production, honest materials, modular sizes.
- **Method:** briefs, analyses, diagrams; the design manual as an output equal to the product.

### Lineage

From the Bauhaus (`bauhaus`) and Swiss Style (`swiss-style`, via Bill); into functionalist product
design (`functionalist-product-design`), German corporate identity, Aicher's wayfinding and
pictogram systems, the US DOT pictograms (1974), and the methodological, research-led strand of
design education worldwide. Maldonado carried it to Milan; Ulm graduates such as Gui Bonsiepe
carried it to Latin America (`argentine-modernism`).

### Remix today

Ulm is the origin of "design thinking" before it became a slogan: the idea that design produces
*systems with documentation*, justified by research. Every design-systems team is an Ulm
development group. What's worth taking back from them is the part we dropped — the insistence that
the designer is socially responsible for what the system does.

### Otl Aicher {#otl-aicher}

German, 1922–1991. Graphic designer, typographer, educator.

**Context.** Born in Ulm; refused to join the Hitler Youth, was arrested in 1937, deserted in 1945
and hid with the Scholl family. Studied sculpture briefly in Munich, opened a studio in Ulm in
1947, married Inge Scholl in 1952 and co-founded the HfG in 1953. He led Ulm's visual
communication work, designed the Lufthansa identity (1962), was appointed lead designer of the
1972 Munich Olympics in 1966, and later created Rotis (1988) at his studio in the hamlet of
Rotis near Leutkirch. He died after an accident in 1991.

**Principles.**
1. **The identity as a complete system.** One manual governs type, colour, grid, photography
   and signs across every touchpoint. *Example:* Lufthansa, 1962 — the crane in a circle,
   Helvetica, blue and yellow, specified in a design manual.
2. **Pictograms on a grid.** Signs built from a strict geometric grid — orthogonal and 45°
   lines only — so a whole family reads as one language. *Example:* the Munich 1972 sports and
   service pictograms.
3. **Colour as mood and politics.** A deliberately un-German palette to recast a nation's image.
   *Example:* Munich 1972's light blue, green, silver, white and orange — no red, black or
   gold — to distance the "cheerful Games" from the 1936 Berlin Olympics.

**Key works.** HfG Ulm posters and communications, 1950s; Braun graphic identity, 1950s;
Lufthansa identity, 1962; Munich 1972 Olympics identity, emblem ("Bright Sun") and pictograms,
1966–72; Rotis, 1988; *Die Welt als Entwurf* (*The World as Design*), 1991.

**Visual vocabulary.** Univers and Helvetica; grids; flat, bright, cool colour; geometric
pictograms; photography printed in tonal posterised colour (the Munich sport posters).

**Lineage.** From Ulm and Swiss Style; into the DOT pictograms (1974), airport and transit
wayfinding everywhere, and Olympic identity programmes since.

**Remix today.** Munich 1972 is still the best answer to "how do you make a system feel joyful?"
— fix the geometry and let the colour do the emotional work. Its pictogram grid is the template
for any icon set.

### Hans Gugelot {#hans-gugelot}

Dutch-German (born in Makassar, Dutch East Indies), 1920–1965. Architect, industrial designer.

**Context.** Grew up partly in Switzerland, qualified as an architect at ETH Zürich in 1946,
was hired by Max Bill in 1948, started his own office in 1950 and began the M125 storage system.
He taught product design at Ulm from 1954, where he met Erwin Braun the same year, and led the
development group that gave Braun its new product language. He died of a heart attack in 1965.

**Principles.**
1. **Modular systems.** Products built from a standard module so they can grow and combine.
   *Example:* the M125 storage system for Bofinger (developed from 1950, produced from 1956),
   based on a 125 mm module.
2. **The product as a quiet box.** Strip the appliance of furniture pretensions and expose its
   function in a clean volume. *Example:* the Braun SK 4 radio-phonograph with Dieter Rams, 1956.
3. **Ergonomics and production as form-givers.** *Example:* the Kodak Carousel-S slide
   projector with Reinhold Häcker (1962 according to Gugelot's biography, 1963 in MoMA's record).

**Key works.** Ulmer Hocker (with Bill), 1954; M125, from 1950; Braun SK 4, 1956; Kodak
Carousel-S, 1962/63.

**Visual vocabulary.** White and light-grey sheet metal, pale wood, clear acrylic lids, grid of
perforations, rectilinear volumes.

**Lineage.** From Bill and the Bauhaus; into Rams and the whole functionalist product tradition.

**Remix today.** Gugelot's module is the physical version of the 4px grid: commit to a base unit
and every combination stays coherent.

### Tomás Maldonado {#tomas-maldonado}

Argentine, 1922–2018. Painter, designer, theorist, educator.

**Context.** A founder of the Asociación Arte Concreto-Invención in Buenos Aires in 1945 and
editor of *Nueva Visión* from 1951, he met Max Bill and went to Ulm, where he taught from 1954
(until 1966 or 1967, by different sources) and served as rector. He led the move away from
Bill's art-centred model to a scientific, semiotic one. From 1967 he worked in Milan, then
Princeton (1967–70) and the University of Bologna.

**Principles.**
1. **Design as rational method.** Design should be grounded in science, semiotics and
   production, not intuition. *Example:* the Ulm curriculum reforms he led after 1957.
2. **Coding systems.** Graphic languages as systems of signs with explicit rules. *Example:* the
   design programme coding system for Olivetti and La Rinascente with Gui Bonsiepe, 1964–67.
3. **Design as hope and critique.** The designer must think about environment and society.
   *Example:* *La speranza progettuale*, 1970 (English: *Design, Nature, and Revolution*).

**Key works.** Arte Concreto-Invención, 1945; *Nueva Visión*, from 1951; *Max Bill*, 1955;
Olivetti/Rinascente coding, 1964–67; *La speranza progettuale*, 1970.

**Visual vocabulary.** In his painting, concrete geometry; in his design, systems, diagrams and
codes rather than a recognisable look.

**Lineage.** From Argentine concrete art (`argentine-modernism`) and Bill; into the Ulm Model,
Italian design theory and Latin American design education.

**Remix today.** Maldonado is the reminder that systems design is a form of argument. A design
system without a written rationale is just a parts list.

## Functionalist Product Design {#functionalist-product-design}

Braun and its circle, c. 1954–1995 (with Vitsœ continuing).

### Context

In 1954–55 Erwin and Artur Braun, newly in charge of their father's Frankfurt electrical
company, decided to change the look of Braun's products and created an in-house design
department. They brought in Ulm (Hans Gugelot, and Otl Aicher for graphics), Wilhelm Wagenfeld
and a young architect hired in 1955, Dieter Rams, who became head of design in 1961 and stayed
until 1997. The Braun products shown at the 1955 Düsseldorf radio fair, and the SK 4 of 1956,
replaced the polished wooden radio cabinet with a rational appliance. In 1959 Rams began working
with Niels Vitsœ and Otto Zapf, whose company produced his furniture systems.

The enabling technologies were the transistor, injection-moulded plastics, sheet-metal
fabrication and clear acrylic. The cultural background was West Germany's *Wirtschaftswunder*
and a deliberate desire for a modest, democratic material culture.

### Principles

1. **Form follows use.** An appliance is a tool; its form comes from what the user does with
   it. *Example:* Braun T3 pocket radio, 1958 — a circular dial and a grid of round holes, no
   more.
2. **Order through hierarchy.** Controls are ordered by importance; colour is reserved for
   signals. *Example:* the ET 66 calculator by Dietrich Lubs for Braun, 1987 — grey body, black
   keys, one yellow "equals" key.
3. **Systems, not products.** Components that add up, last and can be extended. *Example:* the
   606 Universal Shelving System for Vitsœ, 1960, still in production with minor changes.
4. **Restraint.** The product steps back so the user and their environment come forward.
   *Example:* the SK 4, 1956 — white metal, pale wood ends and a clear lid.

### Key works

- Gugelot & Rams — Braun SK 4, 1956.
- Rams — Braun T3 pocket radio, 1958; TP 1 radio/phono, 1959; 606 Universal Shelving System
  (Vitsœ), 1960; RT 20 tischsuper radio, 1961; 620 Chair Programme (Vitsœ), 1962; T 1000 world
  receiver, 1963; T 2 cylindric lighter, 1968.
- Rams & Jürgen Greubel — MPZ 21 citrus press, 1972.
- Dietrich Lubs (Braun) — ET 66 calculator, 1987.

### Visual vocabulary

White, light grey and black bodies; colour only for function (a green or yellow switch); grids
of perforations; circles and rectangles; small, precise sans-serif labels (Akzidenz-Grotesk or
Helvetica); radiused corners at production-friendly values; clear acrylic.

### Lineage

From the Bauhaus (`bauhaus`) and Ulm (`ulm-school`); into Apple's industrial design (Jony Ive's
explicit homage; the iOS calculator after the ET 66), Muji, Jasper Morrison's "super normal",
Teenage Engineering, and flat interface design (`flat-design`).

### Remix today

The Braun lesson for interface work: colour is a signal, not a decoration; one accent is enough
when everything else is neutral. And Rams' tenth principle is a working test for any feature
review — what can be taken away?

### Dieter Rams {#dieter-rams}

German, born 1932. Industrial designer.

**Context.** Studied architecture and interior design in Wiesbaden (graduated 1953), worked for
the architect Otto Apel, and joined Braun in 1955 as an architect and interior designer. He
became head of design in 1961 and retired from Braun in 1997, continuing with Vitsœ. In the late
1970s, troubled by what he called "an impenetrable confusion of forms, colours and noises" and
aware that he contributed to it, he asked himself whether his design was good design. His
answer was the ten principles. His motto is *Weniger, aber besser* — "Less, but better" (also
the title of his 1995 book).

**The ten principles of good design** (in Vitsœ's wording):

1. **Good design is innovative.** The possibilities for innovation are not exhausted;
   technological development keeps offering new opportunities, but innovative design always
   develops in tandem with innovative technology and is never an end in itself. *Vitsœ's
   example:* TP 1 radio/phono combination, 1959.
2. **Good design makes a product useful.** A product is bought to be used and has to satisfy
   functional, psychological and aesthetic criteria; good design emphasises usefulness and
   disregards anything that could detract from it. *Example:* MPZ 21 citrus press, 1972.
3. **Good design is aesthetic.** The aesthetic quality of a product is integral to its
   usefulness, because the things we use every day affect our wellbeing; only well-executed
   objects can be beautiful. *Example:* RT 20 radio, 1961.
4. **Good design makes a product understandable.** It clarifies the product's structure;
   better, it makes the product talk; at best, it is self-explanatory. *Example:* T 1000 world
   receiver, 1963.
5. **Good design is unobtrusive.** Products are like tools — neither decorative objects nor
   works of art — so their design should be neutral and restrained, leaving room for the user's
   self-expression. *Example:* T 2 cylindric lighter, 1968.
6. **Good design is honest.** It does not make a product appear more innovative, powerful or
   valuable than it is, and does not manipulate with promises that cannot be kept. *Example:*
   L 450 loudspeaker, TG 60 tape recorder and TS 45 control unit, 1962–64.
7. **Good design is long-lasting.** It avoids being fashionable and so never appears
   antiquated; it lasts many years, even in a throwaway society. *Example:* 620 Chair
   Programme, 1962.
8. **Good design is thorough down to the last detail.** Nothing is arbitrary or left to chance;
   care and accuracy show respect towards the user. *Example:* ET 66 calculator (Dietrich Lubs),
   1987.
9. **Good design is environmentally friendly.** It conserves resources and minimises physical
   and visual pollution throughout the product's life. *Example:* 606 Universal Shelving
   System, 1960.
10. **Good design is as little design as possible.** Less, but better — it concentrates on the
    essential aspects and doesn't burden products with non-essentials. Back to purity, back to
    simplicity.

**Key works.** SK 4 (with Gugelot), 1956; T3, 1958; TP 1, 1959; 606 Universal Shelving System,
1960; RT 20, 1961; 620 Chair Programme, 1962; T 1000, 1963; T 2 lighter, 1968; ten principles,
late 1970s; *Less but Better*, 1995.

**Visual vocabulary.** Neutral bodies, colour-coded controls, perforation grids, flush surfaces,
small exact sans-serif labels, modular dimensions.

**Lineage.** From Ulm and Gugelot; into Apple, Muji and the flat/minimal interface tradition.

**Quotes.** "Good design is as little design as possible." and "Less, but better" — Rams' tenth
principle, as published by Vitsœ.

**Remix today.** Use the ten principles as a review rubric, not a poster. Principles 4
("understandable") and 6 ("honest") are directly about interface design and dark patterns;
principle 9 has become the most urgent of the ten.

## Dutch Modernism {#dutch-modernism}

Amsterdam and the Netherlands, c. 1955–1985.

### Context

The Netherlands had its own modernist past — De Stijl and Piet Zwart's New Typography — and a
strong tradition of enlightened public clients: the PTT (post and telecom), the Stedelijk
Museum, Schiphol, the national railways. After the war Willem Sandberg, director of the
Stedelijk (1945–62), designed the museum's catalogues himself in a free, torn-paper modernism.
His successor's era brought in Wim Crouwel, who designed the Stedelijk's posters and catalogues
from 1964.

In 1963 Crouwel, Benno Wissing, the industrial designer Friso Kramer and the brothers Dick and
Paul Schwarz founded the Associatie voor Total Design NV in Amsterdam, the first large
multidisciplinary design consultancy in the country, modelled partly on Swiss studios and on
American firms like Unimark. Ben Bos joined at the start. Total Design put systematic
identities into Dutch public life — Schiphol, Randstad, Rabobank, the PTT.

The enabling technology was the computer and early phototypesetting. Crouwel took the cathode-ray
tube seriously as a medium a generation before the screen took over, which is why his work looks
so contemporary.

### Principles

1. **The grid made visible.** The grid isn't hidden; it's printed on the poster as part of the
   image. *Example:* Crouwel, *Vormgevers* exhibition poster for the Stedelijk, 1968, where the
   square grid and the type built on it are both shown.
2. **Type for the machine.** Letterforms drawn to the limits of a new technology rather than
   against them. *Example:* Crouwel, New Alphabet, 1967 — horizontal and vertical strokes only,
   for cathode-ray-tube display.
3. **Total identity.** One office handles graphics, interiors, signage and products for a client,
   so the whole environment speaks with one voice. *Example:* Benno Wissing's wayfinding for
   Amsterdam Airport Schiphol, 1967 — clear type and rigorous colour coding.
4. **Playful rigour.** Systematic does not mean grim: geometry can be bright, witty and
   experimental. *Example:* Crouwel's numeral stamps for the PTT, in circulation 1976–2002.

### Key works

- Crouwel — Stedelijk Museum posters and catalogues, from 1964.
- Total Design founded, 1963.
- Crouwel — New Alphabet, 1967.
- Benno Wissing / Total Design — Schiphol signage, 1967.
- Ben Bos / Total Design — Randstad identity, late 1960s.
- Crouwel — *Vormgevers*, 1968.
- Total Design — Dutch pavilion interior, Expo '70, Osaka, 1970.
- Crouwel / Total Design — Rabobank wordmark, 1973.
- Crouwel — PTT numeral stamps, 1976.

### Visual vocabulary

Geometric, often custom lettering built on square grids (New Alphabet, Fodor, Gridnik);
visible grids; Helvetica and Univers for text; bright flat primary colour; repetition and
pattern; precise but experimental composition.

### Lineage

From De Stijl (`de-stijl`), Piet Zwart and the New Typography (`new-typography`), and Swiss Style
(`swiss-style`); into 1990s Dutch graphic design, the screen-native pixel aesthetic, and the
2000s modernist revival (`new-swiss-revival`) — New Alphabet came back to life on Peter Saville
and Brett Wickens' sleeve for Joy Division's *Substance* in 1988. Its counter-current is Dutch
too: Anthon Beeke's *Naked Ladies* alphabet (1970), made from photographed nude bodies, was a
deliberate jab at Crouwel's machine aesthetic and an early sign of the expressive turn
(`postmodern-typography`).

### Remix today

Crouwel's move — design *with* the constraint of the display rather than around it — is the
right instinct for any new medium, from e-ink to AR to generated imagery. And Total Design shows
how a studio can take responsibility for a whole environment, not just its logo.

### Wim Crouwel {#wim-crouwel}

Dutch, 1928–2019. Graphic designer, type designer, educator, museum director.

**Context.** Studied fine art at Academie Minerva in Groningen (1947–49), learned typography at
evening classes in Amsterdam, and began working in 1955 with the interior designer Kho Liang Ie.
Co-founded Total Design in 1963, designed for the Stedelijk from 1964, taught at Delft
University of Technology (1965–85) and left Total Design to direct the Museum Boijmans Van
Beuningen in Rotterdam (1985 in most sources; the Dutch Wikipedia's Total Design history says
1982). Nicknamed "Mr Gridnik" for his commitment to the grid.

**Principles.**
1. **The grid as visible structure.** The grid is a generator of letterforms and images, shown
   rather than hidden. *Example:* *Vormgevers* poster, Stedelijk, 1968.
2. **Accept the technology's limits.** Design for what the machine can render. *Example:* New
   Alphabet, 1967.
3. **Neutral but not anonymous.** Systematic design can still carry a strong signature through
   custom lettering. *Example:* the Stedelijk posters, from 1964.

**Key works.** Stedelijk posters and catalogues, from 1964; New Alphabet, 1967; *Vormgevers*,
1968; Dutch pavilion, Expo '70, 1970; Rabobank wordmark, 1973; PTT numeral stamps, 1976; Fodor
and Gridnik typefaces.

**Visual vocabulary.** Square-grid lettering, visible grids, bold flat colour, Helvetica/Univers
text, abstract geometry.

**Lineage.** From Swiss Style and De Stijl; into pixel and screen typography and the 2000s
modernist revival.

**Remix today.** The *Vormgevers* poster is the best single argument for showing your system to
the audience. Exposed grids and generative lettering read as honest and contemporary.

### Total Design {#total-design}

Dutch studio, founded in Amsterdam in 1963 (now Total Identity).

**Context.** Founded as the Associatie voor Total Design NV by Wim Crouwel, Benno Wissing,
Friso Kramer, Dick Schwarz and Paul Schwarz; Ben Bos joined in 1963. Kramer, an industrial
designer, left in 1968 because the work was almost all graphic. The studio became the
Netherlands' largest design office and the model for consultancy-scale identity work there.

**Principles.**
1. **Multidisciplinary by design.** Graphic, product, interior and environmental design under one
   roof so identities extend into space. *Example:* the interior of the Dutch pavilion at Expo
   '70, Osaka, 1970.
2. **Wayfinding as identity.** For a public institution, the signage *is* the identity.
   *Example:* Schiphol Airport signage by Benno Wissing, 1967.
3. **Systematic corporate identity.** Logos specified with manuals and applied consistently.
   *Example:* Ben Bos's Randstad logo and house style, late 1960s.

**Key works.** Schiphol signage, 1967; Randstad identity (Bos), late 1960s; Expo '70 pavilion
interior, 1970; Rabobank wordmark (Crouwel), 1973.

**Visual vocabulary.** Sans-serif, rigorous colour coding, geometric logotypes, grids.

**Lineage.** From Swiss Style and American consultancies; into Dutch corporate design and studios
like Studio Dumbar, which defined itself partly against it.

**Remix today.** Total Design's structure — a studio that owns the whole environment of a
client — is close to what in-house brand and product design teams try to be now.

## Italian Modernism {#italian-modernism}

Milan and Ivrea, c. 1936–1975. Corporate modernism around Olivetti, Pirelli, La Rinascente and
the Studio Boggeri.

### Context

Italy's version of modernism was born inside companies, not schools. Adriano Olivetti, who ran
his father's typewriter firm from the 1930s until his death in 1960, believed that industry had
cultural and social duties, and hired painters, poets, architects and graphic designers to
shape everything from typewriters to factories to calendars. Giovanni Pintori joined Olivetti's
advertising office in 1936 and Marcello Nizzoli met Adriano Olivetti in 1938. In Milan, Antonio
Boggeri's Studio Boggeri (from 1933) brought Swiss designers — Max Huber, Walter Ballmer,
Carlo Vivarelli and others — into Italian advertising. After the war, La Rinascente department
store, Pirelli and Campari became patrons too. The Compasso d'Oro award was founded in 1954, and
the 1952 MoMA exhibition *Olivetti: Design in Industry* made the company the world's model of
corporate design.

The Italians took the Swiss toolkit — sans-serif, abstraction, photomontage, grids — and added
colour, wit, sensual form and Futurist energy. It is the modernism that sells.

### Principles

1. **Culture as corporate identity.** A company's identity is everything it makes and
   commissions — products, buildings, ads, art. *Example:* the MoMA exhibition *Olivetti: Design
   in Industry*, 1952.
2. **Abstraction that seduces.** Abstract forms, numbers and colour express a product's idea
   (calculation, speed, writing) instead of showing it. *Example:* Pintori's Olivetti
   advertising of the late 1940s–50s, built from scattered numerals and coloured shapes.
3. **Sculptural products.** The industrial object as a soft, unified sculpture. *Example:*
   Nizzoli's Olivetti Lettera 22 portable typewriter, 1950 (Compasso d'Oro 1954).
4. **Kinetic typography.** Type layered, transparent and in motion. *Example:* Max Huber's Gran
   Premio dell'Autodromo di Monza poster, 1948.

### Key works

- Pintori — Olivetti logotype, 1947; Olivetti posters, 1940s–60s; Olivetti calendars, 1951–69.
- Nizzoli — Lexikon 80, 1948; Lettera 22, 1950; Necchi Mirella sewing machine, 1957.
- Huber — Monza Grand Prix poster, 1948; La Rinascente mark and logotype, 1950.
- Munari — *libri illeggibili*, from 1949; Campari poster *Declinazione grafica del nome
  Campari*, 1964; Falkland lamp (Danese), 1964; *Arte come mestiere*, 1966.
- Bob Noorda — signage for the Milan Metropolitana line 1 (with Franco Albini and Franca
  Helg), 1964.

### Visual vocabulary

Sans-serif and grotesque type in bold colour; overlapping transparent planes; abstract shapes and
numerals; photomontage; a sculptural, rounded product language; wit and play.

### Lineage

From Futurism (`futurism`), the Bauhaus (`bauhaus`), the New Typography (`new-typography`) and Swiss
designers in Milan (`swiss-style`); into Unimark International (founded 1965 by Noorda, Vignelli
and others), American corporate identity (`corporate-identity`), and the Italian radical design
that followed (`memphis`, via Ettore Sottsass, who also designed for Olivetti).

### Remix today

The Italians are the counter-argument to the idea that systematic means cold. A brand system can
be rigorous and still joyful, warm and sensual — Olivetti did it across products, posters and
buildings at once.

### Giovanni Pintori {#giovanni-pintori}

Italian, 1912–1999. Graphic designer, art director, painter.

**Context.** Born in Tresnuraghes, Sardinia; studied at ISIA Monza on a scholarship from 1930;
joined Olivetti's advertising office in 1936 and led it (from 1940 according to Italian sources,
as art director from 1950 according to English ones) until he left in 1967, seven years after
Adriano Olivetti's death. He was the visual voice of Olivetti through its golden age.

**Principles.**
1. **Show the idea, not the machine.** Advertising evokes what a product *does* through
   abstraction. *Example:* his Olivetti posters for calculating machines, built from
   cascading numerals, 1940s–50s.
2. **The logotype as a typographic signature.** A lowercase, wide-spaced sans wordmark.
   *Example:* the Olivetti logotype, 1947.
3. **Painterly modernism.** Colour fields and playful shapes on a structured page. *Example:*
   the Olivetti calendars he edited, 1951–69.

**Key works.** Olivetti logotype, 1947; Olivetti posters, from the 1940s; Olivetti calendars,
1951–69; work shown in *Olivetti: Design in Industry*, MoMA, 1952.

**Visual vocabulary.** Lowercase sans-serif, flat colour shapes, numerals and symbols as pattern,
generous white space.

**Lineage.** From Futurism and the Bauhaus via ISIA; into Olivetti's later identity (Walter
Ballmer's 1970 logotype) and corporate design worldwide.

**Remix today.** Pintori shows how to advertise something abstract (software, data, AI) without
literal illustration: find the symbol of what it does and make it dance.

### Max Huber {#max-huber}

Swiss, 1919–1992. Graphic designer, worked mainly in Italy.

**Context.** Trained in Zürich, joined the Studio Boggeri in Milan in 1940, returned to Switzerland
during the war (working on the magazine *Du* and exhibiting with Allianz), and came back to Milan
after 1945, where he redesigned Einaudi's books, the newspaper *24 Ore* (1948) and La Rinascente's
mark (1950). Compasso d'Oro 1954.

**Principles.**
1. **Transparency and overlap.** Coloured planes, type and photographs layered as if
   transparent, creating depth and speed. *Example:* Monza Grand Prix poster, 1948.
2. **Swiss order, Italian colour.** A constructive base with saturated, festive colour. *Example:*
   La Rinascente identity, 1950.

**Key works.** Sirenella dance hall poster, 1946; Monza Grand Prix poster, 1948; Borsalino
posters, 1949; La Rinascente mark and logotype, 1950; Einaudi book design, from 1940s.

**Visual vocabulary.** Overprinted primary colours, arrows and circles, sans-serif type in motion,
photomontage.

**Lineage.** From Zürich constructivism and Allianz; the bridge between Swiss and Italian
modernism.

**Remix today.** Huber's overprinting is a direct model for motion and layered UI: transparency
can show speed and hierarchy at the same time.

### Bruno Munari {#bruno-munari}

Italian, 1907–1998. Artist, designer, writer, educator.

**Context.** Started with the Futurists in 1927; made his first hanging *macchina aerea* in 1930
and the *macchine inutili* (useless machines) from 1933; worked as art director for Mondadori
magazines; co-founded the Movimento Arte Concreta in 1948; designed objects for Danese from the
late 1950s, toys, children's books and workshops. In 1962 he organised the first exhibition of
*arte programmata* (programmed art) in Olivetti's Milan shop. Compasso d'Oro in 1954, 1955 and
1979, and for his career in 1995.

**Principles.**
1. **Play as method.** Curiosity and play are serious design tools. *Example:* the *libri
   illeggibili* (unreadable books), from 1949 — books with no words, made of cut, coloured and
   threaded paper.
2. **Variation from one element.** Generate many forms by systematically varying one thing.
   *Example:* *Declinazione grafica del nome Campari*, 1964 — one poster made from dozens of
   variations of the word Campari.
3. **Design as an ordinary craft.** The designer is a craftsman of everyday life, not a genius.
   *Example:* *Arte come mestiere*, 1966 (English as *Design as Art*, 1971).

**Key works.** *Macchine inutili*, from 1933; *libri illeggibili*, from 1949; Cubo ashtray
(Danese), 1958; Campari poster, 1964; Falkland lamp (Danese), 1964; *Arte come mestiere*, 1966;
Abitacolo, 1971.

**Visual vocabulary.** Playful geometry, primary colours, kinetic and modular objects, typographic
variation, materials used for touch.

**Lineage.** From Futurism (`futurism`) and concrete art; into programmed and generative art,
children's design and Italian radical design.

**Remix today.** Munari is the patron of generative identity: one word, varied systematically, is
the 1964 version of a dynamic logo. His workshops are a model for design education through play.

### Marcello Nizzoli {#marcello-nizzoli}

Italian, 1887–1969. Painter, architect, industrial and graphic designer.

**Context.** Trained at the Accademia di Belle Arti in Parma (1913); designed posters for Campari
and Martini and exhibition interiors in the Rationalist manner in the 1920s–30s; met Adriano
Olivetti in 1938 and became Olivetti's chief product designer. He won the first Compasso d'Oro,
in 1954, for the Lettera 22, and a second in 1957 for the Necchi Mirella.

**Principles.**
1. **The product as sculpture.** A single, softly modelled shell unifies the mechanism.
   *Example:* Lexikon 80 typewriter, 1948.
2. **Portable and personal.** Mechanical precision in a light, friendly object for one person.
   *Example:* Lettera 22, 1950 (some sources date its design to 1949) — the Illinois Institute of
   Technology named it the best design product of the previous century in 1959.
3. **Integration of the arts.** Product, graphic and architectural design as one practice.
   *Example:* the ENI office building at San Donato Milanese, 1956–58.

**Key works.** Lexikon 80, 1948; Aurora 88 pen, 1948; Lettera 22, 1950; Divisumma 24 calculator,
1956; Necchi Mirella, 1957; ENI office building, 1956–58.

**Visual vocabulary.** Rounded die-cast shells, pastel and neutral colours, integrated keyboards,
sculptural continuity.

**Lineage.** From Italian Rationalism; into Ettore Sottsass's Olivetti work and the soft form of
later consumer electronics.

**Remix today.** Nizzoli's counterpoint to Rams: the same functional honesty, but with a warm,
tactile shell. A product can be clear and still invite touch.

### Bob Noorda {#bob-noorda}

Dutch, 1927–2010. Graphic designer, worked mainly in Milan.

**Context.** Trained in Amsterdam, moved to Milan in the early 1950s, worked for Pirelli, and in
1964 designed the signage for the first line of the Milan Metropolitana with the architects Franco
Albini and Franca Helg. In 1965 he co-founded Unimark International with Massimo Vignelli and
others, through which he worked on the New York subway signage with Vignelli in the late 1960s.

**Principles.**
1. **Signage as continuous system.** Information carried on a continuous band at a fixed height
   so the passenger always knows where to look. *Example:* Milan Metro line 1, 1964 — the
   station name repeated along a red band at eye level.
2. **Typographic consistency at scale.** One modified sans, one colour code, applied everywhere.
   *Example:* the New York City Transit wayfinding work with Vignelli at Unimark, late 1960s.

**Key works.** Pirelli advertising, 1950s–60s; Milan Metro signage, 1964; Unimark International,
from 1965; New York subway signage (with Vignelli), late 1960s.

**Visual vocabulary.** Modified Helvetica and Akzidenz, bold colour bands, consistent placement,
repetition.

**Lineage.** From Dutch and Swiss modernism; into Unimark and American corporate modernism
(`american-modernism`, `corporate-identity`).

**Remix today.** Noorda's repeated name band is the original "persistent navigation": put the
answer to "where am I?" in the same place, every time.

## Events

- **1949** — Max Bill's *Die gute Form* exhibition for the Swiss Werkbund turns "good form" into
  a public argument.
- **1952** — MoMA's *Olivetti: Design in Industry* makes Olivetti the world's model of corporate
  design.
- **1953** — HfG Ulm opens (teaching from 3 August); the building follows in 1955.
- **1954** — First Compasso d'Oro awards (Nizzoli's Lettera 22 among the winners).
- **1956** — Braun SK 4 (Gugelot & Rams): the appliance as a rational object.
- **1957** — Neue Haas Grotesk launched at Graphic 57 in Lausanne; Univers released by Deberny &
  Peignot for Lumitype phototypesetting.
- **1958** — *Neue Grafik* no. 1.
- **1960** — Stempel renames Neue Haas Grotesk "Helvetica" for Linotype.
- **1962** — Aicher's Lufthansa identity, the first complete corporate identity system from Ulm.
- **1963** — Total Design founded in Amsterdam.
- **1964** — Gerstner's *Designing Programmes*; Milan Metro signage.
- **1965** — Hofmann's *Graphic Design Manual*; Unimark International founded.
- **1967** — Ruder's *Typographie*; Crouwel's New Alphabet.
- **1968** — HfG Ulm closes; Weingart begins teaching at Basel, the start of the Swiss reaction.
- **1972** — Munich Olympics identity by Otl Aicher.
- **1974** — US Department of Transportation pictograms, derived from Aicher's Munich system.
- **1981** — Müller-Brockmann's *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*.
