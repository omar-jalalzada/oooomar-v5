# The avant-garde, 1905–1939

Slice id: `avant-garde`. Between the founding of Die Brücke in Dresden (1905) and the outbreak of
the Second World War, a handful of small, quarrelsome groups invented almost everything a graphic
designer still reaches for: asymmetric layout, the sans serif as the voice of the present,
photomontage, the grid as a moral position, the pictogram, the poster as a machine for
stopping traffic, and the idea that a designer is an engineer of communication rather than a
decorator. The movements fed each other through magazines, touring exhibitions and a few people
who never stayed put (El Lissitzky, Theo van Doesburg, László Moholy-Nagy), and they ended the
same way: closed by the Nazis in Germany, dissolved by decree in the USSR, or absorbed into
advertising in Paris, London and New York.

Read the slice in roughly chronological order. Movements are `##`; the figures most tied to each
follow as `###`. Several people belong to more than one movement; they are filed where their
defining work sits.

Backbone: Meggs & Purvis, *Meggs' History of Graphic Design* (chapters on "The Influence of Modern
Art", "Pictorial Modernism", "A New Language of Form" and "The Bauhaus and the New Typography").
Dates were checked against Wikipedia and museum records (V&A, MoMA, Bauhaus-Archiv); disagreements
are flagged in place.

## Expressionism {#expressionism}

**Context.** Die Brücke formed in Dresden in 1905 (Kirchner, Heckel, Schmidt-Rottluff, Bleyl);
Der Blaue Reiter followed in Munich in 1911 (Kandinsky, Marc, Klee, Macke). The term only settled
around 1913. Expressionism reacted against both academic naturalism and the polish of Jugendstil,
and it revived the woodcut as a modern, deliberately crude medium. Herwarth Walden's journal and
gallery *Der Sturm* (Berlin, from 1910) was its publishing engine and later showed Schwitters and
the Russians. For graphic design it matters as the starting point the Bauhaus had to leave: the
school's 1919 manifesto is an Expressionist object.

**Principles.**
- *The cut shows.* The gouge mark of the woodblock is the expression; nothing is smoothed.
  Example: Kirchner's woodcut programme and membership prints for the Brücke, 1905–1906.
- *Emotion over description.* Colour and distortion carry feeling, not likeness. Example:
  Kandinsky and Marc's *Der Blaue Reiter* almanac, 1912.
- *Image and lettering cut as one block.* Letters are carved with the picture, so type inherits
  the image's roughness. Example: Lyonel Feininger's woodcut *Cathedral* on the cover of the
  Bauhaus manifesto, 1919.

**Key works.** *Programm der Künstlergruppe Brücke* (Kirchner, 1906); *Der Blaue Reiter*
almanac (Kandinsky & Marc, 1912); *Der Sturm* journal (Walden, from 1910); Feininger, *Cathedral*,
Bauhaus manifesto cover (1919).

**Visual vocabulary.** Black woodcut on raw paper, hand-cut capitals, jagged contour, hot
unmixed colour, figures compressed into the block's edges, no grid.

**Lineage.** From Jugendstil, Munch and the Arts and Crafts woodcut revival; into early Bauhaus
(Itten's preliminary course, Feininger), Dada's anti-polish, and, much later, the Polish poster
and punk's hand-cut graphics.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Expressionism is permission to leave the tool's mark in the work: a visible
cut, a rough edge, a crop that pinches. Used against a clean system, one hand-made gesture reads
as conviction rather than sloppiness.

## Futurism {#futurism}

**Context.** Marinetti's *Manifesto of Futurism* first ran in the *Gazzetta dell'Emilia* on
5 February 1909 and reached Europe from the front page of *Le Figaro* on 20 February 1909. The
movement was Milan-based (Boccioni, Carrà, Balla, Severini, Russolo) and worshipped speed,
machines, the city and war; it was also, from 1919, entangled with Fascism. Futurism's design
contribution is typographic: *parole in libertà* (words-in-freedom), launched in the *Technical
Manifesto of Futurist Literature* (11 May 1912), threw out syntax and the static page. Cheap
letterpress, the daily paper and the poster wall were the enabling technologies. A "Second
Futurism" (Depero, aeropittura) carried it into advertising through the 1920s and 30s.

**Principles.**
- *Typography as sound.* Size, weight and face render loudness, onomatopoeia and rhythm, so the
  page is performed rather than read. Example: Marinetti, *Zang Tumb Tuuum*, Milan, 1914.
- *Destroy the horizontal line.* Words run diagonally, curve, explode from a centre; the reading
  path is the composition. Example: Marinetti, *Les mots en liberté futuristes*, 1919, with its
  fold-out plates.
- *Many typefaces on one page.* Marinetti's 1913 manifesto *Destruction of Syntax* called for
  several ink colours and as many as twenty faces on a page where needed; mixing is the point.
  Example: Carlo Carrà, *Interventionist Demonstration*, collage, 1914.
- *The book as object.* Binding, material and format are part of the message. Example: Depero,
  *Depero futurista*, the "bolted book", 1927.
- *Advertising is art.* The Futurists treated commerce as modern life's real theatre. Example:
  Depero's Campari campaigns, 1925–1939, and his Campari Soda bottle, 1932.

**Key works.** Marinetti, *Manifesto of Futurism* (1909); Marinetti, *Zang Tumb Tuuum* (1914);
Carrà, *Interventionist Demonstration* (1914); Marinetti, *Les mots en liberté futuristes*
(1919); Depero, *Depero futurista* (1927); Depero, Campari Soda bottle (1932).

**Visual vocabulary.** Mixed wood and metal type in many sizes, bold grotesques next to
italics and scripts, diagonals and radiating lines, onomatopoeic words set huge, mathematical and
musical signs, collage of newspaper fragments, red and black on newsprint; later (Depero) flat,
mechanised, toy-like figures in hard colour.

**Lineage.** From Symbolist poetry, Cubist collage and the popular press; into Russian
Cubo-Futurism (and through it Suprematism and Constructivism), Vorticism, Dada, and the dynamic
diagonal of the New Typography.

**Quotes.** "We affirm that the world's magnificence has been enriched by a new beauty: the beauty
of speed." Marinetti, *Manifesto of Futurism*, 1909 (English translations vary slightly; this
follows R. W. Flint's).

**Remix today.** Futurism is the case for typography with a volume knob. Kinetic type, variable
fonts and audio-reactive layouts are its native medium. Take the idea that the size and position
of a word can *be* its sound, and leave the war-worship behind.

### Filippo Tommaso Marinetti {#filippo-tommaso-marinetti}

**Context.** Italian poet, editor and impresario (1876–1944), born in Alexandria. He founded
Futurism with the 1909 manifesto and ran it as a publicity machine, touring Paris, London, Berlin
and Russia; he co-wrote the Fascist Manifesto in 1919.

**Principles.**
- *Words-in-freedom.* Abolish syntax, adjectives and punctuation; let nouns collide. Example:
  *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Literature*, 1912.
- *The manifesto as medium.* A movement exists once it has published its rules. Example: the
  *Le Figaro* front page, 20 February 1909.
- *Typographic revolution against "harmony".* The page should be dissonant on purpose. Example:
  *Zang Tumb Tuuum*, 1914, an account of the siege of Adrianople set as bombardment.

**Key works.** *Manifesto of Futurism* (manifesto, 1909); *Zang Tumb Tuuum* (book, 1914);
*Les mots en liberté futuristes* (book, 1919).

**Visual vocabulary.** Letterpress at war with itself: bold display caps beside small italics,
lines at every angle, onomatopoeia ("zang tumb tumb") set as image.

**Lineage.** From Mallarmé's *Un coup de dés* and the press; into Apollinaire's calligrammes,
Dada sound poetry and the concrete poetry of the 1950s.

**Quotes.** See Futurism above.

**Remix today.** Write the rules before you make the work, then make work that obeys them
loudly. A manifesto is still the fastest way to give a studio a voice.

### Fortunato Depero {#fortunato-depero}

**Context.** Italian painter and designer (1892–1960) from Rovereto. With Balla he signed
*Futurist Reconstruction of the Universe* (1915), then opened a Casa d'Arte Futurista in
Rovereto in 1919 making toys, tapestries and advertising. He worked in New York 1928–1930.

**Principles.**
- *Self-promotion as a format.* The portfolio is itself a designed object. Example: *Depero
  futurista 1913–1927*, letterpress with two aluminium bolts as the binding, printed by Mercurio,
  Rovereto, 1927, with Fedele Azari.
- *Brand as a total world.* Ads, objects and packaging speak one invented language. Example:
  Campari advertising 1925–1939 and the conical Campari Soda bottle, 1932.
- *Mechanical figuration.* People and things reduced to toy-like, interlocking solids. Example:
  his Campari figures of the late 1920s.

**Key works.** *Depero futurista* (book, 1927); Campari Soda bottle (product, 1932); Campari
advertising (poster series, 1925–1939).

**Visual vocabulary.** Flat interlocking volumes, hard reds and blacks, blocky custom lettering
built from the same geometry as the figures, bold mirror symmetry.

**Lineage.** From Balla and Futurist toys; anticipates Art Deco advertising and the
character-led brand worlds of the 1950s.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Depero shows how one maker's drawing style can become a brand's entire
vocabulary, down to the bottle. A design system can be a hand, not just a set of tokens.

## Vorticism {#vorticism}

**Context.** London's brief avant-garde, formed in 1914 by Wyndham Lewis with Ezra Pound (who
named it), Edward Wadsworth, Henri Gaudier-Brzeska and others. It was a riposte to Marinetti's
London campaign: Lewis's circle heckled his performance on 12 June 1914, and *BLAST* appeared on
2 July 1914 (dated 20 June). The war ended it almost at once; *BLAST 2* came out in July 1915.

**Principles.**
- *Bless and blast.* Lists of enemies and heroes as layout; the magazine page as polemic.
  Example: *BLAST* no. 1, 1914.
- *Typographic shouting with heavy grotesques.* Huge black sans caps, set in blocks, ragged and
  stacked. Example: the *BLAST* cover, 1914, the title printed across a magenta ground.
- *The vortex.* Hard-edged, angular abstraction around a still centre, not Futurist blur.
  Example: Wadsworth's woodcuts, 1914–1917.

**Key works.** *BLAST* no. 1 (magazine, 1914); *BLAST* no. 2 (magazine, 1915); Lewis, *Timon of
Athens* portfolio (1913).

**Visual vocabulary.** Magenta cover, black condensed wood-type grotesques in enormous sizes,
dense justified blocks of caps, splintered geometric drawings.

**Lineage.** From Futurism and Cubism; into Kauffer's posters and, distantly, punk and
1990s magazine typography.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** A two-column list of what you love and hate, set big, is still a devastating
editorial device.

### Wyndham Lewis {#wyndham-lewis}

**Context.** British painter and writer (1882–1957), born off Nova Scotia, founder of the Rebel
Art Centre (March 1914) and author of most of *BLAST*.

**Principles.**
- *Polemic as layout.* The argument is carried by type scale and list structure. Example:
  *BLAST* 1, 1914.
- *Hard edges over speed lines.* Static, mechanical structure rather than Futurist motion.
  Example: *Timon of Athens* portfolio, 1913.

**Key works.** *Timon of Athens* (portfolio, 1913); *BLAST* 1 (magazine, 1914); *BLAST* 2
(magazine, 1915).

**Visual vocabulary.** Black grotesque caps stacked in justified blocks; angular figures.

**Lineage.** Futurism, Cubism; into British modernist publishing and poster design.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Edit like a pamphleteer: every heading is a verdict.

## Suprematism {#suprematism}

**Context.** Kazimir Malevich announced Suprematism at *The Last Futurist Exhibition of Paintings
0,10* in Petrograd in December 1915, hanging *Black Square* in the icon corner. It grew out of
Russian Cubo-Futurism and *zaum* (transrational) poetry: Malevich designed sets and costumes for
the opera *Victory over the Sun* (1913) and later said a square curtain there was the first
appearance of the Black Square. After 1917 it became a state-supported language, taught by
Malevich and Lissitzky through UNOVIS in Vitebsk (1919–1922). Suprematism was anti-utilitarian;
its forms were taken up by the Constructivists who rejected its mysticism.

**Principles.**
- *The zero of form.* Strip painting to elementary shapes on a white ground; pure feeling, no
  object. Example: Malevich, *Black Square*, 1915.
- *Floating planes in infinite white space.* No horizon, no gravity; forms drift at angles.
  Example: Malevich, *Suprematist Composition: Airplane Flying*, 1915.
- *A grammar of elements.* Square, circle, cross and bar as a teachable vocabulary. Example:
  Malevich, *The Non-Objective World*, Bauhaus Book 11, 1927.
- *Abstract forms can narrate.* Shapes as characters in a story. Example: Lissitzky, *About Two
  Squares*, designed 1920, published Berlin 1922.

**Key works.** Malevich, *Black Square* (1915); Malevich, *From Cubism and Futurism to
Suprematism* (1915); Malevich, *Suprematist Composition: White on White* (1918); Lissitzky, *About
Two Squares* (1922); Malevich, *The Non-Objective World* (1927).

**Visual vocabulary.** Black, red and occasional yellow or blue planes on white; squares,
rectangles, circles and thin bars set at diagonals; generous empty field; no type in the
paintings, but the same planes migrate onto book covers.

**Lineage.** From Cubo-Futurism and *zaum*; into Constructivism, Lissitzky's Prouns, the
Bauhaus, the New Typography, and later minimalism and Swiss abstraction.

**Quotes.** Malevich wrote in *From Cubism and Futurism to Suprematism* (1915) that he had
"transformed" himself "in the zero of form" (phrase as given in standard English translations).

**Remix today.** Suprematism is the composition lesson hidden inside every good asymmetric
layout: a few weighted shapes, tension on the diagonal, a lot of white. Try a layout with no
alignment at all, held together only by balance.

### Kazimir Malevich {#kazimir-malevich}

**Context.** Painter and theorist (1879–1935), born in Kyiv (some older sources give 1878).
Cubo-Futurist before 1915, founder of Suprematism, teacher at Vitebsk from 1918 and leader of
UNOVIS from 1919. His only trip abroad was to Warsaw and Berlin in 1927; under Stalin he returned
to figuration.

**Principles.**
- *Feeling over object.* Art should give pure sensation, not depict. Example: *Black Square*,
  1915.
- *Reduction as a radical act.* The smallest possible statement is the most provocative.
  Example: *Suprematist Composition: White on White*, 1918.
- *Teach the elements.* A vocabulary of forms can be transmitted as a school. Example: UNOVIS,
  Vitebsk, 1919–1922.

**Key works.** *Victory over the Sun* designs (other, 1913); *Black Square* (other, 1915); *From
Cubism and Futurism to Suprematism* (manifesto, 1915); *White on White* (other, 1918); *The
Non-Objective World* (book, 1927).

**Visual vocabulary.** Primary planes on white, the square as sign.

**Lineage.** From Cubism, Futurism and Russian icons; into Lissitzky, Klutsis and the whole
geometric tradition.

**Quotes.** See Suprematism above.

**Remix today.** Name your one shape. A brand that owns a single form can be recognised with
nothing else on the page.

## Dada {#dada}

**Context.** Born at the Cabaret Voltaire, opened by Hugo Ball and Emmy Hennings at Spiegelgasse 1
in Zurich on 5 February 1916, among war refugees (Tzara, Arp, Janco, Huelsenbeck, Taeuber-Arp).
Dada answered the slaughter of the First World War with nonsense, chance and anti-art. It
splintered into Berlin (Club Dada, 1918; politically communist, inventor of photomontage), Hanover
(Schwitters, alone), Cologne (Ernst), Paris and New York (Duchamp, Picabia, Man Ray). For design
its gifts are photomontage, the collage of found print, and typographic anarchy. The *First
International Dada Fair* in Berlin (30 June–25 August 1920) was its high point.

**Principles.**
- *Found material over drawn image.* Cut the mass media up and re-assemble it to say what it
  hides. Example: Hannah Höch, *Cut with the Kitchen Knife*, 1919–1920.
- *Photomontage as political weapon.* Recombined photographs make the lie visible. Example:
  John Heartfield, *Adolf, the Superman: Swallows Gold and Spouts Junk*, AIZ, 1932.
- *Typography without hierarchy.* Mixed faces, sizes and directions; the reader is ambushed.
  Example: Ball's sound poem "Karawane", printed in Huelsenbeck's *Dada Almanach*, 1920, each
  line in a different face.
- *Chance and nonsense.* Meaning is freed by removing intention. Example: Ball's performance of
  his sound poems in a cardboard costume, Cabaret Voltaire, June 1916.
- *Everything is material.* Bus tickets, adverts, bank letterheads. Example: Schwitters'
  *Merzbild*, 1918–1919, named after a fragment of "Commerz- und Privatbank".

**Key works.** *Cabaret Voltaire* (magazine, 1916); Höch, *Cut with the Kitchen Knife*
(1919–1920); *First International Dada Fair* (exhibition, 1920); Schwitters, *Merz* (magazine,
1923–1932); Van Doesburg and Schwitters, *Kleine Dada Soirée* poster (1922); Heartfield, AIZ
covers (1930–1938).

**Visual vocabulary.** Letterpress chaos: wood and metal type of every face, upside-down lines,
printers' ornaments and rules as images, newspaper cuttings, photographic heads on mismatched
bodies, black and red on cheap paper.

**Lineage.** From Futurism (typography, performance), Cubist collage and Expressionism; into
Surrealism, the New Typography (Schwitters, Van Doesburg, Lissitzky all crossed over),
Constructivist photomontage, Fluxus, punk and the 1990s grunge page.

**Quotes.** Ball, writing about the war: "The war is founded on a glaring mistake – men have been
confused with machines." (as quoted in his biography; cited via Wikipedia).

**Remix today.** Dada is the licence to build with other people's images and make the seams
the message. Collage of found screenshots and stock is an everyday medium now; the Dada move is
to cut it so the source is legible and the meaning is turned against it.

### Hugo Ball {#hugo-ball}

**Context.** German poet and performer (1886–1927), co-founder of the Cabaret Voltaire in Zurich
in 1916, credited with naming Dada, and a pioneer of sound poetry.

**Principles.**
- *Language below meaning.* Invented words heard as pure sound. Example: "Karawane", 1916,
  opening "jolifanto bambla ô falli bambla".
- *The body as typeface.* Performance as composition. Example: his cardboard "magic bishop"
  costume for the sound poems, Cabaret Voltaire, 23 June 1916.

**Key works.** *Cabaret Voltaire* (magazine, 1916); "Karawane" (other, 1916); Dada Manifesto
(manifesto, 1916).

**Visual vocabulary.** When "Karawane" was printed (1920), each line used a different face,
weight and size, making the poem's sound visible.

**Lineage.** From Futurist *parole in libertà*; into sound and concrete poetry and the variable
typography of later experimental print.

**Quotes.** See Dada above.

**Remix today.** Specify a typeface by how it should sound. Ball is the patron saint of
voice-and-tone work.

### Kurt Schwitters {#kurt-schwitters}

**Context.** German artist (1887–1948) from Hanover. He began Merz collages in late 1918,
published the magazine *Merz* (1923–1932), ran an advertising studio, Merz-Werbezentrale, designed
the City of Hanover's printed matter around 1929–1934, and co-founded the *ring neue
werbegestalter* (circle of new advertising designers) in 1927–28. Exiled from 1937 to Norway and
then England; died in Kendal.

**Principles.**
- *Merz: build from fragments.* Everything had broken; new work is made from the pieces. Example:
  *Das Merzbild*, 1918–1919.
- *Dada and Constructivism are compatible.* Play and order in the same page. Example: *Merz* 4,
  July 1923, which carried Lissitzky's "Topography of Typography".
- *Typography as illustration.* Letters, rules and bars act as characters. Example: *Die
  Scheuche* (The Scarecrow), a typographic fairy tale with Käte Steinitz and Theo van Doesburg,
  1925.
- *Advertising is a form of the work.* Example: *Merz* 11, "Typoreklame", the Pelikan issue,
  1924.

**Key works.** *Das Merzbild* (other, 1919); *Merz* (magazine, 1923–1932); *Merz* 11
"Typoreklame" (magazine, 1924); *Die Scheuche* (book, 1925); *Ursonate* (other, 1922–1932).

**Visual vocabulary.** Found tickets and labels; bold grotesque, thick bars and rules,
asymmetric balance, red and black, letters as actors.

**Lineage.** From Expressionism and Dada; into the New Typography (ring neue werbegestalter),
installation art (the Merzbau) and collage-based graphics ever since.

**Quotes.** "Everything had broken down and new things had to be made out of the fragments; and
this is Merz." Schwitters, as quoted in the standard literature (cited via Wikipedia).

**Remix today.** Schwitters proves the playful and the systematic can live in one studio. Run
a rigorous grid and let one element on every page misbehave.

### John Heartfield {#john-heartfield}

**Context.** Born Helmut Herzfeld (1891–1968) in Berlin; anglicised his name in 1916 in protest
at anti-British nationalism. Co-founded the Malik-Verlag in 1917 with his brother Wieland
Herzfelde and George Grosz. His photomontages for the communist *Arbeiter-Illustrierte-Zeitung*
(AIZ) ran from 1930 until 1938, continuing from Prague after he fled in April 1933.

**Principles.**
- *The photograph cannot lie, so make it tell the truth.* Seamless montage gives satire the
  authority of documentary. Example: *Adolf, the Superman: Swallows Gold and Spouts Junk*, AIZ,
  17 July 1932, an X-ray of Hitler's chest full of coins.
- *One image, one idea.* A single sharp visual pun with a caption. Example: four bloody axes
  forming a swastika, AIZ, 8 March 1934.
- *The book jacket as poster.* The cover argues the book's politics. Example: Malik-Verlag
  jackets, including Upton Sinclair titles, 1920s.

**Key works.** Malik-Verlag book jackets (book, 1920s); *Adolf, the Superman* (magazine, 1932);
*The Meaning of Geneva* (magazine, 1932); *Göring: The Executioner of the Third Reich* (magazine,
1933).

**Visual vocabulary.** Retouched, seamless photomontage in rotogravure, a single central image,
a bold sans or serif caption, AIZ masthead in red.

**Lineage.** From Berlin Dada; into Klutsis, Polish posters, 1980s activist graphics
(Gran Fury, Klaus Staeck) and today's satirical image edit.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Heartfield is the benchmark for the one-image argument. With generative
imaging, seamless montage is trivial; his discipline was the single idea and the caption that
locks it.

### Hannah Höch {#hannah-hoch}

**Context.** German artist (1889–1978), the only woman in Berlin Dada and, with Raoul Hausmann, a
pioneer of photomontage. From 1916 to 1926 she worked for the Ullstein press designing needlework
patterns, which runs through her collages.

**Principles.**
- *Collage as social X-ray.* Scale and juxtaposition expose power. Example: *Cut with the Kitchen
  Knife Dada Through the Last Weimar Beer-Belly Cultural Epoch of Germany*, 1919–1920.
- *Gender as subject.* The "New Woman" of the illustrated press, re-cut. Example: *Da-Dandy*,
  1919.
- *Pattern and craft as source.* Domestic templates meet magazine photography. Example: the
  series *From an Ethnographic Museum*, 1924–1930.

**Key works.** *Cut with the Kitchen Knife* (other, 1919–1920); *Da-Dandy* (other, 1919);
*From an Ethnographic Museum* (other, 1924–1930).

**Visual vocabulary.** Dense, all-over photomontage, wheels and machines among faces, words cut
from headlines, abrupt scale shifts.

**Lineage.** From Dada and craft design; into feminist collage, Barbara Kruger's generation and
contemporary editorial collage.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Höch shows that a dense, apparently chaotic composition can carry a precise
argument. Let every cut-out be a claim.

## De Stijl {#de-stijl}

**Context.** Founded in 1917 in neutral Netherlands around Theo van Doesburg's magazine *De
Stijl*, with Piet Mondrian, Bart van der Leck, Vilmos Huszár, J. J. P. Oud, Antony Kok and, from
1918, Gerrit Rietveld. Its theory, *Nieuwe Beelding* (Neo-Plasticism), sought a universal
harmony of horizontal and vertical, primaries and non-colours. Around 1924 Van Doesburg's
Elementarism admitted the diagonal and Mondrian withdrew. The magazine ran to 1928, with a final
memorial issue in 1932 after Van Doesburg's death. Van Doesburg's 1922 residence in Weimar pushed
the Bauhaus away from Expressionism.

**Principles.**
- *Only orthogonals and primaries.* Red, yellow and blue plus black, white and grey, in
  rectangles bounded by straight lines. Example: Mondrian, *Composition with Red, Blue and
  Yellow*, 1930.
- *Letters built from the grid.* The alphabet constructed from rectangles. Example: Vilmos
  Huszár's masthead for *De Stijl*, 1917, and Van Doesburg's square alphabet, 1919.
- *Asymmetric equilibrium.* Unequal parts balanced, never centred. Example: Rietveld's *Red Blue
  Chair*, c. 1918–1923.
- *Universal over individual.* Remove the personal hand for a shared language. Example: the
  *De Stijl* manifesto, 1918.
- *The diagonal as dynamism (Elementarism).* Example: Van Doesburg, *Counter-Composition* series,
  from 1924.

**Key works.** *De Stijl* magazine (1917–1932); Huszár, *De Stijl* masthead (1917); Rietveld,
*Red Blue Chair* (c. 1918–1923); Rietveld, Schröder House (1924); Mondrian, *Composition with Red,
Blue and Yellow* (1930).

**Visual vocabulary.** Heavy black bars on white, primary rectangles, all-rectangle lettering,
asymmetric balance on an implicit grid, sans serif caps, no ornament.

**Lineage.** From Cubism and Dutch theosophy; into the Bauhaus, the New Typography, Zwart and
Dutch modernism, Swiss style, and fashion and brand quotations ever since (Yves Saint Laurent's
1965 Mondrian dress).

**Quotes.** Mondrian: the new plastic idea "will ignore the particulars of appearance, that is
to say, natural form and colour", from "Neo-Plasticism in Pictorial Art", *De Stijl*, 1917.

**Remix today.** De Stijl is the ancestor of every layout built from weighted rectangles,
including the bento grid. Its harder lesson is restraint: a fixed, tiny palette and a single
structural rule produce endless variety.

### Theo van Doesburg {#theo-van-doesburg}

**Context.** Dutch painter, writer and designer (1883–1931), founder and editor of *De Stijl*.
He also published Dada as "I. K. Bonset" in *Mecano* (1922), taught an unofficial De Stijl course
in Weimar in 1922, and with Arp and Taeuber-Arp remodelled the Café Aubette in Strasbourg
(1926–1928).

**Principles.**
- *The magazine as movement.* An editor's identity system can be a school. Example: *De Stijl*,
  1917–1928.
- *Construct the alphabet.* Letters as modular rectangles. Example: his geometric alphabet, 1919.
- *Dynamism through the diagonal.* Tilt the grid 45 degrees. Example: *Counter-Compositions*,
  1924 onwards.
- *Rigour and play at once.* Example: *Kleine Dada Soirée* poster with Schwitters, 1922.

**Key works.** *De Stijl* (magazine, 1917); geometric alphabet (typeface, 1919); *Mecano*
(magazine, 1922); *Kleine Dada Soirée* (poster, 1922); Café Aubette (building, 1928).

**Visual vocabulary.** Constructed rectangular caps, primary blocks, diagonal tilts, red and
black on white.

**Lineage.** From Mondrian and Cubism; into the Bauhaus, Zwart, Tschichold and concrete art
(he coined "art concret" in 1930).

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Run your own publication as your argument; consistency of format over years is
what makes a position visible.

### Piet Mondrian {#piet-mondrian}

**Context.** Dutch painter (1872–1944), the theoretical core of De Stijl. He moved from
landscape through Cubism to Neo-Plasticism, lived in Paris 1919–1938, then London and, from 1940,
New York.

**Principles.**
- *Dynamic equilibrium.* Asymmetric rectangles of unequal weight held in balance. Example:
  *Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow*, 1930.
- *The line as structure, not outline.* Black bars of varied thickness divide the field.
  Example: same, 1930.
- *Rhythm from a grid.* Repetition and interval as music. Example: *Broadway Boogie Woogie*,
  1942–1943.

**Key works.** *Le Néo-Plasticisme* (book, 1920); *Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow* (other,
1930); *Broadway Boogie Woogie* (other, 1942–1943).

**Visual vocabulary.** White field, black bars, three primaries, no diagonal.

**Lineage.** From Cubism and theosophy; into nearly all grid-based design.

**Quotes.** See De Stijl above.

**Remix today.** Mondrian's late work shows a rigid system becoming lively once its rules
loosen; that's a useful model for a design system's v2.

## Constructivism {#constructivism}

**Context.** Russian Constructivism grew from Tatlin's counter-reliefs (1915) and was defined in
debates at INKhUK in Moscow, 1920–1922, where the First Working Group of Constructivists
(Rodchenko, Stepanova, Gan, and theorists Arvatov and Brik) formed in 1921. Gan's book
*Konstruktivizm* appeared in 1922. After the October Revolution of 1917 the artist was redefined
as a constructor serving the new society: easel painting gave way to "production art" (posters,
books, textiles, kiosks, advertising), taught at Vkhutemas (1920–1930). Photomontage, cheap
lithography and the illustrated press were the tools. The decree of 23 April 1932 dissolving
independent artistic groups, and the adoption of Socialist Realism in 1934, ended it.

**Principles.**
- *Art into production.* The designer is an engineer making useful things for mass life.
  Example: Rodchenko and Mayakovsky's advertising for Mosselprom and other state trusts,
  1923–1925.
- *Faktura and tektonika.* Truth to material and to construction; show how a thing is made.
  Example: Tatlin, *Monument to the Third International*, 1919–1920.
- *Photomontage and factography.* Photography replaces drawing because it is documentary.
  Example: Rodchenko, *Pro eto*, 1923.
- *The diagonal as revolutionary energy.* Compositions on tilted axes, stark red and black.
  Example: Lissitzky, *Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge*, 1919.
- *The page as built space.* Bars, rules and blocks of type as structural members. Example:
  Lissitzky, *Dlia golosa* (For the Voice), 1923.

**Key works.** Lissitzky, *Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge* (1919); Klutsis, *Electrification
of the Entire Country* (1920); Lissitzky, *Dlia golosa* (1923); Rodchenko, *Pro eto* (1923);
Rodchenko, Lengiz *Books* poster (1924); Stenberg brothers, *Man with a Movie Camera* (1929);
Klutsis, *Let Us Fulfill the Plan of Great Works* (1930).

**Visual vocabulary.** Red and black on cream, heavy bold sans and condensed grotesque, type
set on diagonals and stepped, thick rules and bars, circles and wedges, cropped photographs,
radical angles (worm's- and bird's-eye), hand-drawn constructed letterforms.

**Lineage.** From Futurism, Suprematism and Cubism; into the Bauhaus (via Lissitzky and
Moholy-Nagy), the New Typography, Isotype, Polish and Czech modernism, and later every protest
poster, record sleeve and brand that wants "revolution".

**Quotes.** Vladimir Stenberg on the brothers' film posters: "Ours are eye-catching posters,
designed to shock. We deal with the material in a free manner… disregarding actual proportions…
turning figures upside-down; in short, we employ everything that can make a busy passerby stop in
their tracks." (as quoted in the Stenbergs literature; cited via Wikipedia).

**Remix today.** Constructivism is the case for design as infrastructure: posters, packaging and
kiosks treated as one built system. Its formal kit (the diagonal, the red-black-photo triad) is
over-quoted; its operating idea, that the designer co-writes the message with the copywriter (as
Rodchenko did with Mayakovsky), is under-used.

### El Lissitzky {#el-lissitzky}

**Context.** Russian artist, architect and designer (1890–1941). Taught at Vitebsk with Malevich,
developed the Prouns (1919–), then from 1921 carried the Russian avant-garde to Berlin, Hanover
and the Netherlands: the trilingual magazine *Veshch/Gegenstand/Objet* (1922) with Ehrenburg, *Merz*
with Schwitters, and *Die Kunstismen* with Arp. Back in Moscow he designed exhibitions,
including the Soviet pavilion at *Pressa*, Cologne, 1928.

**Principles.**
- *Optics over phonetics.* The printed word is learnt by sight. Example: "Topography of
  Typography", *Merz* 4, 1923.
- *The book as a constructed object.* Sequence, index and spread designed like a building.
  Example: *Dlia golosa*, 1923, with a die-cut thumb index.
- *Abstract narrative.* Geometric forms tell a story. Example: *About Two Squares*, 1922.
- *Exhibition as immersive medium.* Example: Soviet pavilion, *Pressa*, Cologne, 1928.

**Key works.** *Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge* (poster, 1919); *About Two Squares* (book,
1922); *Dlia golosa* (book, 1923); Pelikan advertising (poster, 1924); *Die Kunstismen* (book,
1925); *Pressa* Soviet pavilion (exhibition, 1928); *USSR Russische Ausstellung* (poster, 1929).

**Visual vocabulary.** Red and black on white, bold grotesques, heavy bars, circles and squares,
diagonals, photomontage and photograms, generous white.

**Lineage.** From Suprematism; into the Bauhaus, Tschichold, Zwart and all of modern book and
exhibition design.

**Quotes.** "The words on the printed sheet are learnt by sight, not by hearing." "Topography of
Typography", *Merz* 4, 1923. On *Dlia golosa*, he called himself the book's "constructor".

**Remix today.** Lissitzky's thumb-indexed book is the ancestor of navigation design. Treat a
document's wayfinding as part of its form, not a utility bolted on.

### Alexander Rodchenko {#alexander-rodchenko}

**Context.** Russian artist, photographer and designer (1891–1956), co-founder of
Constructivism, married to Varvara Stepanova. He gave up painting in 1921, formed an "advertising
constructors" partnership with the poet Vladimir Mayakovsky, designed *LEF* and *Novyi LEF*
(1923–1928), and the Workers' Club in Melnikov's Soviet pavilion, Paris, 1925.

**Principles.**
- *Image and word as one shout.* Photograph, slogan and type locked into one structure. Example:
  Lengiz *Books (in All Branches of Knowledge)* poster, 1924, with Lilya Brik calling out.
- *Photography as the new drawing.* Example: photomontages for Mayakovsky's *Pro eto*, 1923.
- *The radical viewpoint.* Diagonal, steep up- and down-shots to make the familiar strange.
  Example: *Pioneer with a Trumpet*, photograph, 1930.

**Key works.** *Pro eto* (book, 1923); *LEF* covers (magazine, 1923–1925); Lengiz *Books*
(poster, 1924); Workers' Club (exhibition, 1925).

**Visual vocabulary.** Bold sans caps in triangles and blocks, red/black/photo, megaphone
shapes, oblique crops.

**Lineage.** From Suprematism and Dada montage; into the poster language of the 20th century
(his Lilya Brik image has been re-used on album covers, including Franz Ferdinand's 2005 *You
Could Have It So Much Better*).

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Pair yourself with a writer from day one. The Rodchenko–Mayakovsky ads work
because image and copy were made together.

### Varvara Stepanova {#varvara-stepanova}

**Context.** Russian artist and designer (1894–1958). Constructivist theorist, stage designer
(*The Death of Tarelkin*, 1922), textile designer at the First State Textile Printing Factory
with Lyubov Popova, and from 1924 professor of textile design at Vkhutemas.

**Principles.**
- *Pattern from production.* Geometric prints designed for the machine and the worker. Example:
  more than 150 textile designs, 1924.
- *Clothes as working tools.* Sports and work clothing for function and legibility. Example:
  sportswear designs, 1923.
- *Visual poetry by hand.* Handwritten word-and-image books. Example: *Gaust chaba*, 1919.

**Key works.** *Gaust chaba* (book, 1919); *The Death of Tarelkin* sets and costumes (other,
1922); textile designs (product, 1924).

**Visual vocabulary.** Interlocking circles, stripes and triangles in two or three flat
colours; collage and handwriting in her books.

**Lineage.** From Constructivism; into modern textile and surface pattern, sportswear graphics.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Stepanova is a reminder that the brand system includes what people wear. Design
the pattern so the machine can print it well, not so the sketch looks good.

### Gustav Klutsis {#gustav-klutsis}

**Context.** Latvian-born designer and photographer (1895–1938), student of Malevich, professor
of colour at Vkhutemas 1924–1930, and with his wife Valentina Kulagina a leading author of Soviet
photomontage posters. He was arrested in January 1938 and shot on 26 February 1938 at Butovo.

**Principles.**
- *Photomontage as monumental scale.* Repeated photographed figures become a mass. Example:
  *Let Us Fulfill the Plan of Great Works*, 1930, a wall of raised hands.
- *Montage plus geometry.* Suprematist planes structure the photographs. Example: *Electrification
  of the Entire Country*, 1920.
- *Multimedia street furniture.* Kiosks combining screen, loudspeaker and print. Example: his
  "radio-orator" and agitprop stand designs, 1922.

**Key works.** *Electrification of the Entire Country* (poster, 1920); agitprop kiosks (other,
1922); *Let Us Fulfill the Plan of Great Works* (poster, 1930).

**Visual vocabulary.** Montaged photographs at radically different scales, red diagonals,
condensed sans slogans.

**Lineage.** From Suprematism and Dada; into Soviet propaganda and, through it, every rally
poster since.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Klutsis' kiosks are digital out-of-home a century early. His fate is also the
plainest warning about who owns the designer's voice.

### Stenberg Brothers {#stenberg-brothers}

**Context.** Vladimir (1899–1982) and Georgii (1900–1933) Stenberg, Moscow-born Constructivists.
They co-founded OBMOKhU (Society of Young Artists) in 1919, then from 1923/24 until Georgii's
death in a motorcycle accident in 1933 made around 300 film posters for Sovkino and imported
films. (Duo dates: 1919, OBMOKhU, to 1933.)

**Principles.**
- *Stop the passer-by.* Distortion, inversion and scale to arrest motion. Example: *Man with a
  Movie Camera*, 1929, a woman whose body spins out of a vortex.
- *Hand-drawn photography.* Film stills projected and redrawn, so montage effects print by
  lithography. Example: *Berlin: Symphony of a Great City*, 1928.
- *The rhythm of cinema in a still image.* Repeated, rotating figures suggest a cut. Example:
  *Man with a Movie Camera*, 1929.

**Key works.** *Berlin: Symphony of a Great City* (poster, 1928); *Man with a Movie Camera*
(poster, 1929).

**Visual vocabulary.** Saturated flat lithographic colour, redrawn photographic faces, spirals,
radical foreshortening, bold condensed titles.

**Lineage.** From Constructivism and film editing; into the film poster tradition (Saul Bass,
the Polish school).

**Quotes.** See Constructivism above.

**Remix today.** The Stenbergs' trick of redrawing photographs in a reproducible style is how
you build a consistent illustration system from inconsistent source imagery.

## Bauhaus {#bauhaus}

**Context.** Founded by Walter Gropius in Weimar on 1 April 1919 by merging the art academy and
the school of arts and crafts. Early Bauhaus was craft-guild Expressionist (Itten's preliminary
course, Feininger's *Cathedral* manifesto cover). Pressure from De Stijl and Constructivism,
Moholy-Nagy's arrival in 1923, and that year's slogan "Art and technology, a new unity" turned it
toward industry. The 1923 Bauhaus exhibition in Weimar showed the change. It moved to Dessau in
1925 (Gropius' building opened December 1926), where the printing and advertising workshop under
Herbert Bayer, then Joost Schmidt, developed sans serif, lowercase, photo-led design. Directors:
Gropius 1919–1928, Hannes Meyer 1928–1930, Mies van der Rohe 1930–1933. The Dessau school closed
in 1932, moved to Berlin, and dissolved itself in July 1933 under Nazi pressure. Emigrés carried
it to the USA: Moholy's New Bauhaus, Chicago (1937), Albers at Black Mountain (1933), Gropius at
Harvard (1937), and MoMA's *Bauhaus 1919–1928* exhibition (1938).

**Principles.**
- *Unity of the arts under architecture.* Every discipline serves the building. Example: Gropius'
  Bauhaus manifesto, April 1919.
- *Learn from materials first.* A preliminary course in form, colour and material before
  specialising. Example: Albers' paper-folding exercises in the *Vorkurs*, 1920s.
- *Design for industry.* Prototypes for mass production, not crafts for the few. Example:
  Bauhaus Dessau building and its fittings, 1925–1926.
- *Elementary type and photography.* Sans serif, lowercase, rules and photographs as the modern
  print vocabulary. Example: Bayer, *universal* alphabet, 1925.
- *Typophoto.* Text and image composed as a single visual statement. Example: Moholy-Nagy,
  *Malerei, Photographie, Film*, 1925.

**Key works.** Gropius, Bauhaus manifesto (1919); Joost Schmidt, *Bauhaus Ausstellung* poster
(1923); Moholy-Nagy, *Staatliches Bauhaus in Weimar 1919–1923* catalogue (1923); Bauhausbücher
series (1925–1930); Bayer, *universal* (1925); Bauhaus Dessau building (1926); Bayer, Kandinsky
60th birthday exhibition poster (1926); *bauhaus* magazine (1926–1931).

**Visual vocabulary.** Sans serif, often all lowercase; heavy horizontal and vertical rules;
red and black with photography; asymmetric layouts; circles, squares and triangles in primary
colours (Kandinsky's form–colour questionnaire, 1923); DIN paper sizes.

**Lineage.** From Arts and Crafts, the Deutscher Werkbund, Expressionism, De Stijl and
Constructivism; into the New Typography, Swiss style, the Ulm school, American modernism and
design education worldwide (the foundation course).

**Quotes.** "Architects, sculptors, painters, we all must return to the crafts!" Gropius, Bauhaus
manifesto, 1919. By 1923 Gropius was saying "we want an architecture adapted to our world of
machines, radios and fast cars". The Bauhaus letterhead of the mid-1920s justified its lowercase
policy with "wir schreiben alles klein, denn wir sparen damit zeit" (we write everything in
lowercase because it saves time).

**Remix today.** The Bauhaus idea worth keeping is the school, not the style: a foundation year
where everyone learns material, colour and form before tools. Its visual signs (primaries,
lowercase sans) are now a costume. Use the method.

### Walter Gropius {#walter-gropius}

**Context.** German architect (1883–1969), founder and first director of the Bauhaus
(1919–1928), architect of the Dessau building. He emigrated to England in 1934 and the USA in
1937, teaching at Harvard.

**Principles.**
- *The school as a design.* A curriculum is a designed system. Example: the Bauhaus manifesto
  and programme, 1919.
- *Art and technology, a new unity.* Example: the 1923 Bauhaus exhibition, Weimar.
- *Building as the total work.* Every workshop contributes to one object. Example: Bauhaus
  Dessau, 1925–1926.

**Key works.** Fagus factory (building, 1911); Bauhaus manifesto (manifesto, 1919); Bauhaus
Dessau (building, 1926); *Bauhaus 1919–1928* at MoMA, with Bayer (exhibition, 1938).

**Visual vocabulary.** Glass curtain wall, flat roof, asymmetric plan; in print, sans serif
signage on the building itself.

**Lineage.** From Peter Behrens and the Werkbund; into the International Style and American
design education.

**Quotes.** See Bauhaus above.

**Remix today.** Gropius' best design was an institution. Leading design means designing the
conditions under which others do their best work.

### László Moholy-Nagy {#laszlo-moholy-nagy}

**Context.** Hungarian artist and teacher (1895–1946). Joined the Bauhaus in 1923, taking over
the preliminary course and co-editing the Bauhausbücher with Gropius. He left in 1928; in 1937 he
founded the New Bauhaus in Chicago (later the School of Design / Institute of Design).

**Principles.**
- *Typophoto.* Typography plus photography is the new, most exact visual communication. Example:
  *Malerei, Photographie, Film*, Bauhausbuch 8, 1925.
- *The New Vision.* The camera, microscope and X-ray extend seeing. Example: photograms, from
  1922.
- *Communication first.* Typography must be clear before it is anything else. Example:
  "Die neue Typographie", in *Staatliches Bauhaus in Weimar 1919–1923*, 1923.
- *Design across every medium.* Light, film, stage and print as one practice. Example:
  *Light-Space Modulator*, 1930.

**Key works.** *Staatliches Bauhaus in Weimar 1919–1923* (book, 1923); Bauhausbücher (book,
1925–1930); *Malerei, Photographie, Film* (book, 1925); *Light-Space Modulator* (other, 1930);
*Vision in Motion* (book, 1947).

**Visual vocabulary.** Heavy bars and rules, bold grotesque, photograms, photographs bled and
cropped, transparent overlapping planes.

**Lineage.** From Constructivism and Dada; into the New Typography, American design education
and the Container Corporation's modernism.

**Quotes.** "Typography is a tool of communication. It must be communication in its most intense
form. The emphasis must be on absolute clarity…" "Die neue Typographie", 1923 (English as in
Meggs).

**Remix today.** Moholy treated every new medium as a new kind of seeing. For interfaces and
generative tools, that means asking what the medium can show that print couldn't, rather than
porting a page.

### Herbert Bayer {#herbert-bayer}

**Context.** Austrian-American designer (1900–1985). Bauhaus student from 1921, head of the
Dessau printing and advertising workshop 1925–1928, then art director at Dorland and of *Vogue*'s
Berlin office until he emigrated to the USA in 1938, where he later worked for the Container
Corporation of America and produced the *World Geo-Graphic Atlas* (1953).

**Principles.**
- *One alphabet.* Abolish capitals; a single geometric lowercase is enough. Example: *universal*,
  1925 (drawn, never cast).
- *Hierarchy by weight, size and rule, not decoration.* Example: Kandinsky 60th birthday
  exhibition poster, 1926.
- *Photography as the designer's tool.* Example: cover of *bauhaus* magazine 1928/1, a
  photograph of his own drafting instruments.
- *Design in emergency.* Rapid, functional typographic ephemera. Example: Thuringian state
  emergency banknotes, 1923.

**Key works.** Emergency banknotes (other, 1923); *universal* (typeface, 1925); Kandinsky
exhibition poster (poster, 1926); *bauhaus* 1928/1 cover (magazine, 1928); *World Geo-Graphic
Atlas* (book, 1953).

**Visual vocabulary.** Geometric lowercase sans, heavy rules, red and black, asymmetry,
photomontage, later surreal photo-collage.

**Lineage.** From Bauhaus teaching (Kandinsky, Moholy); into American corporate modernism and
information design.

**Quotes.** See the Bauhaus lowercase letterhead above.

**Remix today.** Bayer's career spans the avant-garde to corporate America; the transferable
skill is keeping a rigorous vocabulary while serving clients.

### Joost Schmidt {#joost-schmidt}

**Context.** German typographer and teacher (1893–1948). Bauhaus student from 1919, taught
lettering 1925–1932 and ran the advertising, printing and graphic design department 1928–1932.

**Principles.**
- *Geometry as composition.* Circles, squares and rules interlocked like machine parts. Example:
  *Staatliche Bauhaus Ausstellung* poster, Weimar, 1923.
- *Teach advertising as a system.* Typography, photography and exhibition stands as one
  curriculum. Example: the Bauhaus Reklame workshop, 1928–1932.

**Key works.** *Bauhaus Ausstellung* poster (poster, 1923); *Offset* magazine Bauhaus issue
cover (magazine, 1926).

**Visual vocabulary.** Interlocking circles and squares, angled sans caps, red/black/grey.

**Lineage.** From Bauhaus teaching; into postwar German design education (he taught in Berlin
after 1945).

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Schmidt's 1923 poster shows the shift from Expressionism to geometry in one
sheet. It's a model for a single artefact that announces an institution's change of direction.

### Josef Albers {#josef-albers}

**Context.** German-American artist and teacher (1888–1976). Bauhaus student from 1920, master
from 1925, taught the preliminary course; emigrated in 1933 to teach at Black Mountain College
(to 1949) and from 1950 led design at Yale.

**Principles.**
- *Colour is relative.* A colour changes with its neighbours, so learn by experiment, not rule.
  Example: *Interaction of Color*, 1963.
- *Economy of material.* Get the most form from the least material and waste. Example: his
  *Vorkurs* paper-folding exercises, Bauhaus, 1920s.
- *Modular letters.* Build an alphabet from a few geometric parts. Example: *Kombinationsschrift*
  stencil typeface, 1926.

**Key works.** *Kombinationsschrift* (typeface, 1926); *Homage to the Square* (other, from 1950);
*Interaction of Color* (book, 1963).

**Visual vocabulary.** Squares, quarter circles and bars; nested flat colour.

**Lineage.** From Bauhaus craft teaching; into American art and design education and Op Art.

**Quotes.** "In visual perception a color is almost never seen as it really is—as it physically
is." *Interaction of Color*, 1963.

**Remix today.** Albers' colour exercises are the right way to build a palette for a design
system: test every colour against its actual neighbours.

## New Typography {#new-typography}

**Context.** Die neue Typographie was the printing trade's adoption of avant-garde form, codified
by Jan Tschichold. Moholy-Nagy used the term in 1923; Tschichold's special issue *elementare
typographie* of the trade journal *Typographische Mitteilungen* (October 1925) put Lissitzky,
Moholy and Schwitters in front of ordinary compositors, and his book *Die neue Typographie*
(1928) turned it into rules. The DIN 476 paper standard (1922), new geometric sans faces (Erbar,
Futura 1927, Kabel), photo-engraving and the *ring neue werbegestalter* (1927–28) gave it
industrial footing. In Germany it was branded "cultural Bolshevism" in 1933; Tschichold and Renner
were arrested or dismissed, and it moved to Switzerland and the Netherlands.

**Principles.**
- *Clarity is the purpose.* Form follows the message's function. Example: Tschichold, *Die neue
  Typographie*, 1928.
- *Asymmetry over the central axis.* Dynamic, functional arrangement instead of the classical
  symmetrical title page. Example: Tschichold, *Die Hose* film poster, Phoebus-Palast, 1927.
- *Sans serif as the face of the age.* Example: Renner, Futura, 1927.
- *Standardisation.* DIN paper sizes, standard letterheads and envelopes. Example: Tschichold's
  DIN letterhead schemes in *Die neue Typographie*, 1928.
- *Photography over illustration; rules and white space as structure.* Example: Piet Zwart, NKF
  cable catalogue, 1928.

**Key works.** Tschichold, *elementare typographie* (1925); Futura (1927); Tschichold, *Die
Hose* (1927); Tschichold, *Die neue Typographie* (1928); Zwart, NKF catalogue (1928); Tschichold,
*Typographische Gestaltung* (1935).

**Visual vocabulary.** Flush-left ragged-right setting, sans serif (Futura, Akzidenz), black
plus one colour (red), heavy bars, generous white, photographs and photomontage, DIN formats,
type at right angles and occasionally diagonal.

**Lineage.** From Constructivism, De Stijl, Dada and the Bauhaus; into Swiss style and Dutch
modernism, and every corporate stationery system.

**Quotes.** "The essence of the New Typography is clarity." Tschichold, *Die neue Typographie*,
1928 (Ruari McLean's English translation, 1995).

**Remix today.** The New Typography is the root of product UI conventions (left alignment,
system fonts, standard sizes). Take its question, "what does this information need?", rather
than its look.

### Jan Tschichold {#jan-tschichold}

**Context.** German typographer (1902–1974), trained in Leipzig in calligraphy, converted to
modernism by the 1923 Bauhaus exhibition. Taught at Renner's Munich master school from 1926;
arrested in March 1933 and emigrated to Basel. In the 1940s he recanted modernist dogma, then
standardised Penguin Books (1947–1949) and designed Sabon (1967).

**Principles.**
- *Codify the avant-garde for the trade.* Make radical form a teachable standard. Example:
  *elementare typographie*, 1925.
- *Functional asymmetry.* Arrange by reading order and importance. Example: *Die neue
  Typographie*, 1928.
- *Rules, then judgment.* The same rigour later applied to classical books. Example: the Penguin
  Composition Rules, 1947.

**Key works.** *elementare typographie* (magazine, 1925); *Die Hose* (poster, 1927); *Die neue
Typographie* (book, 1928); *Typographische Gestaltung* (book, 1935); Penguin Composition Rules
(system, 1947); Sabon (typeface, 1967).

**Visual vocabulary.** Asymmetric sans settings, heavy rules, photographs, black and red; later
centred classical serif.

**Lineage.** From Lissitzky and the Bauhaus; into Swiss style and, through Penguin, modern
publishing identity.

**Quotes.** See New Typography above.

**Remix today.** Tschichold changed his mind in public and was better for it. Treat a design
dogma as a tool for a period, not an identity.

### Paul Renner {#paul-renner}

**Context.** German type designer and educator (1878–1956), a Werkbund member, author of
*Typographie als Kunst* (1922), and from 1926 head of the Munich school that became the
Meisterschule für Deutschlands Buchdrucker. His pamphlet *Kulturbolschewismus?* (1932) attacked
Nazi cultural policy; he was arrested and dismissed in 1933.

**Principles.**
- *Geometry corrected by the eye.* Circle, triangle and square, then adjusted optically so the
  type reads. Example: Futura, Bauer, 1927 (begun 1924; the most radical alternates dropped).
- *Type for the age.* A face as a manifesto. Example: Bauer's launch slogan for Futura, "die
  Schrift unserer Zeit" (the typeface of our time).
- *Teach printing as a design discipline.* Example: the Munich master school, from 1927.

**Key works.** *Typographie als Kunst* (book, 1922); Futura (typeface, 1927); *Kulturbolschewismus?*
(book, 1932); *Die Kunst der Typographie* (book, 1939).

**Visual vocabulary.** Geometric sans with circular bowls, pointed apexes, tall ascenders.

**Lineage.** From the Werkbund; into the geometric sans tradition (Avant Garde, Avenir); Futura
set the Apollo 11 lunar plaque (1969).

**Quotes.** None beyond the foundry slogan above.

**Remix today.** Futura is the proof that "geometric" type is an optical compromise. Draw the
system, then trust the eye over the construction.

### Piet Zwart {#piet-zwart}

**Context.** Dutch designer (1885–1977), trained as an architect, assistant to Jan Wils and then
H. P. Berlage, who introduced him to the Nederlandsche Kabelfabriek (NKF), Delft, in 1923. He made
about 275 designs for NKF in ten years and called himself a "typotekt", typographer plus
architect.

**Principles.**
- *Build with the printer's case.* Compose from stock type, rules and blanks. Example: Vickers
  House advertisements, 1920s.
- *Photography as fact.* Close-up, cropped, overprinted photographs of the product. Example: NKF
  catalogue, 1928.
- *Play in a functional brief.* Example: *Het boek van PTT*, teaching children to use the post
  office, commissioned 1930, published 1938.

**Key works.** Vickers House ads (poster, 1920s); NKF catalogue (book, 1928); *Het boek van PTT*
(book, 1938); Bruynzeel kitchen (product, 1937).

**Visual vocabulary.** Huge contrasting type sizes, overprinted primaries, diagonals, cut-out
photographs, circles, typographic material as image.

**Lineage.** From De Stijl and Dada; into Dutch modernism (Crouwel, Total Design) and the
graphic design of Dutch public institutions.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Zwart made a cable catalogue into an avant-garde object. The dullest
industrial brief is often the one with the most freedom.

## Art Deco {#art-deco}

**Context.** Named retrospectively after the *Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et
industriels modernes*, Paris (29 April–8 November 1925), Art Deco was the commercial, luxurious
modernism of the 1920s and 30s, from Paris to London to New York. In graphic design it absorbed
Cubism, Futurism and Constructivism into glamorous posters for railways, liners, drinks and
department stores. Lithography, the motor car and the poster hoarding seen from moving vehicles
shaped it; the Depression pushed it toward Streamline Moderne.

**Principles.**
- *Geometric stylisation for speed of reading.* Subjects reduced to a few planes and a single
  vanishing point. Example: Cassandre, *Étoile du Nord*, 1927.
- *Lettering integrated with image.* Custom display type built from the same geometry as the
  picture. Example: Cassandre, Bifur typeface, 1929.
- *Monumentality through scale.* Low viewpoint, huge object, tiny human. Example: Cassandre,
  *Normandie*, 1935.
- *Serial storytelling.* Images in sequence for viewers in motion. Example: Cassandre, *Dubo,
  Dubon, Dubonnet*, 1932.
- *Avant-garde forms for mass commerce.* Example: Kauffer, *Soaring to Success! Daily Herald –
  the Early Bird*, 1918–1919.

**Key works.** Cassandre, *Au Bûcheron* (1923; grand prize, 1925 Exposition); Cassandre, *Étoile
du Nord* and *Nord Express* (1927); Bifur (1929); Kauffer, *Power, the Nerve Centre of London's
Underground* (1930); *Dubo, Dubon, Dubonnet* (1932); *Normandie* (1935); Peignot (1937).

**Visual vocabulary.** Airbrushed gradients, converging perspective lines, stepped and
zig-zag forms, chrome and machine imagery, geometric capitals (Bifur, Broadway, Peignot), metallic
and deep saturated colour, bold symmetry.

**Lineage.** From Art Nouveau, Vienna Secession, Plakatstil, Cubism and Futurism; into American
modernism, Streamline Moderne and later revivalist branding.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Art Deco shows how to make modernism desirable rather than dutiful. Gradients,
perspective and custom display type are back in brand work; the Deco discipline is to build all
of them from one geometry.

### A.M. Cassandre {#am-cassandre}

**Context.** Pseudonym of Adolphe Jean-Marie Mouron (1901–1968), French poster artist and type
designer, born in Kharkiv. Co-founded the agency Alliance Graphique (1930). Designed faces for
Deberny & Peignot, covers for *Harper's Bazaar* after his 1936 MoMA show, and the Yves Saint
Laurent monogram (1961).

**Principles.**
- *The poster as a telegram.* One simple image read at speed from a car or train. Example:
  *Étoile du Nord*, 1927, rails converging on a star.
- *Image and type from one geometry.* Example: Bifur, 1929.
- *Serial motion.* Example: *Dubo, Dubon, Dubonnet*, 1932, the name filling as the man drinks.
- *Monumental viewpoint.* Example: *Normandie*, 1935.

**Key works.** *Au Bûcheron* (poster, 1923); *Étoile du Nord* (poster, 1927); *Nord Express*
(poster, 1927); Bifur (typeface, 1929); *Dubonnet* (poster, 1932); *Normandie* (poster, 1935);
Peignot (typeface, 1937); Yves Saint Laurent monogram (identity, 1961).

**Visual vocabulary.** Airbrushed tonal gradation, vanishing-point compositions, geometric caps
built into the image.

**Lineage.** From Cubism and Purism; into American advertising and luxury identity.

**Quotes.** None included (his often-cited comparison of the poster designer to a telegraph
operator is paraphrased in the literature in several forms; check a primary source before
quoting).

**Remix today.** Cassandre designed for a viewer moving at speed. Think of the scroll and the
billboard the same way: one image, one reading, and the type in the image.

### Edward McKnight Kauffer {#edward-mcknight-kauffer}

**Context.** American designer (1890–1954), born in Great Falls, Montana, working in Britain from
1914 to 1940. He made about 140 posters for London Underground and London Transport under Frank
Pick, plus work for Shell and the Great Western Railway. MoMA gave him a solo show in 1937.

**Principles.**
- *Avant-garde form for a public client.* Vorticist abstraction sold newspapers and travel.
  Example: *Soaring to Success! Daily Herald – the Early Bird*, 1918–1919.
- *Many styles, one client.* A consistent patron, varied idiom. Example: Underground posters,
  1915–1940.
- *Abstract symbol for an abstract service.* Example: *Power, the Nerve Centre of London's
  Underground*, 1930.

**Key works.** *Soaring to Success!* (poster, 1919); *Winter Sales Are Best Reached By
Underground* (poster, 1922); *Power, the Nerve Centre of London's Underground* (poster, 1930).

**Visual vocabulary.** Flat, cubist planes, birds and machines, Johnston sans for the
Underground, strong silhouette.

**Lineage.** From Vorticism and Japanese prints; into British modernist advertising and
American modernism.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Kauffer shows a patron can hold an identity together while the art changes
every season; the system was the client (Pick, Johnston's type, the roundel), not the style.

## Isotype {#isotype}

**Context.** The Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics was developed from 1925 at Otto Neurath's
Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in Vienna to explain social policy to a working public. A
team (Neurath directing, Marie Reidemeister as "transformer", Gerd Arntz as artist from 1928/29)
produced charts for touring exhibitions and the atlas *Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft* (1930). After
the Austrian civil war of 1934 the team moved to The Hague; the method was renamed Isotype
(International System of Typographic Picture Education) around 1935, and after 1940 the Isotype
Institute continued in Oxford until 1971.

**Principles.**
- *Repetition, not scale.* A greater quantity is shown by more symbols, never a bigger symbol.
  Example: *Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft*, 1930.
- *Transformation.* Analyse the data, decide what to say, then design the chart. Example: Marie
  Neurath's charts for the Vienna museum, from 1925.
- *Standardised pictograms.* One reduced, consistent symbol per concept, combinable with others.
  Example: Gerd Arntz's pictogram library, about 4,000 signs.
- *Picture language for everyone.* Charts that cross language and literacy. Example: Neurath,
  *International Picture Language*, 1936.

**Key works.** *Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft* (1930); *International Picture Language* (1936);
*Basic by Isotype* (1937); *Modern Man in the Making* (1939).

**Visual vocabulary.** Flat silhouetted pictograms in rows, a few flat colours, Futura captions,
counted units, no perspective, no decoration.

**Lineage.** From Constructivist woodcut (Arntz) and the New Typography; into airport and
Olympic signage (Otl Aicher), infographics, emoji and data visualisation.

**Quotes.** "Words divide, pictures unite": Neurath's own coinage, displayed on his office wall
in Vienna. "To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures."
Neurath, *International Picture Language*, 1936.

**Remix today.** Isotype is the most practical movement in this slice. Its rules (count, don't
scale; one symbol per concept; transform before you draw) are a spec for data visualisation and
icon systems now.

### Otto Neurath {#otto-neurath}

**Context.** Austrian philosopher, economist and sociologist (1882–1945), member of the Vienna
Circle, founder of the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum (1925). In Moscow in 1934 when the
Austrian civil war broke out, he went to The Hague, then to England in 1940.

**Principles.**
- *Visual education as democracy.* Data belongs to the people it describes. Example: the Vienna
  museum's exhibitions, from 1925.
- *A grammar for pictures.* Rules for combining signs, like a language. Example: *International
  Picture Language*, 1936.

**Key works.** *Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft* (book, 1930); *International Picture Language*
(book, 1936); *Modern Man in the Making* (book, 1939).

**Visual vocabulary.** Isotype rows of silhouettes.

**Lineage.** From logical positivism and adult education; into information design.

**Quotes.** See Isotype above.

**Remix today.** Neurath designed a method so others could do the drawing. That's the job of a
design system lead.

### Marie Neurath {#marie-neurath}

**Context.** German designer and author (1898–1986), born Marie Reidemeister in Braunschweig;
trained in mathematics and physics at Göttingen. Neurath's principal "transformer" from 1925; she
named the method Isotype, married Neurath in 1941, ran the Isotype Institute after his death and
gave its archive to the University of Reading in 1971.

**Principles.**
- *The transformer.* A role between expert and artist that decides what a chart should say.
  Example: charts for *Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft*, 1930.
- *Information design for children and new citizens.* Example: the Isotype Institute's
  children's books and its work in West Africa (Nigeria), 1950s.

**Key works.** *Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft* (book, 1930); Isotype Institute children's books
(book, 1940s–1970s).

**Visual vocabulary.** Isotype rows, explanatory sequences.

**Lineage.** From Neurath's museum; into educational publishing and the "content designer" role.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Transformation is the most under-credited job in design. Marie Neurath shows
that deciding what the data means comes before drawing it, and is a role in its own right.

### Gerd Arntz {#gerd-arntz}

**Context.** German artist (1900–1988), a council-communist woodcut maker with the Cologne
Progressives. Neurath invited him in 1926; he joined the Vienna museum full-time in 1928 or 1929
(sources disagree), followed the team to The Hague in 1934 and stayed in the Netherlands,
working for the Dutch Foundation for Statistics.

**Principles.**
- *Reduce to a type.* Each pictogram is the type of a thing, not a portrait of one. Example: his
  Isotype pictograms, about 4,000, from 1928.
- *Woodcut economy.* Silhouette and minimal interior detail, cut for reproduction. Example:
  political woodcuts, 1920s.

**Key works.** Political woodcuts (other, 1920s); pictograms for *Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft*
(system, 1930).

**Visual vocabulary.** Solid silhouettes, consistent proportion, details only where they
distinguish.

**Lineage.** From the Cologne Progressives' constructivist figuration; into pictogram systems
(Aicher, AIGA DOT signs) and icon design.

**Quotes.** None included.

**Remix today.** Arntz's pictograms are a lesson in icon consistency: one proportion system,
detail only where it disambiguates.

## Turning points

- **1905** Die Brücke founded, Dresden.
- **1909** Marinetti's *Manifesto of Futurism* (*Gazzetta dell'Emilia* 5 February; *Le Figaro*
  20 February).
- **1914** *Zang Tumb Tuuum* published; *BLAST* 1 published (2 July).
- **1915** *0,10*, Petrograd: *Black Square* shown.
- **1916** Cabaret Voltaire opens, Zurich (5 February); Edward Johnston's Underground alphabet.
- **1917** *De Stijl* magazine founded; October Revolution.
- **1919** Bauhaus founded, Weimar (1 April).
- **1920** *First International Dada Fair*, Berlin.
- **1921** First Working Group of Constructivists, INKhUK, Moscow.
- **1922** DIN 476 paper sizes; *First Russian Art Exhibition*, Galerie van Diemen, Berlin.
- **1923** Bauhaus exhibition, Weimar; Lissitzky's "Topography of Typography" and *Dlia golosa*.
- **1925** Bauhaus moves to Dessau; Paris *Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs*;
  *elementare typographie*; Vienna museum founded.
- **1927** Futura released by Bauer.
- **1928** *Die neue Typographie*; *Pressa*, Cologne.
- **1929** *Film und Foto*, Stuttgart.
- **1932** Soviet decree of 23 April dissolves independent artistic groups.
- **1933** Bauhaus closes in Berlin; Beck's diagrammatic London Underground map issued (January).
- **1937** *Degenerate Art*, Munich; New Bauhaus, Chicago.
- **1938** *Bauhaus 1919–1928*, MoMA.

## Disputed and approximate dates

- Malevich's birth year: 1879 (current consensus, 23 February N.S.); older sources give 1878.
  He also backdated some Suprematist works, linking the Black Square to 1913.
- Futurist manifesto: first printed 5 February 1909 in Bologna's *Gazzetta dell'Emilia*;
  20 February 1909 (*Le Figaro*) is the conventional date.
- *Zang Tumb Tuuum*: excerpts from 1912, book 1914; some catalogues date it 1912–1914.
- *Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge*: 1919 (Wikipedia) vs 1920 in some museum records.
- *About Two Squares*: designed 1920, published 1922.
- Constructivism's start: 1915 (Tatlin's counter-reliefs) vs 1921 (First Working Group).
- Bauhaus end: Dessau closed 1932; Berlin school searched and sealed April 1933, self-dissolved
  July 1933.
- Gerd Arntz joined the Vienna museum in 1928 (Marie Neurath accounts) or at the start of 1929.
- Cassandre's Acier Noir: dated 1930, 1935 and 1936 in different sources. *Au Bûcheron* is
  1923; its prize was at the 1925 Exposition.
- Kauffer's *Soaring to Success!*: V&A dates it 1918–1919.
- Beck's map: drawn 1931, first issued as a pocket map in January 1933.
- Stenberg brothers: posters from 1923 or 1924 depending on source; OBMOKhU founding 1919 is used
  as the duo's start.
- Isotype as a name: "around 1935".
