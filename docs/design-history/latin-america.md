# Latin America, 1937–2026

Latin American graphic design is usually told as a footnote to Europe: Max Bill visits São Paulo,
Ulm graduates go home, Swiss order arrives in the tropics. That story is half true and wholly
misleading. The region's designers didn't just import modernism. They used it for problems Europe
didn't have: a capital city built from nothing, a revolution that abolished advertising, a metro
for passengers who couldn't read station names, a mural-painting brigade, a cybernetic economy.
Several of the ideas this slice traces went the other way, too. Concrete poetry was named in
São Paulo. The most widely reproduced political poster of the 1960s was printed in Havana. Tomás
Maldonado, a Buenos Aires painter, wrote much of the theory behind the Ulm model.

This slice covers nine movements from 1937 to the present, in roughly chronological order of
their start dates. Each figure sits under the movement where their most defining work happened.

Two names the brief asked for appear only in the prose, not as figure entries: **Elena Serrano**,
who designed OSPAAAL's *Day of the Heroic Guerrilla* poster (1968), and **Vicente Larrea**, the
Chilean designer of Nueva Canción record covers. Their work is well documented, but no reliable
source available for this research gives their birth years. (The Wikidata record for "Vicente
Larrea" belongs to a Bilbao sculptor born in 1934.) Both are discussed under their movements, and
their works appear in the movement key-works lists without a figure link.

## Taller de Gráfica Popular {#taller-grafica-popular}

**Context.** The Taller de Gráfica Popular (TGP) was founded in Mexico City in 1937 by Leopoldo
Méndez, Pablo O'Higgins and Luis Arenal, after the Liga de Escritores y Artistas Revolucionarios
(LEAR) dissolved. It began as the Taller Editorial de Gráfica Popular. The country had already
spent two decades turning the Revolution into public art through the muralists; the TGP took the
same purpose into print, where it could be cheap, fast and portable. Under Lázaro Cárdenas its
work backed government policy, including the 1938 oil nationalisation, and it fought fascism
through the war years. The enabling technology was the oldest one available: linoleum and wood
blocks, hand-cut and printed on a press any union hall could reach. Foreign artists came to work
there, among them Mariana Yampolsky (its first female member, from 1945) and Elizabeth Catlett
(1946–1966), and the Taller became a model for politically committed print collectives in the
United States and elsewhere. It declined from the 1960s amid political splits, though it still
exists in Mexico City's Colonia Doctores.

**Principles.**

1. **The cheapest block wins.** Lino and wood blocks cut quickly, print on any paper, and need no
   commercial printer, so a message can go from a meeting to the street in days. *Example:* the
   broadsides and handbills of the Cárdenas years, from 1937.
2. **Collective authorship.** Prints were often made together, and the Taller deliberately didn't
   number its editions, refusing the economy of the collectible print. It still sold work under
   its La Estampa Mexicana imprint. *Example:* La Estampa Mexicana portfolios.
3. **The vernacular as the source.** The model was the popular broadside of José Guadalupe Posada
   and Manuel Manilla, not academic art, and the Taller revived the *calavera*, the rhymed
   skeleton caricature of politicians and public figures. *Example:* TGP calaveras of the 1940s.
4. **Narrative in black and white.** Dense gouged blacks, figures massed into crowds and a single
   driving diagonal tell a whole political story in one image, without colour. *Example:* Méndez,
   *En el nombre de Cristo*, a series of seven prints on the murder of rural teachers, 1942.

**Key works.** Leopoldo Méndez, *En el nombre de Cristo*, 1942. Méndez, illustrations for Juan de
la Cabada's *Incidentes melódicos del mundo irracional*, 1944. The Taller's calaveras and its
anti-fascist and labour posters, 1937–1950s.

**Visual vocabulary.** Relief print, almost always black ink on cheap off-white or newsprint
stock. Heavy gouged blacks with white line-work cut out of them, never a smooth tone. Crowds of
figures used as a single mass; exaggerated hands and faces; skeletons as satire. Lettering cut
into the block or set in plain wood type below the image. The format is the broadside, a sheet
meant for a wall.

**Lineage.** It came from Posada's turn-of-the-century broadsides, the muralist movement's public
mission, and the Stridentist magazines of the 1920s, where Méndez started. It fed straight into
later political print collectives, including the Oaxacan groups ASARO and Lapiztola, and its
raised fist became a durable symbol of resistance. The Cuban poster took the same public purpose
into colour and silkscreen.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** The TGP shows that a constraint you choose is an aesthetic, not a compromise.
One colour, one cut, no edition numbers, a shared studio name instead of a signature. For a team
making campaign or community material in 2026, borrowing the method matters more than the
woodcut look: a fixed, cheap production technique everyone can use, a shared visual grammar,
and the decision to put the cause above the author.

### Leopoldo Méndez {#leopoldo-mendez}

*Mexico City, 1902 – Mexico City, 1969.* Printmaker and co-founder of the TGP.

**Context.** Méndez trained at the Academia de San Carlos and the open-air painting school at
Chimalistac. In the 1920s he was part of Stridentism, working on its magazines *Irradiador* and
*Horizonte* and living in Xalapa from 1925 to 1927. He taught in the Cultural Missions programme
from 1929, was a founder of LEAR in 1933, and left it to found the TGP in 1937. He received a
Guggenheim Fellowship in 1939. Mexican tradition treats him as Posada's successor.

**Principles.**

1. **Anonymity in the service of the message.** He kept his own credit small and worked through
   collectives. That choice is part of why he's less famous than the muralists. *Example:*
   collaborative TGP broadsides from 1937.
2. **The print as cinema.** After 1950, as political prints lost their market, he cut engravings
   for Mexican films, starting with Emilio Fernández's *Río Escondido*, then *Pueblerina* (1948),
   *Un día en la vida* (1949) and *La rosa blanca* (1959). *Example:* the *Río Escondido* series.
3. **Posada's line, modern politics.** He kept the broadside's directness but aimed it at fascism
   and at violence against teachers, not just at local villains. *Example:* *En el nombre de
   Cristo*, 1942.

**Key works.** *En el nombre de Cristo*, 1942. Illustrations for *Incidentes melódicos del mundo
irracional*, 1944, which won a local prize. Engraved mural dedicated to Posada at the Talleres
Gráficos de la Nación, 1956 (since destroyed).

**Visual vocabulary.** Linocut and wood engraving; dramatic chiaroscuro; crowded compositions; a
cinematographer's sense of low angle and strong light. In 1946 he won Mexico's first Premio
Nacional de Grabado.

**Lineage.** From Posada and Stridentism to the TGP and Mexican cinema's graphic material. In
1971 David Alfaro Siqueiros put his portrait among Mexico's great artists on the Polyforum
Cultural Siqueiros.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Méndez treated a still image as a frame in a sequence. If you're designing
key art for film or series, the lesson is to compose like a cinematographer — light, angle, a
crowd as one mass — rather than as a collage of the cast.

## Argentine Modernism {#argentine-modernism}

**Context.** In Buenos Aires in the mid-1940s, a group of young painters around the magazine
*Arturo* (1944) split into rival concrete movements. One was the Asociación Arte
Concreto-Invención, founded in 1945 by Tomás Maldonado, Lidy Prati, Alfredo Hlito, Enio Iommi,
Manuel Espinosa and others. They issued the *Manifiesto Invencionista* in 1946. The other was
Madí. These were some of the earliest concrete art groups anywhere, and they came out of a
peripheral city reading Mondrian, Van Doesburg and the Russians from a distance.

In 1951 Maldonado and Hlito founded *Nueva Visión*, a magazine that ran until 1957 and put art,
architecture, industrial design and typography side by side. Meanwhile, at the University of
Buenos Aires architecture faculty, a generation of teachers brought in Bauhaus rationalism.

The second phase was corporate and cultural. The Siam Di Tella industrial group set up its own
advertising and design agency, Agens, where young designers including Roberto Alvarado,
Guillermo González Ruiz, América Sánchez and Ronald Shakespear trained. In 1958 the Di Tella
family founded the Instituto Di Tella, which became the "temple of the avant-gardes" of the
1960s. Its graphic design department, formalised in 1963 under Juan Carlos Distéfano, is
described in the institute's history as the first place in Argentina where graphic design was
practised professionally. The Onganía dictatorship's hostility, together with financial
problems, closed the art centres in 1970. The movement's last great statement was public: the
Plan Visual de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires (1971–72), a citywide signage system. The 1976 coup ends
the period.

**Principles.**

1. **Invent, don't represent.** For the invencionistas a work isn't an image of something else
   but a concrete object of plane, colour and line. They often cut the frame itself into
   irregular shapes so the painting couldn't read as a window. *Example:* the *Manifiesto
   Invencionista*, 1946.
2. **Theory travels in print.** A modest magazine that puts architecture, industrial design and
   typography next to painting can spread a whole design culture across a continent. *Example:*
   *Nueva Visión*, 1951–1957, and Maldonado's monograph *Max Bill* (1955).
3. **One house, one voice.** An institution's posters, programmes and catalogues share one
   identity, produced in-house by a small team. The Di Tella team mixed Swiss order with the
   idea-led wit of New York advertising. *Example:* Di Tella posters and catalogues, 1963–1970.
4. **Design the street.** Street names, signs and public information form one system, designed as
   public infrastructure rather than as separate objects. *Example:* González Ruiz and
   Shakespear, Plan Visual de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires, 1971–1972.

**Key works.** *Manifiesto Invencionista*, 1946. *Nueva Visión*, 1951–1957. Di Tella graphic
identity and posters, from 1963. Shakespear's *Hamlet* poster, 1964. The Plan Visual de Buenos
Aires, 1971–1972. Ronald and Raúl Shakespear's signage for the municipal hospitals, 1978.

**Visual vocabulary.** In the 1940s: flat unmodulated colour, hard-edged geometric planes, and
irregular *marco recortado* (cut-out frame) supports. In the 1960s Di Tella work: grotesque
sans-serifs on asymmetric layouts, strong photographic crops, playful conceptual images in the
New York manner, and pop colour around the Di Tella's own artists. In the signage: a disciplined
sans-serif, colour coding by function, and pictograms for urban services.

**Lineage.** It came from Constructivism, De Stijl and the Bauhaus, received as texts and
reproductions rather than in person. Through Maldonado it fed directly into the Ulm school, and
through the Di Tella and Agens graduates it fed the Argentine professional design culture that
the University of Buenos Aires formalised in 1985 with its graphic design degree. It also
underpins the region's later type scene (see [Latin American Type Design](#latin-american-type)).

**Quotes.** On the Plan Visual, Rubén Fontana: «Fue el más completo realizado en tal sentido y
significó el posicionamiento definitivo de la cultura del diseño en la vía pública» ("It was the
most complete ever done of its kind, and it definitively established design culture on the
public street"), quoted in the Spanish Wikipedia article on Guillermo González Ruiz.

**Remix today.** The Argentines showed that a design culture can be bootstrapped from the
periphery through three things you can still build: a magazine that argues, a patron that keeps
an in-house studio, and a public commission large enough to make design visible on the street.
The cut-out frame is a live idea too. When the container's shape breaks the rectangle, the
viewer reads the work as an object rather than a picture. That's useful anywhere a screen or
card wants to feel physical.

### Tomás Maldonado {#tomas-maldonado}

*Buenos Aires, 1922 – Milan, 2018.* Painter, designer, theorist, educator.

**Context.** Maldonado trained at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredón. He
co-founded Arte Concreto-Invención in 1945 and *Nueva Visión* in 1951. In 1954 he went to the
Hochschule für Gestaltung in Ulm, where he taught until the mid-1960s and served as rector and
prorector. Sources give his Ulm years as 1954–1966 or 1954–1967. He was the main architect of the
"Ulm model", which pushed design teaching away from Bauhaus art-craft and towards systems
thinking, semiotics and method. Later he taught at Princeton (1967–1970) and in Bologna and Milan.

**Principles.**

1. **Design as science-informed method.** Design has to balance science and making, using
   planning methods, perception theory and semiotics, not artistic intuition alone. *Example:*
   "Ulm, Science and Design", 1964.
2. **Systems over objects.** A company's codes and sign systems matter more than any one handsome
   artefact. *Example:* code systems for Olivetti and the department store La Rinascente, with
   Gui Bonsiepe, 1964–1967.
3. **Design as environmental responsibility.** Design has to answer to ecology and society, not
   only to industry. *Example:* *La speranza progettuale*, 1970 (in English, *Design, Nature,
   and Revolution*, 1972).

**Key works.** *Manifiesto Invencionista* (co-signatory), 1946. *Nueva Visión*, 1951–1957. *Max
Bill*, 1955. *La speranza progettuale*, 1970.

**Visual vocabulary.** As a painter: hard-edged concrete compositions in flat colour. As a
designer and teacher: rationalist sans-serif typography, grid systems, and sign codes.

**Lineage.** He took Buenos Aires concretism to Ulm (see `ulm-school` in the Swiss/International
slice). Ulm then returned to Latin America through Wollner, Bergmiller and Bonsiepe, by way of
ESDI in Rio and Cybersyn in Chile.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Maldonado's *La speranza progettuale* argued in 1970 that design is
environmental policy. In 2026, when design teams own systems that touch millions of people, his
move from "the object" to "the code that generates objects" is the job description.

### Juan Carlos Distéfano {#juan-carlos-distefano}

*Villa Celina, 1933 – 25 July 2026.* Graphic designer, sculptor, painter.

**Context.** Distéfano trained as a graphic designer at the Escuela Nacional de Artes Gráficas and
then at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Manuel Belgrano. He worked in printing houses and
agencies from 1951. In 1960 he won first prize in a poster competition for Siam Di Tella's
fiftieth anniversary. That led to work for the Di Tella companies and to organising the
institute's graphic area, which became a formal department in 1963. His team included Rubén
Fontana, Juan Andralis, Humberto Rivas, Roberto Alvarado, Norberto Coppola, Carlos Soler and
Juan Carlos Indart. From 1969 he spent a year in Rome on a fellowship, and the department closed
in 1970. He then became one of Argentina's most important figurative sculptors, whose work deals
with pain and political violence. He represented Argentina at the 56th Venice Biennale in 2015.

**Principles.**

1. **A horizontal studio.** He later described the Di Tella group as a horizontal organisation
   with no rigid division between those who directed and those who executed. *Example:* the Di
   Tella Departamento de Diseño Gráfico, 1963–1970.
2. **Swiss structure, New York wit.** He brought Swiss design and New York School advertising
   together into one institutional voice. *Example:* Di Tella exhibition posters and catalogues
   of the 1960s.
3. **Make it by hand, fast and cheap.** Small budgets and short deadlines were worked with
   directly. The material was made by hand, not hidden behind a production process. *Example:*
   Di Tella theatre and concert programmes.

**Key works.** Prize-winning Siam Di Tella anniversary poster, 1960. Instituto Di Tella graphic
identity, from 1963.

**Visual vocabulary.** Sans-serif typography on a clear structure; a single strong idea per piece;
photography and drawing used as concepts rather than illustration.

**Lineage.** From Swiss modernism and the New York School to the Argentine design profession. His
team (Fontana, Alvarado and others) became its first generation.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** The Di Tella department is a model for an in-house team: one identity across
many very different programmes, made fast by a flat team. The lesson is that the system lets you
move fast without losing a recognisable voice.

### Rubén Fontana {#ruben-fontana}

*Born 1942.* Graphic designer, typographer, editor, educator.

**Context.** Fontana was a member of the Di Tella graphic department, and ran it with Carlos Soler
in 1969–1970 while Distéfano was in Rome. He went on to become the central figure of Argentine
typographic culture. He founded the magazine *tipoGráfica* (tpG) in Buenos Aires, generally dated
to 1987; that date comes from secondary knowledge and wasn't independently confirmed for this
slice. He also designed the typeface family Fontana ND, published by Neufville Digital as part of
its Bauer Types modern collection.

**Principles.**

1. **Typography is culture, not styling.** A magazine and a teaching culture can make typography
   a shared intellectual discipline for a whole region. *tpG* published international writers
   alongside Latin Americans. *Example:* *tipoGráfica*, from 1987.
2. **A Latin American text face.** Text type can be designed in the region for international
   distribution. *Example:* Fontana ND.

**Key works.** Co-direction of the Di Tella department, 1969–1970. *tipoGráfica* magazine, 1987.
Fontana ND typeface family (Neufville Digital).

**Visual vocabulary.** Editorial typography with classical proportions and modernist structure.

**Lineage.** From the Di Tella to the region's type scene. His Plan Visual quote above is one of
the standard assessments of that project.

**Quotes.** See the Argentine Modernism section above.

**Remix today.** Fontana shows how much leverage there is in publishing about your own discipline.
A team that writes publicly about its type and system decisions builds a culture around them that
outlasts any single product.

### Guillermo González Ruiz {#guillermo-gonzalez-ruiz}

*Chascomús, born 1937.* Architect, graphic designer, educator. Some sources give 1938; Spanish
Wikipedia and Wikidata give 18 March 1937.

**Context.** He studied architecture at the University of Buenos Aires, graduating in 1965, and
worked for advertising agencies from 1961 to 1966, passing through Agens. In 1968 he joined the
studio González Ruiz, Méndez Mosquera y Ronald Shakespear, and was Shakespear's partner until
1973. In 1971–72 he directed the design group behind the Buenos Aires signage system. From 1985
to 1990 he directed the graphic design degree at the University of Buenos Aires (FADU), where he
taught until 2011.

**Principles.**

1. **Signage is architecture.** Wayfinding is designed with an architect's sense of space,
   sightlines and city scale. *Example:* Plan Visual de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires, 1971–1972.
2. **Teach the profession into existence.** A public university degree is how a country makes
   design a recognised discipline. *Example:* director of graphic design at FADU, 1985–1990.

**Key works.** Plan Visual de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires (with Shakespear), 1971–1972. Signage
consultancy for major architecture studios, from 1974.

**Visual vocabulary.** Systematic sans-serif signs, modular panels, and colour coding by use.

**Lineage.** From UBA's Bauhaus-influenced architecture school and Agens to Argentine urban
signage and design education.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Treat wayfinding, and by extension any navigation system, as architecture. Start
from the space and the person moving through it, not from the sign as an object.

### Ronald Shakespear {#ronald-shakespear}

*Rosario, born 1941.* Graphic designer, wayfinding and identity designer.

**Context.** Shakespear came through Agens and then partnered with González Ruiz. His studio,
Diseño Shakespear, later run with his children Lorenzo, Juan and Bárbara, became the leading
wayfinding practice in Argentina. Its work spans the Buenos Aires signage plan (1971–72), the
municipal hospitals (1978, with Raúl Shakespear), the Subte (1995–2008, with Lorenzo and Juan)
and more than a thousand identities, including Banco Galicia, Boca Juniors and Newell's Old Boys.
In 2008 he became the first Latin American to receive the SEGD Fellow Honour Award.

**Principles.**

1. **The city as a client.** Signage for streets, hospitals and the subway is the most public
   graphic design there is. *Example:* municipal hospitals signage, 1978.
2. **A family studio across generations.** Passing projects to the next generation keeps a
   system language consistent across decades. *Example:* the Subte, 1995–2008.

**Key works.** *Hamlet* poster, 1964, now in the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires. Plan
Visual de Buenos Aires, 1971–1972. Municipal hospitals signage, 1978. Subte signage, 1995–2008.

**Visual vocabulary.** Bold pictograms, strong colour coding, large-format sans-serif lettering,
and identities built on a single graphic idea.

**Lineage.** From Agens and the Di Tella era to contemporary Latin American wayfinding.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Shakespear's hospital and subway systems show wayfinding as care. For anyone
designing for stressed users, in a hospital, a transit app or an emergency flow, the
visual language is part of the service, not a layer on top of it.

## Brazilian Concretism {#brazilian-concretism}

**Context.** After the Second World War, Brazil's industrial production overtook agriculture for
the first time. Juscelino Kubitschek's Plano de Metas, from 1956, promised "fifty years in five".
São Paulo got new museums: the Museu de Arte de São Paulo (MASP), whose Instituto de Arte
Contemporânea ran Latin America's first industrial design course from 1951, and the Museu de Arte
Moderna. Then came the 1951 Max Bill retrospective at MASP and the first São Paulo Bienal, where
Bill's work won a prize. Antonio Maluf's poster for that Bienal (1951) is counted as a landmark of
Brazilian graphic design.

In 1952 Waldemar Cordeiro's Grupo Ruptura opened its exhibition at MAM São Paulo with a manifesto
calling for geometric research and for bringing art and industry together. Its members included
Geraldo de Barros, Luiz Sacilotto and Lothar Charoux. In the same year Augusto and Haroldo de
Campos and Décio Pignatari formed the Noigandres group of poets. The National Exhibition of
Concrete Art (São Paulo 1956, Rio 1957) joined the poets and painters. In 1959 the Rio artists
around Ferreira Gullar, Lygia Clark, Hélio Oiticica and Lygia Pape broke away with the
*Neoconcrete Manifesto*, wanting a more bodily, less mathematical art.

Design became a profession in 1958, when Geraldo de Barros, Alexandre Wollner, Ruben Martins and
Walter Macedo founded Forminform in São Paulo, one of the country's first design offices. It
became a university discipline in 1963, when the Escola Superior de Desenho Industrial (ESDI)
opened in Rio on the Ulm model. Brasília, inaugurated on 21 April 1960, made modernism the
nation's official image. Through the 1960s and 70s this rational, constructed language became
Brazil's corporate and civic identity, carried by banks, broadcasters, utilities and the currency.

**Principles.**

1. **The word is a thing.** A poem is a visual-verbal object built from the space of the page.
   Syntax is replaced by spatial arrangement, so typography carries the meaning. *Example:* the
   *Plano-piloto para poesia concreta*, *Noigandres* 4, 1958.
2. **Art meets industry.** Geometric art is a method for industrial society, not decoration. The
   same artists started design offices and furniture cooperatives. *Example:* Geraldo de Barros,
   Unilabor, 1954–1967.
3. **The sign without the name.** Identity rests on a simple constructed symbol strong enough
   that the company name can be dropped. *Example:* Forminform's Sardinhas Coqueiro mark, a palm
   built from semicircles, with a fish made of a triangle and a lozenge on the packaging, 1958.
4. **Teach it as a discipline.** Design becomes a university subject with method, theory and
   links to industry, on the Ulm model. *Example:* ESDI, Rio de Janeiro, opened 1963.
5. **The nation as client.** Modernist identity is applied to the state itself: anniversaries,
   broadcasters and the currency. *Example:* Aloísio Magalhães's winning cruzeiro novo banknote
   design, 1966.

**Key works.** Geraldo de Barros, *Fotoformas*, MASP, 1950. Antonio Maluf, first Bienal poster,
1951. *Ruptura* exhibition and manifesto, 1952. Augusto de Campos, *Poetamenos*, published 1955.
Décio Pignatari, "beba coca cola", 1957. *Plano-piloto para poesia concreta*, 1958. Forminform,
Sardinhas Coqueiro identity, 1958. Magalhães, IV Centenário do Rio symbol, 1964. Wollner, Banco
Itaú mark, adopted 1973.

**Visual vocabulary.** Geometric sans-serif type in lowercase, with no punctuation. Words broken
into syllables and spaced across the page so that reading becomes looking. Colour used
structurally (each colour a voice, in *Poetamenos*). Marks built from circles, semicircles,
triangles and squares on visible construction grids. Strict modular layouts; black and a single
strong colour. In photography, scratched and multiply-exposed negatives that turn buildings into
geometry.

**Lineage.** From Constructivism, De Stijl and the Bauhaus, through Max Bill and the Ulm school.
It ran in parallel with Eugen Gomringer in Switzerland: the two strands of concrete poetry met in
1955. It fed into Tropicália (whose designers knew its rules well enough to break them), into the
international concrete poetry movement, and into the identity practice of Brazilian design to
this day.

**Quotes.** The 1961 postscript to the *Plano-piloto* adopts Mayakovsky's line: "sem forma
revolucionária não há arte revolucionária" ("without revolutionary form there is no
revolutionary art"), as recorded in the Portuguese Wikipedia article on *Poesia concreta*.

**Remix today.** Concretism is the strongest historical case for typography as the content
rather than its container. For interface and motion designers, the *Plano-piloto*'s idea of
"verbivocovisual" structure — sound, meaning and shape designed together — describes kinetic
type, variable fonts and generative text layout more precisely than most current writing does.
The Forminform marks are a second lesson: build a sign from parts you can name, and it will
survive any number of redraws.

### Geraldo de Barros {#geraldo-de-barros}

*Chavantes, 1923 – São Paulo, 1998.* Photographer, painter, graphic and furniture designer.

**Context.** In 1949 de Barros set up MASP's photography lab. His 1950 exhibition *Fotoformas*
made abstract, constructed photography a new language in Brazil. On a French scholarship in 1951
he studied lithography in Paris and engraving with Stanley William Hayter, and met Max Bill in
Zurich and Otl Aicher at Ulm. He co-founded Ruptura (1952), the Unilabor furniture cooperative
(1954–1967), Forminform (1958) and later the furniture company Hobjeto. He moved from geometry to
pop and back again. After strokes in the 1990s he made his last photographic series, *Sobras*,
with his assistant Ana Moraes.

**Principles.**

1. **Cut the negative.** Photographs are constructed by scratching, masking and multiple
   exposure, so the camera produces form rather than recording it. *Example:* *Fotoformas*, 1950.
2. **Art for the masses through production.** He considered modern design reaching people
   through a workers' cooperative more important than reaching them through a gallery.
   *Example:* Unilabor furniture, from 1954.
3. **The right to error.** For him error was part of the method; mistakes are how new form
   appears. *Example:* *Sobras*, from 1996.

**Key works.** *Fotoformas*, 1950. *Ruptura*, 1952. Posters for the 1954 IV Centenário de São
Paulo festival, with Alexandre Wollner, which won the competitions there. Unilabor furniture,
from 1954.

**Visual vocabulary.** High-contrast black-and-white photographs with incised lines; flat
geometric painting; modular furniture in plain wood and metal.

**Lineage.** From European Constructivism via Bill and Ulm to Brazilian industrial design.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** De Barros treated the photographic negative as a surface to draw on. That
translates directly to image-generation pipelines: the interesting work happens when you
intervene in the material, not when you accept the first output.

### Décio Pignatari {#decio-pignatari}

*Jundiaí, 1927 – São Paulo, 2012.* Poet, advertising professional, semiotician, translator.

**Context.** Pignatari published his first book, *Carrossel*, in 1950 and co-founded Noigandres
in 1952. He worked in advertising, translated Marshall McLuhan, and taught communication theory
and semiotics, becoming a bridge between concrete poetry and Brazilian design education.

**Principles.**

1. **Detourn the slogan.** The grammar of advertising, short words and repetition, is rearranged
   until the slogan critiques itself. "beba coca cola" slides into "babe cola" and ends on
   "cloaca". *Example:* "beba coca cola", 1957.
2. **Information before ornament.** Communication theory, meaning how much a sign actually
   carries, is a design tool. *Example:* *Informação, Linguagem, Comunicação*, 1968.

**Key works.** "beba coca cola", 1957. *Plano-piloto* (co-author), 1958. *Teoria da Poesia
Concreta* (with the Campos brothers), 1965. *Informação, Linguagem, Comunicação*, 1968.

**Visual vocabulary.** Lowercase sans-serif words in a tight grid; repetition and permutation;
deliberate echoes of the visual language of advertising.

**Lineage.** From Mallarmé, Pound and cummings via Noigandres into Brazilian communication theory.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** "beba coca cola" is a template for brand critique: take a brand's own type and
cadence and permute it until it says the opposite. That's still the sharpest form of satirical
design.

### Haroldo de Campos {#haroldo-de-campos}

*São Paulo, 1929 – São Paulo, 2003.* Poet, critic, translator.

**Context.** Haroldo co-founded Noigandres with his brother Augusto and Pignatari in 1952, and
co-edited the magazines *Noigandres* and *Invenção*. He was the movement's chief theorist and a
prolific translator, from Homer to Goethe to Mayakovsky.

**Principles.**

1. **Transcreation.** Translation re-creates form as well as meaning, so a text's visual and
   sonic structure is what gets carried across. *Example:* his translation work alongside the
   theory in *Teoria da Poesia Concreta*, 1965.
2. **Structure over line.** The spatial structure of the poem, not the verse line, is the unit
   of composition. *Example:* *Plano-piloto*, 1958.

**Key works.** *Plano-piloto* (co-author), 1958. *Teoria da Poesia Concreta*, 1965.

**Visual vocabulary.** As for the movement: spatial arrangement, lowercase sans-serif, isolated
words.

**Lineage.** From Noigandres into Latin American critical theory and the global concrete poetry
movement.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** "Transcreation" is the right word for localisation done well. When an identity
or interface moves across scripts and languages, carry over the structure and the effect, not a
literal copy of the letterforms.

### Augusto de Campos {#augusto-de-campos}

*São Paulo, born 1931.* Poet, translator, visual artist.

**Context.** Augusto published *O Rei Menos o Reino* in 1951 in conventional verse. His
colour-coded *Poetamenos*, published in *Noigandres* 2 in 1955, is counted as
the first consistent body of concrete poetry in Brazil, and he was the first to use the name.
He helped organise the 1956 National Exhibition of Concrete Art. He later made political poems
such as *Greve* (1962), poster-poems, pop-up objects, and digital and animated work.

**Principles.**

1. **Colour as voice.** Each colour in the poem marks a separate voice or timbre, like a score.
   *Example:* *Poetamenos*, 1955.
2. **The poem as poster.** Political poems are printed at poster scale and built from found
   commercial type. *Example:* *Popcretos*, 1964–1966.
3. **The reader assembles it.** Folding three-dimensional structures make reading a physical,
   interactive act. *Example:* *Poemóbiles*, with Julio Plaza, 1974.

**Key works.** *Poetamenos*, 1955. *Popcretos*, 1964–1966. *Poemóbiles*, 1974. *Viva Vaia*
(collected poems), 1979.

**Visual vocabulary.** Multi-coloured type on white; collaged found headlines and display
letters; die-cut and folded paper.

**Lineage.** From Webern's "klangfarbenmelodie" and Mallarmé into concrete poetry, and from there
into digital poetry.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** *Poetamenos* is a lesson in coding meaning with colour: assign colour to role
(voice, speaker, state) and never to decoration. It's the same rule a good data or UI colour
system follows.

### Alexandre Wollner {#alexandre-wollner}

*São Paulo, 1928 – São Paulo, 2018.* Graphic designer, educator.

**Context.** The son of Yugoslav immigrants, Wollner learned the trade in his father's print
shop. He studied at MASP's Instituto de Arte Contemporânea, and on a scholarship went to the
newly founded Ulm school in the mid-1950s, returning in 1958. In 1954, before leaving, he
designed the Cinemateca Brasileira logotype and worked with Geraldo de Barros on the IV
Centenário festival posters. He co-founded Forminform (1958) and ESDI (1963), where the Ministry
of Education gave him special permission to teach without a design degree. He is often called
the father of modern Brazilian design.

**Principles.**

1. **Construct the mark.** Symbols are built from simple geometric parts so they can be redrawn,
   scaled and remembered. *Example:* Sardinhas Coqueiro mark and packaging, 1958.
2. **Research before form.** Identity starts from historical and functional research, so form and
   colour are argued, not chosen for mood. His Itaú mark was later recoloured orange by Francesc
   Petit of the DPZ agency, a change criticised for ignoring the original project's research.
   *Example:* Banco Itaú mark, adopted 1973.
3. **Concrete art as a design grammar.** The concrete painter's training in proportion and the
   module carries straight into identity work. *Example:* Cinemateca Brasileira logotype, 1954.

**Key works.** Cinemateca Brasileira logotype, 1954. Sardinhas Coqueiro, 1958. Identities for
Elevadores Atlas, Klabin, Ultragás, Philco and Hering. Banco Itaú, 1973. *Design visual 50 anos*,
2003.

**Visual vocabulary.** Marks built on visible construction geometry (circles, semicircles, square
modules); monoline or solid forms; sans-serif logotypes; restrained palettes.

**Lineage.** From concrete art and Ulm to Brazilian corporate identity and design education. He
links this slice to `ulm-school`.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Wollner's marks are drawn with a compass and a rule, and they look it. In an era
of animated, responsive logos, constructed geometry is what keeps a mark coherent when it has to
move, scale or rebuild itself in code.

### Aloísio Magalhães {#aloisio-magalhaes}

*Recife, 1927 – Padua, 1982.* Graphic designer, educator, cultural policymaker.

**Context.** Magalhães graduated in law in 1950 and worked in theatre design in Recife. He
studied museology in Paris (1951–53) and engraving at Hayter's Atelier 17. In 1954 he co-founded
the private press O Gráfico Amador in Recife. From 1956 he was in the United States, where he
made the books *Doorway to Portuguese* and *Doorway to Brasília* with the printer Eugene Feldman
and taught at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts. He returned around 1960 and opened a
design office, then co-founded ESDI in 1963. He won the competitions for the IV Centenário do Rio
symbol (1964), the Fundação Bienal de São Paulo symbol and the Light utility symbol, designed the
first Rede Globo symbol (1965, a pinwheel evoking the channel number four), and won the 1966
competition for the cruzeiro novo banknotes. From 1975 he led the Centro Nacional de Referência
Cultural, and from 1979 the national heritage institute IPHAN. 5 November, his birthday, is
Brazil's Graphic Designer's Day.

**Principles.**

1. **Design for a nation's identity.** Currency, anniversaries and broadcasters are where a
   country meets its own image every day. *Example:* cruzeiro novo banknotes, 1966.
2. **Pattern as security and beauty.** Moiré patterns and mirrored compositions make a note hard
   to forge and readable whichever way up it is held. *Example:* the 1,000-cruzeiro "Barão" note,
   1978.
3. **Heritage is design's raw material.** Vernacular craft and cultural memory are the resources
   a modern design culture builds from. *Example:* the Centro Nacional de Referência Cultural,
   from 1975.

**Key works.** O Gráfico Amador, from 1954. IV Centenário do Rio symbol, 1964. First Rede Globo
symbol, 1965. Cruzeiro novo banknotes, from the 1966 competition. 1,000-cruzeiro "Barão" note,
1978.

**Visual vocabulary.** Modular symbols built by rotating and repeating a single unit; moiré
patterns; symmetrical, mirror-image banknote layouts; printmaker's textures in the early books.

**Lineage.** From the printmaking of Atelier 17 and American modernism to Brazilian state
identity, then on to heritage policy.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** The mirrored banknote is a pattern for orientation-agnostic design: if something
will be held, rotated or seen upside-down, design it so every orientation works. That applies to
watch faces, tabletop interfaces and anything printed on objects.

## Venezuelan Modernism {#venezuelan-modernism}

**Context.** In the 1950s oil money made Caracas a laboratory of modernism. Carlos Raúl
Villanueva's Ciudad Universitaria brought art into architecture, and a wave of European émigrés
arrived: Gerd Leufert (from 1952) and his partner, the artist Gego, Nedo Mion Ferrario (1950),
and the commercial designer Larry June. Corporate patrons gave them a free hand, above all the
Creole Petroleum Corporation's house magazine *El Farol*. Leufert redesigned it in 1957, removing
its "criollista" illustration and giving it coherent typography and abstract covers. Nedo then
art-directed it for thirteen years. In 1961 Leufert's *Gráfica 1* at the Museo de Bellas Artes
was the country's first graphic design exhibition. Alongside the designers, Venezuelan kinetic
artists made perception itself their material: Carlos Cruz-Diez (first *Physichromie* and
*Additive Color* works, 1959) and Gego (first *Reticulárea*, 1969). Cruz-Diez had also founded
the Estudio de Artes Visuales, a graphic and industrial design studio, in 1957. The three
designers, Leufert, Nedo and June, are treated as the founding triad of contemporary Venezuelan
graphic design.

**Principles.**

1. **The magazine as laboratory.** A corporate magazine, freed from selling, becomes a place to
   test paper, flaps, halftone screens and typographic structure issue by issue. *Example:* *El
   Farol*, 1957–1972.
2. **Exhibit the discipline.** Showing design in a museum makes it a culture in its own right,
   not a by-product of advertising. *Example:* *Gráfica 1*, Museo de Bellas Artes, 1961.
3. **Perception as material.** Colour and line are events that change as the viewer moves, not
   fixed fills. *Example:* Cruz-Diez, first *Physichromie*, 1959.
4. **Letters as impossible objects.** An alphabet can be a geometric puzzle — reversible,
   impossible or computable — and still read. *Example:* Nedo, *Letromaquia*, 1975.

**Key works.** *El Farol* redesign, 1957. Cruz-Diez, first *Physichromie*, 1959. *Gráfica 1*,
1961. Leufert's emblem for the Venezuelan pavilion at the New York World's Fair, 1964. Gego,
*Reticulárea*, 1969. Nedo, *Reversibles*, 1969, and *Letromaquia*, 1975.

**Visual vocabulary.** Abstract geometric covers; halftone screens enlarged until they become
pattern; changes of paper stock and die-cut flaps inside a single magazine; parallel-line
structures that vibrate optically; alphabets drawn on isometric and reversible grids.

**Lineage.** From the German and Italian training of its émigrés and from Swiss modernism, mixed
with Venezuelan kinetic art. It fed later Venezuelan designers including Santiago Pol and Álvaro
Sotillo, who shared prizes with Leufert and Nedo.

**Quotes.** Gego, at a reception at the Tamarind workshop in 1966: "I discovered that sometimes
the in-between lines is as important as the lines by [themselves]" (quoted in the English
Wikipedia article on Gego). The critic Esteban Muro on Leufert's painting, 1962: "Leufert encara
una síntesis fundamental entre las posibilidades de la luz y la materia como resultado de haber
entendido la pintura como cuestión experimental" (quoted in the Spanish Wikipedia article on
Leufert).

**Remix today.** Venezuelan modernism is the best precedent for treating a branded publication
as research. If a company keeps a magazine, annual or microsite, let it be the place where the
design system is tested, where paper, screen and structure change from issue to issue, rather
than where the system is merely applied.

### Gerd Leufert {#gerd-leufert}

*Memel, 1914 – Caracas, 1998.* Graphic designer, painter, curator, educator.

**Context.** Leufert trained in Hanover, Mainz and Munich, the last under F. H. Ehmcke, and ran
Ehmcke's Munich studio from 1947. He emigrated to Venezuela in 1952 as art director at McCann
Erickson. From 1953 to 1956 he worked in Tarma with Gego. He redesigned *El Farol* (1957–59),
taught at the Central University of Venezuela's architecture faculty and at the Escuela Cristóbal
Rojas, and from 1961 to 1966 worked at the Museo de Bellas Artes, first on its magazine *Visual*
and then as curator. He also co-founded the Instituto Neumann design school.

**Principles.**

1. **The client publication as experiment.** An oil company's magazine is used to test a new
   graphic language. *Example:* *El Farol*, 1957–1959.
2. **The museum as client and stage.** Museum catalogues and exhibitions both practise design
   and present it. *Example:* *Gráfica 1*, 1961.

**Key works.** *El Farol*, 1957. *Gráfica 1*, 1961. New York World's Fair emblem, 1964. His
*Tijoques* paintings, shown at the VIII São Paulo Bienal in 1965.

**Visual vocabulary.** Abstract covers; colour oppositions on reversible fields (his *Union
Square* series, 1964); disciplined typographic structure.

**Lineage.** From the German Gebrauchsgraphik tradition to Venezuelan modernism.

**Quotes.** See the Esteban Muro quote above.

**Remix today.** Leufert put design on museum walls in 1961, in a country without a design
profession. If your discipline is invisible in your organisation, exhibit it.

### Nedo Mion Ferrario {#nedo-mion-ferrario}

*Milan, 1926 – 2001.* Graphic designer, typographer, illustrator, educator. Known as Nedo or
Nedo M.F.

**Context.** Nedo trained in Milan and arrived in Caracas in 1950 with his father, a metal
chaser. He worked for McCann Erickson (1952–53), then took over *El Farol* from Leufert in 1959
and designed sixty consecutive issues until 1972. He was art director of *CAL* (1962–67) and
taught design at the Escuela Cristóbal Rojas for 27 years. He designed around sixty alphabets,
grouped as Imposibles, Reversibles and Computables. In 1993 he won the Premio Nacional de Artes
Plásticas.

**Principles.**

1. **The total magazine.** Paper changes, flaps, the flow from cover into body, and halftone
   experiments make turning the page part of the design. *Example:* *El Farol*, 1959–1972.
2. **Impossible letters.** Alphabets built on optical paradox can still be read as type.
   *Example:* *Reversibles*, Museo de Bellas Artes, 1969.
3. **Teach for decades.** A chair held for 27 years forms a national profession. *Example:* the
   design chair at the Cristóbal Rojas school, from 1959.

**Key works.** *El Farol*, 1959–1972. *CAL*, 1962–1967. *Reversibles*, 1969. *Letromaquia*, 1975.
*Reversámbitos*, Museo Soto, 1974.

**Visual vocabulary.** Isometric and impossible-figure letterforms; black-and-white geometric
illusions; editorial layouts that use the physical structure of the magazine.

**Lineage.** From Milanese craft training to Venezuelan modernism and its later typography.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Nedo's alphabets are ready-made briefs for variable and generative type. Define
a letter by a geometric rule (reversible, impossible, computable) and let the rule produce the
family.

## Cuban Poster {#cuban-poster}

**Context.** The Cuban Revolution triumphed on 1 January 1959. In March of that year the new
government founded the Instituto Cubano del Arte e Industria Cinematográficos (ICAIC), treating
film as an instrument of social change. As commercial advertising disappeared, the poster lost
its old job of selling products and gained new clients: ICAIC (posters for Cuban and foreign
films), Casa de las Américas (cultural events), the Comisión de Orientación Revolucionaria
(government campaigns), and from 1966 OSPAAAL, the Organisation of Solidarity with the People of
Asia, Africa and Latin America. OSPAAAL came out of the Tricontinental Conference, held in Havana
on 3–16 January 1966 and attended by around 500 delegates from 82 countries. Its posters were
printed in offset in several languages, many folded into the magazine *Tricontinental*, and
distributed worldwide. ICAIC's posters were mostly hand-pulled silkscreens in short runs. The
peak came between the mid-1960s and mid-1970s. The Special Period after 1991, with its paper and
ink shortages, ended mass production, though the tradition has revived in a new generation
including Fabián Muñoz, Nelson Ponce and Erick Ginard.

Elena Serrano's *Día del Guerrillero Heroico* (*Day of the Heroic Guerrilla*, OSPAAAL, 1968) is
the movement's best-known image. It repeats Alberto Korda's photograph of Che Guevara in
diminishing squares that spread across a red map of South America, lettered in Spanish, English,
French and Arabic, and it marks 8 October, the anniversary of Che's capture. Serrano isn't
entered as a figure because no birth year could be verified.

**Principles.**

1. **A poster with nothing to sell.** With advertising gone, posters advertised films and causes,
   so designers could use metaphor and wit rather than product benefit. *Example:* René Azcuy, *De
   la guerra americana*, 1969.
2. **Silkscreen as the house technique.** Short runs of hand-pulled screen prints in flat,
   saturated, overprinted colour made each film poster a small edition and gave the school its
   look. *Example:* Alfredo Rostgaard, *Hanoi martes 13*, signed "rostgaard/67", 1967.
3. **One image, every language.** Solidarity posters were lettered in four languages and folded
   into a magazine, so one image travelled across the Global South. *Example:* Serrano, *Day of
   the Heroic Guerrilla*, 1968.
4. **Repetition as rhetoric.** A photographic icon that is multiplied, scaled or tiled becomes a
   mass rather than a portrait. *Example:* Serrano's telescoping Che, 1968.
5. **Pop against pop.** The visual language of US pop and psychedelia is borrowed and turned
   against US policy. *Example:* Rostgaard, *Black Power* poster for OSPAAAL, 1968.

**Key works.** Eduardo Muñoz Bachs, first ICAIC poster, 1960. Azcuy, *Sin esperanza*, 1967.
Rostgaard, *Hanoi martes 13*, 1967. Serrano, *Day of the Heroic Guerrilla*, 1968. Raúl Martínez,
*Day of World Solidarity with the Cuban Revolution, 26 July*, OSPAAAL, 1968. Azcuy, *De la guerra
americana*, 1969.

**Visual vocabulary.** Silkscreened flat colour (often three to five inks) with visible overprint;
hand-drawn lettering and psychedelic line on ICAIC posters; for OSPAAAL, offset lithography,
bright flat grounds, a single central icon, and the same message in Spanish, English, French and
Arabic. The range runs from Azcuy's black-and-white photographic austerity to Muñoz Bachs's
childlike drawing, Rostgaard's pop wit and Martínez's serial pop portraits. A recurring device is
a photographic portrait reduced to high-contrast line, then repeated.

**Lineage.** It drew on the Polish poster school's metaphorical film posters, US Push Pin
illustration and psychedelia, Saul Bass and Swiss modernism (through designers like Félix
Beltrán), and pop art. It fed Chilean popular graphics, the international solidarity poster
movement, and the work of the Center for the Study of Political Graphics and similar archives.

**Quotes.** OSPAAAL's motto, from Fidel Castro's *Second Declaration of Havana* (1962): "This great
humanity has said: enough! And has started to move forward." The Organization of American States
called OSPAAAL "the most dangerous threat that international communism has yet made against the
inter-American system." Both are quoted in the English Wikipedia article on OSPAAAL.

**Remix today.** The Cuban poster is the strongest argument that constraint plus a clear public
purpose beats budget. Take the multilingual single image, the one-icon composition, and the
rule that a film poster should give the viewer an idea rather than a summary of the plot. For
campaign and cultural identity work in 2026 the OSPAAAL model (one central image, four
languages, a format that folds into another medium) maps directly onto global social
distribution.

### Eduardo Muñoz Bachs {#eduardo-munoz-bachs}

*Valencia, 1937 – Havana, 2001.* Poster designer, illustrator, comics artist.

**Context.** Born in Valencia, Muñoz Bachs went into exile in Cuba with his family in 1941. With
no formal design training, in 1960 he made the first poster for ICAIC. He went on to design more
than 2,000 film posters for the institute, illustrated more than fifty children's books, and drew
comics, including *El Cuento* with scripts by Félix Guerra in the 1980s.

**Principles.**

1. **Draw like a child, think like an adult.** A naive, comic drawing style and hand-made
   lettering carry sophisticated irony. *Example:* *La trampa*, 1967.
2. **The figure is the poster.** The human face or whole body dominates; colours are bright but
   never shocking. *Example:* *En busca de un hombre*, 1967.
3. **Volume as a style.** Thousands of posters over a lifetime build a recognisable personal
   language inside an institution. *Example:* *No hay regreso para Johnny*, 1971.

**Key works.** First ICAIC poster, 1960. *La trampa*, 1967. *En busca de un hombre*, 1967. *10
aniversario de los CDR*, 1970. *No hay regreso para Johnny*, 1971.

**Visual vocabulary.** Cartoon figures with large heads; hand-lettered titles integrated into the
drawing; warm, varied colour.

**Lineage.** From children's illustration and comics into film posters. Younger Cuban designers
Fabián Muñoz, Nelson Ponce and Erick Ginard cite him as an inspiration.

**Quotes.** On illustration (as quoted in translation in the English Wikipedia article on him):
"Illustrating a children's notebook represents an artistic liberation that demands an overflow of
ideas from me."

**Remix today.** Muñoz Bachs proves that a hand-drawn, personal mark can be an institution's voice
across thousands of outputs. Illustration-led brand systems should take note: consistency comes
from the hand, not from a template.

### Raúl Martínez {#raul-martinez}

*Ciego de Ávila, 1927 – 1995.* Painter, graphic designer, photographer.

**Context.** Martínez studied at the San Alejandro Academy and the School of the Art Institute of
Chicago, worked in the advertising agency OTPLA in the 1950s, and was a member of the abstract
group Los Once. After 1959 he worked freelance for ICAIC, Casa de las Américas and the Cuban Book
Institute, and taught design at the University of Havana's architecture school. From about 1965
he left abstraction for colourful pop portraits of national figures, José Martí and Camilo
Cienfuegos among them.

**Principles.**

1. **The hero, multiplied.** Heroes are painted in grids of flat colour, pop serialisation turned
   into revolutionary iconography. *Example:* his portraits of José Martí, mid-1960s.
2. **Painter and designer as one practice.** He moved between painting, book design and posters
   without a hierarchy. *Example:* *Day of World Solidarity with the Cuban Revolution*, OSPAAAL,
   1968.

**Key works.** *Day of World Solidarity with the Cuban Revolution, 26 July*, 1968. Poster for
Humberto Solás's film *Lucía* (1968).

**Visual vocabulary.** Flat, saturated pop colour; outlined faces; repeated portraits in grids.

**Lineage.** From Abstract Expressionism (Los Once) to pop and the Cuban poster.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Martínez's grids of heroes show how repetition can make an icon feel collective
rather than individual. That's useful for any brand or campaign that wants to stand for a
community rather than a personality.

### Félix Beltrán {#felix-beltran}

*Havana, 1938 – Mexico, 2022.* Graphic designer, educator, writer.

**Context.** Beltrán worked at McCann Erickson in Havana (1953–56), then studied in New York at
the School of Visual Arts, the American Art School and the Art Students League, where he absorbed
Saul Bass, Raymond Savignac and Josef Müller-Brockmann. Back in Cuba in 1962, he led the
Comisión de Orientación Revolucionaria's poster work with Muñoz Bachs, Antonio Pérez, Martínez
and Rostgaard. He began teaching in 1963, wrote three books on design, and from 1982 lived in
Mexico City, where he founded the first international graphic design archive in Latin America at
the Universidad Autónoma Metropolitana.

**Principles.**

1. **Simplicity as effectiveness.** One idea and the minimum of elements are the basis of
   communication that works. *Example:* *Vietnam will win*, 1968.
2. **Theory alongside practice.** A designer writes about design to build the discipline.
   *Example:* *Desde el diseño* (1970), *Letragrafía* (1973), *Acerca del diseño* (1975).
3. **The archive as infrastructure.** Collecting design builds the discipline's memory.
   *Example:* the international design archive at UAM, Mexico City.

**Key works.** *Vietnam will win*, 1968. *Desde el diseño*, 1970. *Letragrafía*, 1973. *Seremos
reivindicados por la historia*, 1975. His duotone Che Guevara, after Korda, c. 1973.

**Visual vocabulary.** Swiss-influenced economy: one symbol, flat colour, sans-serif type, and lots
of empty space.

**Lineage.** He links American and Swiss modernism to the Cuban poster, and the Cuban poster to
Mexican design education.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Beltrán is the bridge figure: Swiss reduction serving political urgency. If you
need a single-message graphic to work at thumbnail size, his rule of one symbol and one idea is
the brief.

### René Azcuy {#rene-azcuy}

*1939 – 2019.* Poster designer, painter. Wikidata gives his birth as 28 April 1939 and his death
as 25 March 2019. He has no Wikipedia article in English, Spanish or Portuguese, so his career
details below rest on the V&A collection records.

**Context.** Azcuy was one of the core ICAIC designers of the 1960s and 70s, known for the most
severe and photographic posters in the Cuban canon.

**Principles.**

1. **One image, one idea.** A single high-contrast photographic fragment stands for the whole
   film. *Example:* *De la guerra americana* (for Pastor Vega's film), signed "azcuy/69", 1969.
2. **Restraint as intensity.** A minimal palette and large empty areas make violence or desire
   more charged than illustration would. *Example:* *Sin esperanza*, 1967.

**Key works.** *Sin esperanza*, 1967. *De la guerra americana*, 1969. His poster for Truffaut's
*Besos robados* (*Baisers volés*) is often cited as his best-known work; its date wasn't verified
for this slice.

**Visual vocabulary.** Black and white plus one colour; photographic halftone pushed to
high-contrast line; large negative space; small, quiet type.

**Lineage.** Close to the Polish poster school in its metaphorical approach, but photographic
rather than painterly.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Azcuy is the model for key art that trusts one cropped image. For film, music or
editorial covers in 2026, his posters show how far you can reduce before the idea disappears,
and that the answer is "further than you'd think".

### Alfredo Rostgaard {#alfredo-rostgaard}

*Guantánamo, 1943 – Havana, 2004.* Graphic designer, poster artist, caricaturist.

**Context.** Rostgaard studied art in Santiago de Cuba. In 1963 he became art director of
*Mella*, the Young Communists' magazine, where as caricaturist he learned to mix humour and
politics. He moved to Havana in 1965, designed for ICAIC, and was OSPAAAL's art director from 1966
for nine years. From 1975 he worked for the Union of Cuban Writers and Artists.

**Principles.**

1. **Humour for serious subjects.** Caricature and playfulness make grave subjects memorable
   rather than solemn. *Example:* *Create two, three... many Vietnams*, 1967.
2. **Borrow the enemy's pop.** Warhol-like commercial graphics and psychedelic line are turned to
   anti-imperialist ends. *Example:* *Black Power* poster, 1968.
3. **Silkscreen colour as structure.** Flat overprinted inks carry the composition rather than
   just filling it. *Example:* *Hanoi martes 13*, 1967.

**Key works.** *Hanoi martes 13* (for Santiago Álvarez's documentary), 1967. *Create two,
three... many Vietnams*, 1967. *Black Power*, 1968. Folding Nixon poster, 1972.

**Visual vocabulary.** Psychedelic contour line; saturated flat colour; caricature; pop-art
appropriation; posters that fold or transform physically.

**Lineage.** From political caricature and US pop to the OSPAAAL house style.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Rostgaard's folding and transforming posters treat the poster as an interactive
object. Print designers can still do this, and screen designers can learn the lesson that a
reveal or fold can be the whole message.

## Mexico 68 {#mexico-68}

**Context.** Mexico won the 1968 Summer Olympics, the first held in Latin America, and the
architect Pedro Ramírez Vázquez (1919–2013), designer of the Museo Nacional de Antropología
(1964), chaired the organising committee. The committee created a Programa de Identidad Olímpica.
Eduardo Terrazas was its general coordinator and head of urban design, and Beatrice Trueblood ran
publications. With Ramírez Vázquez's permission they recruited designers from abroad, including
the American Lance Wyman, who arrived in December 1966. The resulting logotype set the year 68
inside the Olympic rings and joined it to the word MEXICO, with radiating parallel lines growing
out of the letters. It was extended into a full alphabet, event pictograms, posters, stamps,
clothing and colour coding painted onto the city. The Games opened days after the Tlatelolco
massacre of 2 October 1968, and student protesters answered the Games' dove of peace with a
version pierced by a bayonet. In 1969 the same design language went underground: the Mexico
City Metro opened on 4–5 September with a pictogram for every station.

Authorship is disputed. Wyman and Ramírez Vázquez disagreed over who originated the graphic
concept, and Terrazas also worked on it under Ramírez Vázquez's direction. The V&A credits the
official poster to Terrazas and Wyman together.

**Principles.**

1. **The logotype generates the system.** Parallel lines grown out of the letters of MEXICO68
   become a pattern that extends to posters, clothing, stamps and the street. *Example:* MEXICO68
   logotype and posters, 1968.
2. **Local form, international code.** The concentric-line pattern is widely read as both op art
   and Wixárika (Huichol) yarn work, so one mark spoke to world media and to Mexicans.
   *Example:* the official Mexico 1968 poster.
3. **Signs for people who can't read the words.** Each station has a simple pictogram drawn from
   its name or neighbourhood (a fountain for Salto del Agua, a coyote for Coyoacán), coloured by
   its line, at a time when illiteracy rates were still high. *Example:* Mexico City Metro
   pictograms, 1969.
4. **Urban design as identity.** Colour coding on pavements, kiosks and signs lets the whole city
   wear the event, not just the stadiums. *Example:* Terrazas's urban design programme, 1968.

**Key works.** MEXICO68 logotype and poster programme, 1968. Olympic urban design programme,
1968. Mexico City Metro pictograms, 1969.

**Visual vocabulary.** Bold geometric display letters with multiple parallel outlines radiating
outwards, like ripples; the same artwork printed in many vivid colourways as flat fields;
simple solid pictograms; colour coding for venues and lines; at interchange stations,
diagonal stripes in each line's colour.

**Lineage.** From op art, Swiss and American corporate identity, and the pictogram tradition of
Isotype and the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, joined to Mexican vernacular pattern. It fed directly into
Olympic identity practice (Munich 1972 followed) and into Latin American transit signage: the
Metro system was later adopted in Guadalajara, Monterrey and the Mexico City Metrobús.

**Quotes.** See Lance Wyman below.

**Remix today.** Mexico 68 is the template for a generative identity: one rule (offset parallel
lines) that can be applied to any surface, at any scale, by people who never met the designer.
Its Metro pictograms are the template for inclusive wayfinding. Design every station, screen or
state so it can be recognised without reading.

### Lance Wyman {#lance-wyman}

*Born 1937; raised in Kearny, New Jersey.* Graphic designer, wayfinding designer.

**Context.** The son of a commercial fisherman, Wyman worked summers in factories and graduated
from Pratt in industrial design in 1960. He worked at General Motors (a packaging system that
unified 1,200 Delco packages), then with George Nelson. There, a "pointing hand" logo for the
Chrysler pavilion at the 1964 New York World's Fair, extended into directional signs, convinced
him that a logo could drive a whole design programme. He joined Ramírez Vázquez's office in
December 1966 and left the committee in October 1968 to design the Metro graphics. He has taught
at Parsons since 1973 and founded Lance Wyman Ltd in 1979. In 2017 he received the AIGA Medal.

**Principles.**

1. **A logo should become a programme.** A mark that adapts to signs and environments can drive a
   whole design system. *Example:* the Chrysler pavilion hand, 1964, then MEXICO68.
2. **Functional aesthetic.** He credits the plain functional look of the sea and the factories he
   grew up around. *Example:* Mexico City Metro pictograms, 1969.
3. **Clarity over geography.** A transit map shows routes, transfers and landmarks clearly instead
   of distances to scale. *Example:* the Washington Metro map, mid-1970s, which he revised himself
   in 2012.

**Key works.** MEXICO68 logotype and applications, 1968. Mexico City Metro pictograms, 1969.
Washington Metro map, mid-1970s, revised 2012. Retrospective *Urban Icons*, MUAC, Mexico City,
2014–15.

**Visual vocabulary.** Simple, solid pictograms; line-coded colour; geometric display alphabets;
diagrammatic maps.

**Lineage.** From American corporate identity (see `corporate-identity`) and Paul Rand's influence
to Mexican civic identity, then back into US wayfinding.

**Quotes.** Describing what he took from his childhood: the "no-nonsense functional aesthetic of
the sea and the factories", which he called "an important influence in my approach to design"
(quoted in the English Wikipedia article on Wyman).

**Remix today.** Wyman revised his own Washington map nearly forty years later, which is the
design-systems dream: a system robust enough that its author can extend it decades on. Build
icon and map systems on rules, not on one-off drawings.

### Eduardo Terrazas {#eduardo-terrazas}

*Born 1936.* Architect, urban designer, artist. Wikidata gives 5 March 1936.

**Context.** An architect by training, Terrazas worked with Ramírez Vázquez on Mexico's pavilion
at the 1964 New York World's Fair, where he introduced Beatrice Trueblood to Ramírez Vázquez. In
1966–68 he coordinated the Olympic identity programme and its urban design. He later became an
artist known for systematic geometric series, now catalogued as a Mexican painter.

**Principles.**

1. **Identity at city scale.** An event's identity is applied to streets, kiosks and pavements so
   the whole city becomes the medium. *Example:* Mexico 68 urban design programme, 1968.
2. **Assemble an international team.** Local direction combined with recruited international
   talent produces a system neither would produce alone. *Example:* the Olympic identity team,
   1966–1968.

**Key works.** Mexico pavilion, New York World's Fair (with Ramírez Vázquez), 1964. Mexico 68
identity and urban design programme, 1968.

**Visual vocabulary.** Large-scale colour coding of urban space; the MEXICO68 line pattern at
architectural scale.

**Lineage.** From modern Mexican architecture to Olympic identity and on to geometric abstraction.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** Terrazas thought about identity as urbanism. For event, campus or city brands,
start with the ground plane: the floor, the routes, the colours underfoot.

## Tropicália {#tropicalia}

**Context.** The military coup of 1964 broke Brazil's optimistic modernist project. Cinema Novo,
especially Glauber Rocha's films, gave the left a new, raw image of Brazil, and Rogério Duarte's
poster for *Deus e o Diabo na Terra do Sol* (released in June 1964, two months after the coup) is
the graphic precursor. The movement got its name from Hélio Oiticica's installation *Tropicália*
at the *Nova Objetividade Brasileira* exhibition at MAM Rio in 1967. Caetano Veloso, Gilberto
Gil, Gal Costa, Tom Zé, Os Mutantes and the arranger Rogério Duprat turned it into music, and the
1968 album *Tropicália ou Panis et Circencis* served as its manifesto. Its ideas went back to
Oswald de Andrade's 1928 *Manifesto Antropófago*: Brazil should "cannibalise" foreign culture and
remake it. On 27 December 1968, after the AI-5 decree, Veloso and Gil were arrested; they then
went into exile in London until 1971. The period after that is known as post-tropicalismo.

**Principles.**

1. **Cannibalise everything.** Foreign and local, high and low are swallowed and recombined
   rather than chosen between. *Example:* *Tropicália ou Panis et Circencis*, 1968.
2. **Colour as provocation.** Saturated, clashing colour works as political temperature under
   military rule, not as decoration. *Example:* *Deus e o Diabo na Terra do Sol* poster, 1964.
3. **Concrete discipline, psychedelic surface.** Ulm-literate designers kept structure and type
   control while the surface went lysergic. *Example:* Rogério Duarte's cover for Gilberto Gil's
   1968 album.
4. **Environment over object.** The work is a space you walk through, not a picture you look at.
   *Example:* Oiticica, *Tropicália*, 1967.

**Key works.** Duarte, *Deus e o Diabo na Terra do Sol* poster, 1964. Oiticica, *Tropicália*,
1967. Duarte, *Terra em Transe* poster, 1967. Duarte, covers for Caetano Veloso's and Gilberto
Gil's 1968 albums. *Tropicália ou Panis et Circencis*, 1968.

**Visual vocabulary.** Psychedelic hand lettering over concrete structure; tropical motifs
(parrots, bananas, plants) collaged with pop and Northeastern popular imagery; clashing
saturated colour; family-album photography; kitsch used knowingly.

**Lineage.** From Brazilian concretism (as discipline), Oswald de Andrade's anthropophagy, and
international psychedelia (see `psychedelic`). It fed Brazilian album-cover design for decades,
and its global revival from the 1990s.

**Quotes.** See Rogério Duarte below.

**Remix today.** Tropicália's "anthropophagy" is a better model for working with global
references than either copying them or rejecting them. Digest them, and make the mixture
unmistakably local. That's the brief for any brand from outside the design centres that has to
compete inside them.

### Rogério Duarte {#rogerio-duarte}

*Ubaíra, 1939 – Brasília, 2016.* Graphic designer, illustrator, musician, writer.

**Context.** Duarte moved from Bahia to Rio in 1959–60, where he came to know Alexandre Wollner
and the Ulm circle's ideas: Max Bense, Maldonado, Otl Aicher. He ran visual arts at the student
union's Centro Popular de Cultura in the early 1960s. His posters for Cinema Novo and his record
covers made him the graphic voice of Tropicália. He was a polymath who also composed, translated
and taught.

**Principles.**

1. **Colour researched, then released.** He described the *Deus e o Diabo* poster as using "a
   whole new conception of colour", the product of deep research. *Example:* *Deus e o Diabo na
   Terra do Sol*, 1964.
2. **Know the rules to break them.** Grounding in Ulm-style rational design is what lets the pop
   and psychedelic break land. *Example:* Gilberto Gil album cover, 1968.
3. **Design for the left without its clichés.** Political graphics don't have to look like
   socialist realism; vernacular and pop sources can carry the politics. *Example:* his work for
   the Centro Popular de Cultura, early 1960s.

**Key works.** *Deus e o Diabo na Terra do Sol*, 1964. *Terra em Transe*, 1967. Covers for
Caetano Veloso (1968) and Gilberto Gil (1968). Other film posters include *Vidas Secas* and
*Selva Trágica*; later, the cover of Veloso's *Qualquer Coisa* (1975).

**Visual vocabulary.** Woodcut-like silhouettes, saturated reds, yellows and blues, swirling hand
lettering, and collage.

**Lineage.** From Ulm-influenced Brazilian modernism to Tropicália.

**Quotes.** On the *Deus e o Diabo* poster: "Neste cartaz eu utilizo toda uma nova concepção de
cor, que é fruto de toda uma pesquisa profunda" ("In this poster I use a whole new conception of
colour, the fruit of deep research"), quoted in the Portuguese Wikipedia article on him.

**Remix today.** Duarte shows that expressive work is more convincing when there's a system
underneath it. Learn the grid, then decide exactly where and how loudly to break it.

## Chilean Popular Graphics {#chilean-popular-graphics}

**Context.** Between the founding of the record label Discoteca del Cantar Popular (DICAP) in
the late 1960s and the coup of 11 September 1973, Chile developed a public graphic language for
the left and then for Salvador Allende's Unidad Popular government (1970–73). DICAP was the
Communist Youth's label for artists the multinationals wouldn't record; its first release was
Quilapayún's *X Vietnam* in 1968. Vicente Larrea, often working with his brother Antonio,
designed around 120 album covers, about 300 posters and DICAP's logo. His cover for Víctor Jara
and Quilapayún's *Canciones folklóricas de América* (1967) shows birds in a jungle in flat,
peaceful colour. The Brigada Ramona Parra, created in 1968 by the Communist Youth, painted fast
collective murals with a fixed iconography, and in 1971 painted *El primer gol del pueblo
chileno* in La Granja with Roberto Matta (covered over after the coup, recovered in 2005, and
restored in 2008). The state publisher Quimantú, founded in February 1971 from the nationalised
part of Zig-Zag, printed cheap books and magazines for a mass readership. And the Ulm-trained Gui
Bonsiepe led the team that designed the operations room for Project Cybersyn (1971–73), a
cybernetic economic management system and an early piece of political interface design. The
coup ended all of it: murals painted over, designers exiled.

**Principles.**

1. **The record sleeve as poster.** Nueva Canción covers were the movement's most widely owned
   images, carrying its politics into homes. *Example:* Larrea, *Canciones folklóricas de
   América*, 1967.
2. **Murals by brigade.** Fast, collective wall painting uses a fixed iconography (wheat ear,
   fist, star, birds, workers) outlined in black over flat colour. *Example:* *El primer gol del
   pueblo chileno*, 1971.
3. **Print for the majority.** A state publisher produces cheap books and magazines for mass
   readership, making editorial design a social service. *Example:* Quimantú, from 1971.
4. **Design the state's instruments.** Industrial and interface design applied to national
   infrastructure. *Example:* the Cybersyn operations room, 1972.

**Key works.** *Canciones folklóricas de América* cover, 1967. Brigada Ramona Parra and Matta,
*El primer gol del pueblo chileno*, 1971. Cybersyn operations room, 1972.

**Visual vocabulary.** Flat, bright colour with thick black outlines; stylised birds, flowers,
hands and stars; hand-drawn, rounded, psychedelia-influenced lettering on record covers; for
Cybersyn, seven swivel chairs with control buttons facing wall screens and status panels, an
Ulm-style modernist interior.

**Lineage.** From the Cuban poster, international psychedelia and Push Pin illustration, plus,
through Bonsiepe, the Ulm school. It fed exile graphic work in Europe in the 1970s and the
politically engaged poster culture of Chile's return to democracy.

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** The Brigada Ramona Parra's fixed icon set (a dozen motifs anyone in the brigade
could paint) is a design system in the most literal sense: a constrained vocabulary that lets
many hands produce coherent work fast. Cybersyn's control room is still the reference image for
civic dashboards.

## Latin American Type Design {#latin-american-type}

**Context.** From the late 1980s, type design and typographic publishing became one of Latin
America's most internationally visible design practices. In Buenos Aires, Rubén Fontana's
*tipoGráfica* magazine (generally dated to 1987) gave the region a serious typographic journal,
and the University of Buenos Aires graphic design degree (from 1985) trained a generation. Large
wayfinding commissions, notably Diseño Shakespear's Subte signage (1995–2008), tested type in
public space. In 2004 the first Bienal Tipos Latinos began a travelling regional showcase. Its
third edition, in 2008, was organised in Chile. The same year Miguel Hernández and Luciano
Vergara founded the digital foundry Latinotype in Concepción, Chile. By 2013 its typeface Trend,
by Daniel Hernández, was among the most popular on MyFonts and was used in the Olympique Lyonnais
crest redesign. Digital distribution let foundries far from Europe and the US sell directly to
global brands.

**Principles.**

1. **A regional canon.** A biennial that moves between countries builds a shared Latin American
   type culture rather than many national ones. *Example:* Bienal Tipos Latinos, from 2004.
2. **Sell to the world from the periphery.** Digital distribution lets a foundry outside the
   centres reach global clients directly. *Example:* Latinotype, founded 2008.
3. **Signage as a national typeface practice.** Large wayfinding commissions are the testing
   ground for custom lettering. *Example:* Buenos Aires Subte signage, 1995–2008.

**Key works.** *tipoGráfica*, 1987. Subte signage, from 1995. Bienal Tipos Latinos, 2004.
Latinotype, 2008.

**Visual vocabulary.** A strong tradition of display scripts and lettering alongside rigorous
text and signage families; large multi-weight digital families built for branding and interfaces.

**Lineage.** From Argentine modernism and its typographic culture, and from the digital type
revolution that Emigre opened up (see `emigre-digital-type`).

**Quotes.** None verified.

**Remix today.** This is the ongoing chapter. The opportunity in 2026 is variable and
multi-script families designed from Latin America's own lettering traditions (signwriting,
*fileteado porteño*, popular hand lettering), not just Latin versions of Swiss grotesques.
