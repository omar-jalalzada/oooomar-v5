# Eastern Europe and the Middle East, 1920–2026

Slice id: `eastern-europe-middle-east`.

This slice puts two regions side by side that the Western canon usually files as footnotes: the
Czech and Polish traditions on one side, the Iranian and Arab traditions on the other. They
belong together for more than convenience. In both, design grew up under strong patrons (a
socialist state, a royal court, a post-colonial nation), where the commercial client that
shaped American and Swiss modernism was weak or absent. In both, censorship or ideology made
*allusion* a working method. And in both, the deepest question was what to do with an
inheritance: Czech designers had to absorb Constructivism without becoming Russian; Polish
designers built a poster culture deliberately opposed to the Swiss grid; Iranian and Arab
designers had to make a modern visual language out of a script that comes with a thousand years
of calligraphic rules. Influence also ran *between* these places, not only out from Paris and
Basel. Morteza Momayez first met modern graphic design in Polish magazines at the Polish
embassy library in Tehran. Polish poster exhibitions toured to Beirut in 1961, and Polish
designers taught in Havana in 1970.

Movements, in order: [Czech avant-garde](#czech-avant-garde),
[Polish Poster School](#polish-poster-school),
[Czechoslovak film poster](#czechoslovak-film-poster), [Saqqakhaneh](#saqqakhaneh),
[Iranian graphic design](#iranian-graphic-design), [Hurufiyya](#hurufiyya),
[Arabic modernism](#arabic-modernism).

## Czech Avant-garde {#czech-avant-garde}

**Context.** Czechoslovakia was founded in 1918, and its avant-garde grew up with the new
country. Devětsil ("butterbur") was set up in Prague in 1920 by Karel Teige, Jaroslav Seifert,
Vladislav Vančura and Adolf Hoffmeister, with a Brno group from 1923. It took in poets,
architects, painters, actors and typographers. It started with proletarian art, then from 1923
to 1924 turned to *Poetism*, the programme Teige and Vítězslav Nezval worked out. Poetism wanted
an optimistic, playful art of the modern everyday: fireworks, cinema, jazz, the circus, the
airplane. Devětsil brought Le Corbusier, Gropius, Man Ray, Klee and Mayakovsky to Prague and
published *Disk*, *Pásmo* and *ReD* (*Revue Devětsilu*, 1927–1931). The technology that made it
possible was the halftone photo block and a strong local printing industry. The ideas came from
Russian Constructivism, the Bauhaus, Dada and the New Typography, which Prague absorbed almost as
fast as they appeared. The movement ended twice. Devětsil itself wound down around 1930 to 1931,
and its spirit passed into the Prague Surrealist group of 1934. The Communist takeover of 1948
then silenced Teige for good.

**Principles.**

1. **The picture poem.** Image and verse share one surface; the page is treated as a canvas,
   not a column of text. Teige announced it in the 1923 essay "Painting and Poetry" in *Disk*.
   *Example:* Teige's typography for Jaroslav Seifert's *Na vlnách TSF* (*On the Waves of
   Wireless*), 1925, where each poem's content is expressed in its typographic layout.
2. **Letters as bodies.** The alphabet is something you can perform. *Example:* *Abeceda*
   (Vítězslav Nezval, Karel Teige, Milča Mayerová), published by Jan Otto, Prague, 1926. Each
   letter gets a poem and a photomontage of the dancer Mayerová posing as that letter, set
   against heavy sans-serif and rules.
3. **Constructivist economy for a lyrical end.** Sans-serif type, bars, arrows and photographs
   were used for joy rather than for agitation. *Example:* Teige's covers for *ReD*,
   1927–1931.
4. **Exhibition and catalogue as information systems.** Sutnar carried the same grammar into
   exhibition design and publishing for the Družstevní práce cooperative. *Example:* Sutnar,
   poster *Visit the Modern Textile Exhibition*, 1930, which uses type alone in a rectangular
   hierarchy of sizes.

**Key works.** *Na vlnách TSF*, Seifert/Teige, 1925. *Abeceda*, Nezval/Teige/Mayerová, 1926.
*ReD*, Teige ed., 1927–1931. *Visit the Modern Textile Exhibition*, Sutnar, 1930. *Nejmenší byt*
(*The Minimum Dwelling*), Teige, 1932.

**Visual vocabulary.** Grotesque sans-serifs set large and often on diagonals. Thick black and
red rules. Photomontage cut from postcards, maps and press photographs. Asymmetric layouts that
borrow from Lissitzky and Moholy-Nagy but are lighter in mood. A two-colour palette of black and
red on white or cream. Pictograms and punctuation used as ornament. Picture poems built like
travel souvenirs.

**Lineage.** Comes from [constructivism], [dada], [new-typography] and [bauhaus], all cross-slice.
Teige lectured at the Bauhaus in Dessau in 1929–30. It fed Czech Surrealism (Štyrský, Toyen),
the later Czechoslovak film poster (below) and, through Sutnar's emigration in 1939,
[american-modernism] information design.

**Quotes.**

- "Není třeba umění ze života a pro život, ale umění jakožto součást života." ("What is needed is
  not art from life and for life, but art as a part of life.") — Karel Teige, 1922, as quoted
  in the Czech Wikipedia entry on Teige. The translation is mine.
- André Breton, Prague lecture, 1935, on Surrealism: "Constantly interpreted by Teige in the most
  lively way, made to undergo an all-powerful lyric thrust by Nezval, Surrealism can flatter
  itself that it has blossomed in Prague as it has in Paris."

**Remix today.** Poetism is the missing half of modernist typography: the same sans-serif and
rules, used for delight rather than for order. A designer in 2026 can take the *Abeceda* move,
the alphabet as choreography, into variable fonts and motion, where a letter really can be a
body in a pose. A brand built on picture poems rather than on hero images is still rare.

### Karel Teige {#karel-teige}

Karel Teige (1900–1951, Prague) was a critic, theorist, collagist and typographer, and the
leader of Devětsil. He co-founded the group in 1920, edited *ReD* from 1927, lectured at the
Bauhaus in 1929–30, co-founded the Left Front, and from 1934 was the main theorist of the Prague
Surrealist group. After February 1948 he was isolated and attacked in the press, and he died of
a heart attack in 1951. The secret police destroyed his papers.

**Principles.**

- **Typography is poetry's second voice.** The layout has to carry the poem's meaning, not just
  hold it. *Na vlnách TSF*, 1925.
- **Montage over illustration.** Photographs are cut and recombined into a new statement rather
  than commissioned to depict. *Abeceda*, 1926. Later, his private Surrealist collages of
  1935–1951.
- **Function as a social programme.** Design follows from the "scientific solution of exact
  tasks of rational construction". This is his phrase from the 1929 critique of Le Corbusier's
  Mundaneum, which he applied to housing in *The Minimum Dwelling*, 1932.

**Key works.** *Na vlnách TSF*, 1925 (book). *Abeceda*, 1926 (book). *ReD*, 1927–1931
(magazine). *Nejmenší byt*, 1932 (book). *Surrealismus proti proudu*, 1938 (book).

**Visual vocabulary.** Heavy grotesques, black and red, diagonal axes, circles and bars from
Lissitzky. In the collages, figures cut from magazines are set in empty space.

**Lineage.** Constructivism and Dada lead into Poetism and then Czech Surrealism. He was later
claimed by Czech designers of the 1960s.

**Remix today.** Treat typography as an argument about the text, not as packaging for it.

### Ladislav Sutnar {#ladislav-sutnar}

Ladislav Sutnar (1897 Plzeň – 1976 New York) taught at the State School of Graphic Arts in
Prague from 1923 and became its director in 1932. From 1929 to 1939 he designed books and
interiors for the Družstevní práce cooperative and its Krásná jizba shop. He won a gold medal
for exhibition design at Barcelona in 1929 and a Grand Prix at the Milan Triennale in 1936. Sent
to New York for the 1939 World's Fair, he stayed after the Nazi occupation. As art director of
Sweet's Catalog Service (1941–1960) he co-wrote, with Knud Lönberg-Holm, the books that founded
American information design.

**Principles.**

- **Controlled visual flow.** Colour, shape and size direct the eye through dense information.
  *Catalog Design*, 1944, and *Catalog Design Progress*, 1950.
- **Punctuation as navigation.** Brackets, tabs, numbers and arrows are wayfinding tools. He put
  parentheses around telephone area codes for the Bell System in the late 1950s, uncredited.
- **Type alone can hold attention.** A strict hierarchy of sizes can stand in for a picture.
  *Visit the Modern Textile Exhibition*, 1930.

**Key works.** *Visit the Modern Textile Exhibition*, 1930 (poster). Jacket for Shaw's *Obrácení
kapitána Brassbounda*, 1932 (book). *Catalog Design*, 1944 (book). *Visual Design in Action*, 1961
(book).

**Visual vocabulary.** Primary red, blue and black. Diagonals and bands. Large index numerals,
tabs and icons on a modular page.

**Lineage.** Czech avant-garde, then [american-modernism] (cross-slice). He is a direct ancestor
of interface navigation. The Ladislav Sutnar Faculty of Design and Art in Plzeň was named after
him in 2014.

**Remix today.** Sutnar is the bridge between Poetism and the design system. His catalogues are
component libraries made of paper.

## Polish Poster School {#polish-poster-school}

**Context.** After 1945 Poland was rebuilt inside the Soviet bloc. The state controlled every
client: Film Polski, and then the Centrala Wynajmu Filmów (CWF) distribution agency, the theatres,
the opera, the state circus, the publishing house Wydawnictwo Artystyczno-Graficzne (WAG). There
was no product to sell and no market to please. A poster only had to pass the censor and to
fill the bare walls of a ruined city. In 1947 Film Polski asked Henryk Tomaszewski and Eryk
Lipiński to design film posters. Tomaszewski answered not with a portrait of the stars but with
a painterly, metaphorical sign for the film's mood, and he won five first prizes at the 1948
international poster exhibition in Vienna. From 1952 his poster studio at the Warsaw Academy of
Fine Arts trained the school. Socialist Realism held until Stalin's death and the thaw of 1956.
After that the school flowered, and its name stuck around 1956 to 1965. The movement built its
own institutions: the magazine *Projekt* (1956), the International Poster Biennale in Warsaw
(1966), the world's first such event, and the Poster Museum at Wilanów (1968), the world's first
poster museum. The conditions that made it ended with communism in 1989.

It deliberately refused both Socialist Realism and the rational Western grammar of
Bauhaus-to-Swiss typography. That makes it the great counter-model to [swiss-style] (cross-slice).

**Principles.**

1. **The poster as an authored picture.** The designer is also the image-maker, and the
   personal hand is the point. *Example:* Tomaszewski, *Obywatel Kane* (*Citizen Kane*), 1948.
2. **Metaphor over depiction.** One shortcut image stands for the whole film or play, and that
   indirectness also got past the censor. *Example:* Wiktor Górka, *Kabaret* (*Cabaret*), 1973.
   The dancers' black-stockinged legs and Joel Grey's made-up face together form a swastika.
3. **Hand-lettering as image.** The type is drawn, not set, and often carries the whole design.
   *Example:* Tomaszewski, *Moore* (exhibition poster), 1959.
4. **Painterly gesture in a printed medium.** Gouache, watercolour, crayon and collage go
   straight into offset lithography. *Example:* Waldemar Świerzy's portrait posters of the 1970s,
   and later his expressionist "spaghetti" faces that only become legible from a distance.
5. **Style follows the subject, not the house.** No school style was enforced. Starowieyski's
   baroque skulls, Lenica's art-nouveau line and Cieślewicz's halftone photomontage all count as
   "Polish School". *Example:* Roman Cieślewicz, *Zawrót głowy* (*Vertigo*), 1963.

**Key works.** Tomaszewski, *Czarny Narcyz* (*Black Narcissus*), 1948. Tadeusz Trepkowski,
*Nie!* (*No!*), 1952. Świerzy, *Czerwona Oberża* (*The Red Inn*), 1955. Starowieyski, *Franek
V*, 1962. Cieślewicz, *Zawrót głowy* (*Vertigo*), 1963. Lenica, *Wozzeck*, 1964. Górka,
*Kabaret*, 1973.

**Visual vocabulary.** Drawn, uneven, often naive lettering that is part of the picture. Flat,
saturated, sometimes clashing colour, or ink drawing on raw white paper. Painted textures
reproduced in offset. Surreal juxtaposition and visual puns (a face that is also a landscape).
Collage and photomontage. Generous empty paper. No grid, and no photograph of the star.

**Lineage.** The roots are pre-war Polish posters (Trepkowski, Gronowski, close to [art-deco]),
Surrealism, and, for Cieślewicz, [constructivism] and Polish Blok. It fed [cuban-poster]
(cross-slice): Górka and Świerzy led workshops in Havana in 1970. It shaped French activist
graphics through Tomaszewski's students Pierre Bernard of Grapus and Alain Le Quernec, and
[psychedelic] (cross-slice) borrowed its line. It also reached [iranian-graphic-design] through
Momayez.

**Remix today.** The Polish School shows that a cultural client gets a better poster by asking
for an *idea* than for a layout. In 2026, when most cultural marketing is a photo with a type
lock-up, the move is to commission one metaphor per production and let the designer's own hand
carry the type. The constraint of the censor maps neatly onto the constraint of the feed: say
it in one image that rewards a second look.

### Henryk Tomaszewski {#henryk-tomaszewski}

Henryk Tomaszewski (1914–2005, Warsaw) is called the father of the school. He won five first
prizes at Vienna in 1948, ran the poster studio at the Warsaw Academy from 1952 to 1985, and was
made an Honorary Royal Designer for Industry. Wikipedia dates that honour to 1975; culture.pl and
Polish Wikipedia give 1976. His students included Pierre Bernard, Alain Le Quernec, Andrzej
Klimowski, Jan Młodożeniec and Mieczysław Wasilewski.

**Principles.**

- **Strip to the sign.** Analyse the brief, throw away what is superfluous, and arrive at a
  shortcut that works as a sign. Drop the adjectives. *Moore*, 1959; *Cyrk*, 1963.
- **The lettering is the image.** From the late 1950s his handwriting increasingly *is* the
  poster. *Henryk Tomaszewski – Love*, 1991.
- **Train the eye, not the hand.** He taught abstract concepts as riddles to be solved, not
  technique.
- **Design is a service, and that is no excuse for dullness.** He called himself a graphic
  artist "who carries furniture".

**Key works.** *Symfonia pastoralna*, 1947. *Obywatel Kane*, 1948. *Moore*, 1959. *Król Edyp*
(*Oedipus Rex*), 1961. *Cyrk*, 1963. *Książka zażaleń* (*Book of Complaints*), 1961 (book). *Love*,
1991.

**Visual vocabulary.** Large areas of empty paper. Brushed or scrawled capitals. Cut-paper
shapes. One witty gesture.

**Quotes.**

- "Podaję temat, z którego student drogą analizy powinien odrzucić co zbędne, aby dojść do
  skrótu – znaku. (...) Przyzwyczajam ich do rezygnacji z orzeczeń, przymiotników i wszelkich
  pięknych, a najczęściej zbędnych ozdobników." ("I give a subject from which the student, by
  analysis, should throw out what is superfluous to reach a shortcut, a sign... I accustom them
  to giving up predicates, adjectives and all the beautiful, mostly unnecessary
  embellishments.") — Tomaszewski on teaching, quoted in Polish Wikipedia. The translation is
  mine.
- "Wszystko idzie od OKA. Ono musi być wykształcone, niepodległe – abstrakcyjne." ("Everything
  starts from the EYE. It must be educated, independent, abstract.") — same source.
- "...ja jestem grafik, co nosi meble, bo kiedy przychodzi klient i mówi: zanieś pan te meble, to
  ja zanoszę." ("I am a graphic artist who carries furniture: when a client comes and says,
  carry these pieces, I carry them.") — same source.

**Remix today.** His brief is still the best design exercise there is: one abstract noun, one
sheet, no adjectives.

### Józef Mroszczak {#jozef-mroszczak}

Józef Mroszczak (1910 Nowy Targ – 1975 Warsaw) trained in Kraków and at the Graphische Lehr- und
Versuchsanstalt in Vienna (1934–37). He organised post-war design education in Silesia and then
in Warsaw, where he taught at the Academy from 1952 and was dean in 1968–71. He co-founded
*Projekt* in 1956 and edited it until 1966. He founded the International Poster Biennale in
Warsaw in 1966 and chaired its committee until his death, pushed for the Wilanów museum, and was
vice-president of ICOGRADA from 1968 to 1970.

**Principles.**

- **Build the institution, and the style follows.** A magazine, a biennale and a museum gave the
  school an international stage. *Projekt*, 1956; the first Biennale, 1966.
- **Balance design and expression.** His posters are rigorous and still painterly. *Don Carlos*,
  1964.
- **The poster as public conscience.** *Auschwitz – Never Again* (project) won the Minister of
  Defence prize at the first Warsaw Biennale, 1966.

**Key works.** *Projekt* (magazine), 1956. *Polnische Plakatkunst* (book), 1962. *Don Carlos*,
1964. First International Poster Biennale, Warsaw, 1966 (exhibition). *Słowo i obraz* (*Word
and Image*), 1971, gold medal at Brno 1974.

**Remix today.** Curating a scene is design work. Mroszczak's biennale is why we know the
Polish School at all.

### Jan Lenica {#jan-lenica}

Jan Lenica (1928 Poznań – 2001 Berlin) trained as an architect and a pianist. He drew satire
for *Szpilki*, assisted in Tomaszewski's poster department from 1954, and co-founded Polish
avant-garde animation with Walerian Borowczyk (*Był sobie raz...*, 1957; *Dom*, 1958). He lived
in France from 1963 to 1986 and in Berlin from 1987. He won the gold medal (Prix Max Ernst) at
the first Warsaw Biennale in 1966.

**Principles.**

- **The contour as a living thing.** Flowing multiple outlines revive Art Nouveau line for the
  1960s. *Wozzeck*, 1964.
- **Poster and animation are one art.** Cut-out figures move between the street and the screen.
  *Labirynt*, 1962.
- **Absurdity as political commentary.** Bureaucracy and the machine swallow the individual.
  *Labirynt*, and *Monsieur Tête* with Ionesco, 1959.

**Key works.** *Dom* (film, with Borowczyk), 1958. *Labirynt* (film), 1962; some sources say
1963. *Wozzeck* (opera poster), 1964. *Ubu et la Grande Gidouille* (film), 1976, released 1979.

**Lineage.** [art-nouveau] (cross-slice) line reborn, and a direct echo in [psychedelic] posters.

**Remix today.** A single animated contour is a complete identity system. Lenica designed for
motion before screens demanded it.

### Roman Cieślewicz {#roman-cieslewicz}

Roman Cieślewicz (1930 Lviv – 1996, Paris region) studied at the Kraków Academy (1949–55). He
co-designed *Projekt* in 1956 and was art director of *Ty i Ja* from 1959 to 1962. He moved to
Paris in 1963, art-directed *Elle* (1965–69) and *Opus International*, belonged to the Panique
group, and designed major Centre Pompidou catalogues and posters such as *Paris–Moscou
1900–1930*, 1979.

**Principles.**

- **Maximum picture, maximum information.** The poster is the "street picture" and must hit
  hard.
- **Mechanise the image.** An enlarged halftone screen, mirroring and repetition make the
  picture pulse like op art. *Zawrót głowy*, 1963.
- **Montage from the archive.** Old masters and press photographs are collided into new
  meaning. *Kamikaze*, 1976.

**Key works.** *Kamienne niebo*, 1959. *Zawrót głowy* (*Vertigo*), 1963. *Dziady*, 1967.
*Kamikaze* no. 1 (magazine), 1976. *Paris–Moscou*, 1979. *Liberté = Wolność*, 1981.

**Visual vocabulary.** Coarse halftone dots blown up to texture. Symmetrical doubling. Eyes and
targets. Black, red and flesh tones.

**Quote.** "It was my dream to make public pictures that could be seen by as many people as
possible. Hence the utmost importance of the poster – the street picture." — Cieślewicz,
interview with Wiesława Wierzchowska, *Autoportrety / Self-Portraits*, Interster, Warsaw 1994, as
quoted on culture.pl.

**Lineage.** Constructivist photomontage and Polish Blok lead to him. He in turn reaches
[punk] and [new-wave] collage (cross-slice) through his Paris magazine work.

**Remix today.** Treat the halftone screen, today the pixel and the dither, as expressive matter,
not as a defect to hide.

### Waldemar Świerzy {#waldemar-swierzy}

Waldemar Świerzy (1931 Katowice – 2013 Warsaw; English Wikipedia gives only "c. 1931") studied
under Mroszczak, who brought him to WAG in Warsaw. He made more than 1,500 posters, taught in
Poznań from 1965 and in the Warsaw poster studio from 1994, and lectured in Havana in 1970. He
won the Toulouse-Lautrec Grand Prix at Versailles in 1959 for *Czerwona Oberża*.

**Principles.**

- **Bring painting into the poster.** From the mid-1950s gesture, lyrical abstraction and
  pop-art go straight onto the sheet.
- **The portrait as a sign.** A few traits plus a metaphor for the profession, over 250
  cultural portraits in all. *Czerwona Oberża*, 1955, with Fernandel's face grotesquely
  elongated.
- **Mix high and low on purpose.** Pop-art meets folk art. *Hej kolęda, kolęda* for the band No
  To Co, 1968.
- **Legible only at a distance.** His 1980s faces dissolve into dots and squiggles up close.

**Key works.** *Czerwona Oberża* (*The Red Inn*), 1955. *Bulwar zachodzącego słońca* (*Sunset
Boulevard*), 1957. *Hej kolęda*, 1968. *Auf Jagd nach Polen*, 1969. *Ziemia obiecana* (*The
Promised Land*), 1975. His *Jimi Hendrix* portrait poster is famous but its year is not
confirmed here.

**Remix today.** Portrait-led identities for cultural institutions can be painterly and
deliberately "wrong" in colour and still work in the street.

### Franciszek Starowieyski {#franciszek-starowieyski}

Franciszek Starowieyski (1930 Bratkówka – 2009 Warsaw) studied in Kraków and Warsaw (diploma
1955) and made about 300 posters. He was the first Polish artist with a solo show at MoMA:
culture.pl dates it 1985, English Wikipedia 1986. From 1980 he staged his "Drawing Theatre" in
public.

**Principles.**

- **The baroque as resistance.** Skulls, anatomy and 17th-century calligraphy subvert both
  Socialist Realism and the cheerful circus poster. *Franek V*, 1962, where a bank corner turns
  into a skull.
- **The poster as an autonomous work.** He signed them as "Franciszek Starowieyski presents...".
  *Cyrk – Homage to Picasso*, 1966.
- **A persona as method.** From 1970 he back-dated his works by 300 years.

**Key works.** *Płaczący gołąbek pokoju* (*The Crying Dove of Peace*), 1956, made in solidarity
with Hungary. *Franek V*, 1962. *Cyrk – Homage to Picasso*, 1966. Grand Prix for a film poster,
Cannes, 1974.

**Quote.** "Ever since I remember, drawing has been my language. It best expresses my thoughts."
— Starowieyski, as quoted on culture.pl.

**Remix today.** An ornamental, morbid, hand-drawn register is a real alternative to the clean
neutral, and it rarely gets confused with anything else.

### Wiktor Górka {#wiktor-gorka}

Wiktor Górka (1922 Komorowice – 2004 Warsaw) studied in Kraków (diploma 1952, with painting
under Nowosielski and Kantor) and made about 300 posters, for *Spartacus*, *The Great Escape*,
*2001: A Space Odyssey* and *Cabaret* among others. In 1970 he went to Havana with Polish
designers to run workshops, then moved to Mexico, where he taught at UNAM and elsewhere until
the mid-1990s. He received the José Guadalupe Posada Medal at the Mexico poster biennial in
2000.

**Principles.**

- **The composite emblem.** Two motifs fuse into a third, damning meaning. *Kabaret*, 1973.
  Wikipedia's list of examples gives 1972.
- **Teaching as export.** He carried the school's method to Cuba and Mexico.

**Key works.** *Kabaret*, 1973. First prize, International Tourist Poster Competition, Berlin,
1967.

**Lineage.** Polish School into [cuban-poster] and Mexican poster teaching.

**Remix today.** The fused-emblem trick still works in a square social tile at 300 px.

## Czechoslovak Film Poster {#czechoslovak-film-poster}

**Context.** In communist Czechoslovakia, as in Poland, the central film distributor (Ústřední
půjčovna filmů) commissioned its own posters for both domestic and foreign films. These were
usually far more artistic than the originals. Because film posters also served as promotion
abroad, bringing prestige and hard currency, censorship of them was lighter than elsewhere. From
the late 1950s through the Prague Spring of 1968 and into the 1980s, architects, painters and
typographers produced a body of film posters that sat beside the Czechoslovak New Wave in
cinema. Their roots ran straight back to Devětsil photomontage and typography.

**Principles.**

1. **Typography as the film's voice.** The title is designed, not illustrated. *Example:*
   Zdeněk Ziegler, *Křik* (*The Cry*, dir. Jaromil Jireš), 1963.
2. **Magic realism and surrealist painting on the street.** *Example:* Josef Vyleťal
   (1940–1989), poster for *Panna a netvor* (*The Virgin and the Monster*, dir. Juraj Herz),
   awarded at the Chicago International Film Festival in 1979.
3. **The painter's catalogue.** Karel Vaca (1919–1989) made 341 theatre and film posters and
   141 book designs, treating the poster as one more medium of a painter's practice.

**Key works.** Ziegler, *Křik*, 1963. Vyleťal, posters for *Amarcord* and *Panna a netvor*,
1970s.

**Visual vocabulary.** Photomontage and painted surrealism. Bold experimental lettering. Muted
inks with one loud colour.

**Lineage.** Develops from the [czech-avant-garde] and runs parallel to the
[polish-poster-school].

**Remix today.** Film and streaming key art is now almost entirely photographic. The
Czechoslovak model of a *typographer's* film poster is open territory.

### Zdeněk Ziegler {#zdenek-ziegler}

Zdeněk Ziegler (1932 Prague – 2023) studied architecture at ČVUT (1955–61) and worked freelance.
From 1963 he designed film posters for Jireš, Vláčil, Zeman, Kachyňa and Menzel, and book
series for Odeon and Československý spisovatel. He taught at the Academy of Arts, Architecture
and Design in Prague (VŠUP) from 1990 to 2005 and was its rector in 2000–03. He received the
ICOGRADA award in 2004.

**Principles.**

- **Choose the typeface for the nature of the text.** He thought each typeface through as part
  of the meaning.
- **One designer, one whole.** He designed the poster, the book series, the identity and the
  stamp for the same institution's world. Aulos bibliophile editions, from 1992.

**Key works.** *Křik*, 1963 (poster). Aulos bibliophile series, 1992 onwards (books). Czech
postage stamps, from 1994 (other).

**Remix today.** A typographer's poster can be a complete statement without an image.

## Saqqakhaneh {#saqqakhaneh}

**Context.** Modern art in Iran began in the late 1940s. In 1949 the Apadana gallery opened in
Tehran, and the College of Fine Arts at Tehran University, modelled on the Beaux-Arts, moved
away from the academic painting of Kamal-ol-Molk. In the late 1950s and 1960s a group of young
artists turned to the visual culture of popular Shi'a devotion. The *saqqakhaneh* is a small
public water shrine, often hung with locks, talismans, hands and inscriptions. These artists
combined such objects with modern painting and sculpture. The critic Karim Emami named them the
"Saqqakhaneh school". Its core members were Parviz Tanavoli, Hossein Zenderoudi, Faramarz
Pilaram, Massoud Arabshahi, Mansoor Ghandriz, Nasser Ovissi, Sadegh Tabrizi and Jazeh
Tabatabai. Zenderoudi won a prize at the Paris Biennale in 1958. By the 1970s they had an
international profile, which helped prepare the ground for the Tehran Museum of Contemporary Art
in 1977. Saqqakhaneh matters to graphic design because it gave Iranian designers permission to
use script, seal, amulet and pattern as modern form.

**Principles.**

1. **Folk-devotional objects as modern material.** Locks, hands, talismans, astrolabes and
   zodiac signs. *Example:* Tanavoli's locks and *Heech* sculptures, from the 1960s.
2. **Script as visual, not legible, matter.** *Example:* Zenderoudi, *Who is this Hossein the
   world is crazy about?*, linocut on linen, 1958 (British Museum).
3. **Repetition and grid of signs.** Tablets covered edge to edge in letters and numbers.
   *Example:* Zenderoudi, *K+L+32+H+4. Mon père et moi*, 1962 (MoMA).

**Key works.** Zenderoudi, *Who is this Hossein...*, 1958. Zenderoudi, *K+L+32+H+4*, 1962.
Tanavoli, *Poet Turning into Heech*, 2007 (Met).

**Visual vocabulary.** Votive hands, padlocks and grilles. Nasta'liq and pseudo-script repeated
as texture. Gold, turquoise, earth red. Frontal, iconic composition taken from coffee-house
painting and religious prints.

**Lineage.** Persian popular art, coffee-house painting and Shi'a votive culture. It is the
Iranian branch of [hurufiyya] and fed [iranian-graphic-design]. Edward Said's *Orientalism*
(1978) later prompted the debate over whether it resisted or intensified exoticism.

**Remix today.** Vernacular ritual objects are a richer source than "heritage pattern" packs.
Take the *logic* of the talisman, a dense sign meant to protect a threshold, rather than its
surface.

### Parviz Tanavoli {#parviz-tanavoli}

Parviz Tanavoli (born 1937, Tehran) is a sculptor, painter, educator and historian of Persian
crafts. He studied in Carrara and at the Brera in Milan (1956–59), taught at the Tehran College
of Decorative Arts (Zenderoudi was among his students) and at the Minneapolis College of Art and
Design (1961–64), then headed sculpture at Tehran University until 1979. He co-founded the Rasht
29 Club with Kamran Diba and Roxana Saba in 1967.

**Principles.**

- **A word as an object.** *Heech* ("nothing") is three nasta'liq letters, he, ye and che, cast
  as a solid figure that sits on a chair, in a cage, or in the arms of a lover.
- **The craftsman's archive.** He studied locks, rugs and salt bags as sources. *Locks from
  Iran*, Smithsonian, 1976.
- **Nothingness as content.** The Met relates his Heech to Rumi and the Sufi idea of creation
  out of nothing.

**Key works.** *Heech* series, from the 1960s. *Locks from Iran* (book), 1976. *Heech in a Cage*,
2005. *Poet Turning into Heech*, 2007.

**Remix today.** A one-word logotype can be built as a three-dimensional character, a mascot
made of script.

## Iranian Graphic Design {#iranian-graphic-design}

**Context.** Modern Iranian graphic design came out of painting studios and magazines in the
late 1950s. Momayez joined Mohammad Bahrami's atelier in 1958 (1337 in the Iranian calendar),
where he designed his first cigarette packs, book covers and the Iran Tea Organization mark. The
1960s magazine boom brought work for *Ketab-e Hafteh*, *Kavosh*, *Negin* and *Farhang*. The 1970s
golden age was underwritten by the Pahlavi court's cultural institutions under Farah Pahlavi:
Kanoon (the Institute for the Intellectual Development of Children and Young Adults, founded in
1965), the Shiraz Arts Festival (1967–1977), the Tehran International Film Festival (Momayez art
director, 1973–77), and the Tehran Museum of Contemporary Art (1977). Momayez founded the graphic
design programme at Tehran University's Faculty of Fine Arts in 1970 and that same year opened
Atelier 42 with Ali Asghar Masoumi and Farshid Mesghali. A designers' syndicate followed in 1976.

The 1979 revolution broke the patronage system. Momayez and his students had already been
designing posters for the revolution in 1978, and through the 1980s the Islamic Republic and the
Iran–Iraq war (1980–88) made the political and martyrdom poster the dominant public genre. The
profession rebuilt itself through the biennials of Iranian graphic designers, the Iranian Graphic
Designers Society (founded in 1997 with Momayez as president) and the magazine *Neshan*. A
post-1990s generation (Reza Abedini, Saed Meshki, Majid Abbasi, Ebrahim Haghighi, then the
"fifth generation" born in the 1970s and 80s, such as Mehdi Saeedi) made the Persian-script
cultural poster one of the most awarded bodies of work in the international biennial circuit.

**Principles.**

1. **The mark built from Persian letters.** A logotype grows out of the script's own rhythm.
   *Example:* Momayez, mark for the Institute of Standards and Industrial Research of Iran
   (ISIRI), 1985 (1364), which turns the initials ISIRI into the word "Iran".
2. **The poster as one concentrated image, learned partly from Poland.** *Example:* Mesghali,
   poster for Dariush Mehrjui's *Gav* (*The Cow*), 1969.
3. **Children's culture as an avant-garde laboratory.** At Kanoon, designers worked across
   illustration, animation and books. *Example:* Mesghali, illustrations for Samad Behrangi's
   *Mahi-ye siyah-e kuchulu* (*The Little Black Fish*), 1968, first prize at Bologna.
4. **Calligraphy as structure, not decoration.** Letters are enlarged and interlaced until they
   become architecture. *Example:* Ehsai's calligraphy for Momayez's Reza Abbasi Museum mark,
   1976 (1355).
5. **Script as the image itself (post-1990s).** Hand-built Persian lettering is the whole
   poster. *Example:* Reza Abedini's cultural and film posters, Fajr Film Festival best-poster
   awards from 1993.

**Key works.** Momayez, Iran Tea Organization mark, 1958. Mesghali, *The Little Black Fish*,
1968. Mesghali, *Gav*, 1969. Shiva, poster for the 3rd Shiraz Arts Festival, 1969. Momayez,
Reza Abbasi Museum mark, 1976. Momayez, ISIRI mark, 1985. Shiva, IGDS mark, 1997. Abedini,
Prince Claus Award, 2006.

**Visual vocabulary.** Nasta'liq, thuluth and shekasteh pushed to extreme scale, compression and
overlap. Flat fields of turquoise, saffron, black and red. Persian miniature, Qajar
lithographic-book and coffee-house imagery redrawn in flat modern shapes. In the contemporary
work, dense black type texture on white, with the grid ignored in favour of the letter's own
axis.

**Lineage.** Its sources are [saqqakhaneh] and [hurufiyya], the [polish-poster-school] (Momayez
read Polish magazines as a student), French decorative-arts training (Momayez at ENSAD in Paris,
1965–68) and [constructivism]. Abedini names Rodchenko, Ikko Tanaka ([japanese-modernism]) and Cieślewicz,
alongside the Qajar painters Sani-ol-Molk and Mirza Gholam-Reza Esfahani. It feeds contemporary
Persian and Arabic type and poster design across the region.

**Remix today.** Iranian design is the strongest proof that a non-Latin script can carry a whole
modern design language without a grid borrowed from Basel. The transferable lesson is to derive
the system *from the script*: its baseline, its stroke contrast, its stacking and compression.
Start there rather than adding "local flavour" to an imported layout.

### Morteza Momayez {#morteza-momayez}

Morteza Momayez (1936 – 2005, Tehran) is called the father of modern Iranian graphic design. He
studied painting at Tehran University (graduating in 1965) and then at the École Nationale
Supérieure des Arts Décoratifs in Paris (1965–68). He founded the university's graphic design
programme in 1970, was expelled from the university by SAVAK in 1971 and later returned. He was
art director of the Tehran International Film Festival (1973–77), the founding president of the
Iranian Graphic Designers Society (1997) and editor-in-chief of *Neshan*. His death day is
reported as either 25 October or 26 November 2005; see the disputed dates below.

**Principles.**

- **A mark comes from the letters' own rhythm.** His first monogram, for the Iranian
  Association of Certified Accountants (1974), began when he noticed that the initials made a
  rhythm.
- **Derive the symbol from local form.** The Tehran Municipality mark (1989) turns the city's
  twelve old gates into a flower. The SAIPA mark (1987) comes from the brick patterns of Maragheh
  buildings.
- **The designer builds the profession.** Atelier 42, a syndicate in 1976, the biennials, IGDS
  and *Neshan*.

**Key works.** Iran Tea Organization mark, 1958 (identity). Reza Abbasi Museum mark, 1976
(identity, with Ehsai). ISIRI mark, 1985 (identity). SAIPA mark, 1987 (identity). Tehran
Municipality mark, 1989 (identity). Tehran Fire Department mark, 1995 (identity).

**Quote.** "در طراحی نشانهٔ استاندارد توانستم از قلمی استفاده کنم که به خوبی حروف اختصاری ISIRI
را به کلمهٔ ایران تبدیل کند." ("In designing the standard mark I was able to use a pen that
turns the abbreviation ISIRI into the word Iran.") — Momayez, quoted in Persian Wikipedia. The
translation is mine.

**Remix today.** A national-scale identity system grown from one script, whose marks are still
on Iranian goods, fire engines and street signs decades later.

### Ghobad Shiva {#ghobad-shiva}

Ghobad Shiva (born 1941, Hamadan; English and Persian Wikipedia differ by a day on his birth
date) graduated in painting from Tehran University in 1966. He set up the graphics department of
Iranian national radio and television in 1968 and of Soroush publishers in 1971, earned an MFA
at Pratt in 1980, and has taught in Tehran since 1976. He co-founded IGDS and designed its mark
(1997), planned the first Tehran poster biennial, and is a member of AGI.

**Principles.**

- **Iranian themes in a graphic idiom.** Persian motifs are restated as flat poster
  shapes. The 3rd Shiraz Arts Festival poster, a competition winner, 1969.
- **Design departments as infrastructure.** Broadcast and publishing in-house studios were set up
  as public design.

**Key works.** 3rd Shiraz Arts Festival poster, 1969. IGDS mark, 1997 (identity). *Palestine*
poster, honoured at the first International Biennial of the Islamic World Poster, 2003.

**Remix today.** Make the broadcaster's in-house studio the place where a national visual
language is set.

### Farshid Mesghali {#farshid-mesghali}

Farshid Mesghali (born 1943, Isfahan) studied painting at Tehran University and worked at Kanoon
from 1967 (1346) to 1978, making books, animated films and posters. He co-founded Atelier 42 in
1970. He is the only Iranian to have won the Hans Christian Andersen Award (illustration, 1974).
He moved to Paris in 1979 and to Los Angeles in 1986, where he opened Desktop Studio, and later
returned to work in Tehran.

**Principles.**

- **Cross every medium.** The same designer makes the book, the film and the poster. *The Little
  Black Fish*, 1968; *Look Again*, 1974.
- **Use whatever technique the story asks for.** Lithography, collage, watercolour and
  lithographed-book style.
- **One clear image for an auteur's film.** He made posters for Mehrjui's *Gav* (1969), *Aghaye
  Hallou* (1970) and *Dayereh-ye Mina* (1974).

**Key works.** *The Little Black Fish* (book), 1968. *Gav* (poster), 1969. *Arash-e Kamangir*
(book), 1971. *Look Again* (film), 1974. Third prize, Warsaw Poster Biennale, 1977.

**Remix today.** A studio that treats picture book, motion and poster as one practice is the
model for most cultural design work now.

### Mohammad Ehsai {#mohammad-ehsai}

Mohammad Ehsai (born 1939, Qazvin) is a calligrapher trained under Gholam Hossein Amirkhani. He
studied painting and graphic design at Tehran University and taught there. He worked alongside
Momayez at the Bahrami atelier, wrote the calligraphy for Momayez's Reza Abbasi Museum mark
(1976), and is a pioneer of *naqqashi-khatt*, calligraphic painting.

**Principles.**

- **Enlarge until meaning yields to rhythm.** Letters are repeated and interlaced into monumental
  abstract structures. The *Eternal Alphabet* series.
- **Mastery first, then rupture.** He still writes traditional nasta'liq, thuluth and muhaqqaq,
  including the *Divan* of Hafez and the Quran.

**Key works.** Reza Abbasi Museum logotype calligraphy, 1976 (identity). *Eternal Alphabet* series
(other).

**Visual vocabulary.** Bright letters on dark grounds. Overlapping strokes at architectural
scale.

**Remix today.** Display type for Arabic-script brands that comes out of the calligrapher's pen,
not out of the Latin type-design workflow.

### Reza Abedini {#reza-abedini}

Reza Abedini (born 1967, Tehran) studied in Tehran (1985–90), has practised since 1989 and
founded his studio in 1993. He has taught at Tehran University since 1996 and was a visiting
professor at the American University of Beirut. He received the principal Prince Claus Award in
2006, joined AGI, and now lives in the Netherlands.

**Principles.**

- **The Persian letter as both aesthetic and concept.** The script carries the idea, not just
  the caption.
- **Reread the heritage, don't quote it.** He names Rodchenko, Ikko Tanaka and Cieślewicz
  alongside Qajar painters as his sources.
- **Dissolve design into art.** His posters double as typographic artworks.

**Key works.** Fajr International Film Festival best film poster, 1993 (poster). First prize and
gold medal, 8th International Poster Biennial, Mexico, 2004 (poster). Prince Claus Award, 2006
(other).

**Remix today.** Script-first typographic posters that read as Persian before they read as
"international".

## Hurufiyya {#hurufiyya}

**Context.** Hurufiyya (from *huruf*, "letters") is the name usually given to the mid-20th-century
movement in which artists across the Arab and Muslim world used Arabic calligraphy inside modern,
mostly abstract art. It borrows the name of a 14th-century Sufi letter-mysticism. It arose
independently in many places, often at the moment of independence from colonial rule, as a way
to find a national and pan-Arab visual identity: the Old Khartoum School in Sudan (Ibrahim
el-Salahi, Osman Waqialla), the One Dimension Group in Iraq (founded with a manifesto by Shakir
Hassan Al Said in 1971), Jordan (Wijdan Ali), Lebanon (Etel Adnan, Saloua Raouda Choucair),
Morocco, Tunisia (Nja Mahdaoui), and Saqqakhaneh in Iran. Madiha Omar's 1949 exhibition in
Washington is generally taken as its first public appearance. Some scholars, including Wijdan
Ali, Nada Shabout and Karen Dabrowska, reject the label and prefer "the calligraphic school of
art". The art historian Sandra Dagher has called it the most important movement in 20th-century
Arab art.

**Principles.**

1. **Free the letter from the rules.** Classical calligraphy had strict proportions and
   devotional uses; hurufiyya breaks, distorts and abstracts the letter. *Example:* Madiha Omar,
   22 calligraphy-based paintings, Georgetown Public Library, Washington, 1949.
2. **The letter as sign, emptied and refilled.** A character keeps its cultural link while
   losing its linguistic one. *Example:* Shakir Hassan Al Said and the One Dimension manifesto,
   1971.
3. **Geometry as a bridge between faiths.** *Example:* Kamal Boullata's silkscreens built on
   angular Kufic and the square, *Three Quartets (Opus 1–12)*, 1994.
4. **Generations.** First the nationalist pioneers, then exiles (Dia Azzawi), then a third
   generation using script in a purely abstract way (Golnaz Fathi, Lalla Essaydi).

**Key works.** Madiha Omar, Washington exhibition, 1949. One Dimension Group manifesto, 1971.
Boullata, *Three Quartets*, 1994. *Word into Art*, British Museum, 2006 (exhibition).

**Visual vocabulary.** Kufic angles and thuluth curves enlarged. Pseudo-script. Letters stacked
into grids or set floating on colour-field grounds. Silkscreen flat colour. Henna, leather and
wood instead of canvas in Sudan and Morocco.

**Lineage.** It draws on classical Islamic calligraphy, Sufi letter-mysticism and Western
abstraction. It feeds [arabic-modernism] in type and poster design, calligraffiti (eL Seed), and
[iranian-graphic-design].

**Quote.** "From a philosophical point of view, the One-Dimension is eternity, or an extension of
the past to the time before the existence of pictorial surface; to the non-surface." — Shakir
Hassan Al Said, One Dimension Group, as quoted in Wikipedia's article on the group.

**Remix today.** Hurufiyya is a working method for any script with a calligraphic tradition:
decouple the letter's shape from its reading, and you get a texture that still carries
identity. Use it with care. The third generation shows how quickly it can slide into
decoration.

### Madiha Omar {#madiha-omar}

Madiha Omar (1908 Aleppo – 2005) was an Iraqi painter of Circassian and Syrian descent. She
trained in Istanbul and at the Maria Grey Training College in London (1933), taught at the
Baghdad Academy of Fine Arts until 1942, then moved to Washington. There a book on Arabic
calligraphy by Nabia Abbott led her to put letters into abstract painting. In 1949, encouraged
by Richard Ettinghausen, she exhibited 22 such paintings, the first known exhibition of modern
art based on Arabic letters. She joined Al Said's One Dimension Group in 1971.

**Principles.**

- **The letter as an abstract element.** Calligraphy is used as an inspiration, not as text.
  *Arabic Calligraphy: An Element of Inspiration in Abstract Art*, written in 1949. The
  hurufiyya article dates its publication to 1950.
- **Secular use of a sacred script.** Her 48 paintings at the Ibn Sina exhibition in Baghdad,
  1952, all used Arabic letters in secular modern works.

**Key works.** Georgetown Public Library exhibition, 1949 (exhibition). *Arabic Calligraphy: An
Element of Inspiration in Abstract Art*, 1949 (book). Ibn Sina exhibition, Baghdad, 1952
(exhibition).

**Remix today.** She is the origin point for every Arabic-script abstract brand mark.

### Kamal Boullata {#kamal-boullata}

Kamal Boullata (1942 Jerusalem – 2019 Berlin) was a Palestinian artist and art historian. He
studied in Rome (1965) and at the Corcoran (MFA 1971), and worked in 1974 as art director of a
pioneering publishing house in Beirut. He held Fulbright fellowships in Morocco in 1993–94.
*Palestinian Art: From 1850 to the Present* (2009) is the standard history of its subject.

**Principles.**

- **The square as root.** The intersection of two squares in a circle links the octagonal star
  of Islamic art, the mandorla of the icon and Jerusalem's sacred buildings.
- **Write the image.** He took this from icon painting, where "the iconographer does not paint an
  icon he writes it".
- **Stencil precision.** French pochoir and silkscreen give crisp, flat, measured colour.

**Key works.** *Three Quartets (Opus 1–12)*, 1994 (silkscreen portfolio, with poems by Mohammed
Bennis). *Twelve Lanterns for Granada*, 1995 (book object after Adonis). *Palestinian Art: From
1850 to the Present*, 2009 (book).

**Quote.** "I keep reminding my self that Jerusalem is not behind me, it is constantly ahead of
me." — Boullata, interview, as quoted on Wikipedia.

**Remix today.** Geometric Kufic on a square grid is a ready-made bridge between a pixel grid and
an Arabic letter.

## Arabic Modernism {#arabic-modernism}

**Context.** Arabic print had a structural problem that Latin never had. Each letter takes up to
four contextual forms, and metal type and typewriters needed hundreds of sorts, so the
machine-made page always looked like a poor copy of the pen. Modernist Arabic design is the
long effort to make the script work with new technology without killing it. One line ran
through reform. Nasri Khattar's *Unified Arabic*, patented in 1947 and launched in 1957, gave
each letter a single form (33 glyphs). Linotype and later digital Arabic followed. A second line
ran through the press and the book in Cairo and Beirut, the Arab world's two publishing
capitals. Abdel Salam El Shereef (1911–1997) pioneered Egyptian press layout (*al-ikhraj
al-sahafi*). Hussein Bicar (1913–2002) illustrated Taha Hussein's *al-Ayyam* (*The Stream of
Days*) and in 1952 launched *Sindbad*, Egypt's first children's magazine. In the 1970s
Mohieddine Ellabbad and Helmi El Touni built, from Cairo and Beirut, a modern Arab book and
children's culture: Dar al-Fata al-Arabi (founded 1974), and thousands of covers. A third line
is today's type and research culture: Huda Smitshuijzen AbiFarès's *Arabic Typography* (2001)
and the Khatt Foundation (2004), Nadine Chahine's Arabic versions of Frutiger, Helvetica and
Zapfino at Linotype, and bilingual "typographic matchmaking" that pairs Arabic and Latin
designers. Polish posters were already being shown in Beirut in 1961.

**Principles.**

1. **Simplify the script for the machine and keep its identity.** *Example:* Khattar, Unified
   Arabic, 1947/1957. The type foundry 29LT revived it in 2013.
2. **The book cover as a public art.** *Example:* Helmi El Touni's more than 3,000 book covers
   for Cairo and Beirut publishers.
3. **Design for children as cultural sovereignty.** Arab children's books should be written and
   drawn in the region, not translated. *Example:* Ellabbad's Dar al-Fata al-Arabi (1974), and
   the Arab Workshop for Children's Books and Arab Graphic Center (1976).
4. **Bilingual parity.** Arabic and Latin are designed together as equals, not with Arabic
   retrofitted. *Example:* the Khatt Foundation's *Typographic Matchmaking*, 2007, which led to
   Peter Bilak and Kristyan Sarkis's TPTQ Arabic. Chahine's Koufiya was the first typeface whose
   Arabic and Latin were designed simultaneously.
5. **Research as design.** Writing the history is part of the practice. *Example:* AbiFarès,
   *Arabic Typography: A Comprehensive Sourcebook*, 2001; Yara Khoury Nammour, *Nasri Khattar: A
   Modernist Typotect*, 2014.

**Key works.** Khattar, Unified Arabic (patent), 1947. Khattar, *Shuf Baba Shuf* (primer), 1955.
Bicar, *Sindbad* (magazine), 1952. El Touni, UNICEF International Year of the Child poster,
1979. Ellabbad, *Kashkoul al-Rassam*, Golden Apple at Bratislava, 1989. AbiFarès, *Arabic
Typography*, 2001. Khatt Foundation, 2004.

**Visual vocabulary.** Monolinear and geometric Kufic-derived display faces. Naskh text faces
simplified for machines. Folk-ornament borders, Pharaonic and Coptic motifs, and popular-print
illustration in Egyptian book culture. Strong flat colour. In the bilingual era, matched x-height
and stroke weight across both scripts.

**Lineage.** It draws on [hurufiyya], [new-typography] and [swiss-style] grids (via AUB and
Western-trained designers), and the [polish-poster-school] (exhibitions in Beirut). It feeds
contemporary multi-script type design and Gulf and Levantine identity work.

**Remix today.** Treat Arabic as a primary script with its own system, not a "localisation"
of a Latin one. Design both scripts in the same session, match the colour of the text rather
than its letter shapes, and look at Cairo and Beirut book covers before reaching for generic
"Islamic pattern".

### Nasri Khattar {#nasri-khattar}

Nasri Khattar (1911–1998) was a Lebanese architect and type designer. He devoted his life to
*Unified Arabic*, a printing script that gives each letter one form by combining features of its
isolated and connected shapes, cutting the glyph count to 33. Patented in 1947 and launched in
1957, it was revived digitally by 29LT in 2013. He was nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize in
1986 for the project.

**Principles.**

- **Rationalise the system, not the letter.** Reduce the forms and keep the shapes recognisable.
- **For print only.** Handwriting was to remain unchanged; the reform was for the machine.

**Key works.** *Al-Abjadiyya al-Muwahhada*, 1947 (typeface). *Shuf Baba Shuf*, 1955 (book).

**Remix today.** Unified Arabic is a live lesson in designing for a constrained renderer, the
same problem as pixel fonts and early web shaping.

### Mohieddine Ellabbad {#mohieddine-ellabbad}

Mohieddine Ellabbad (1940–2010) was an Egyptian illustrator and graphic designer who studied
painting at the Cairo Faculty of Fine Arts (1957–62), drawing for *Sindbad* and Dar al-Maaref
while still a student. He was a newspaper cartoonist, co-founded Dar al-Fata al-Arabi (1974), and
founded the Arab Workshop for Children's Books and the Arab Graphic Center (1976).

**Principles.**

- **Visual literacy for the reader.** He wrote and designed books *about* images: *Lugha bidun
  kalimat* (*Language without Words*), *Hikayat al-kitab* (*The Story of the Book*).
- **Memory as material.** *Kashkoul al-Rassam* (*The Illustrator's Sketchbook*) is visual
  memories of childhood addressed to adults, and discoveries of the working illustrator addressed
  to children.
- **Arab publishing for Arab children.** He built the publisher, not only the pages.

**Key works.** *Kashkoul al-Rassam*, Golden Apple at the Bratislava illustration biennial 1989,
later published in French, German and Dutch (book). *Nazar* albums, four volumes (book).

**Remix today.** A designer-author writing visual-culture books for young readers is still rare
in any language.

### Helmi El Touni {#helmi-el-touni}

Helmi El Touni (1934 Beni Suef – 2024 Cairo) was an Egyptian painter and designer. He graduated
from the Cairo Faculty of Fine Arts in 1958 (stage design) and worked for three years in
Beirut. He designed more than 3,000 book covers plus magazine covers and layouts, art-directed
the first three issues of *Shumu'* (1986), and made posters for plays, films and UN agencies.

**Principles.**

- **Popular imagery, modern frame.** Folk and popular-print motifs are restated in flat, poster
  colour.
- **The cover as a repeated public gallery.** Volume made his style a shared visual language for
  Arab readers.

**Key works.** UNICEF International Year of the Child poster, 1979 (poster). Beirut International
Book Fair prizes, 1977–79 (book). *Shumu'* magazine, 1986 (magazine).

**Remix today.** A publishing house's identity can be a single illustrator's hand across a
thousand covers.

### Huda Smitshuijzen AbiFarès {#huda-smitshuijzen-abifares}

Huda Smitshuijzen AbiFarès (born 1965, Beirut) took a BFA at RISD (1987), an MFA at Yale (1990)
and a PhD at Leiden (2017). Teaching at AUB, she found the literature on Arabic typography had
not been written, so she wrote it. She founded the Khatt Foundation in Amsterdam in 2004 and its
imprint Khatt Books in 2010, and joined AGI in 2016.

**Principles.**

- **Structure before style.** She analyses Arabic typography from its structure and theory.
  *Arabic Typography*, 2001.
- **Matchmaking.** Pair Arabic and Latin designers to make genuinely bilingual families.
  *Typographic Matchmaking*, 2007.
- **Build the library.** Publish the region's design history.

**Key works.** *Arabic Typography: A Comprehensive Sourcebook*, 2001 (book). Khatt Foundation,
2004 (other). *Typographic Matchmaking*, 2007 (book). *Typographic Matchmaking in the City*, 2011
(book). *Against the Grain* (with Nagla Samir and Mouneer Al-Shaarani), 2023 (book).

**Quote (the foundation's stated aim).** Its aim was "modernising the image of Arabic design by
making it contemporary and innovative, particularly in the field of bilingual typography." —
Khatt Foundation, as quoted on Wikipedia.

**Remix today.** Any multi-script brand should start with a matchmaking brief, not a Latin
master with a localised companion.

## Disputed or uncertain dates

- Tomaszewski's Honorary RDI: 1975 (English Wikipedia) or 1976 (culture.pl, Polish Wikipedia).
- Lenica's *Labirynt*: 1962 (Polish Wikipedia) or 1963 (English Wikipedia filmography, Annecy
  1963).
- Górka's *Kabaret* poster: 1972 (English Wikipedia list) or 1973 (culture.pl). 1973 is used here.
- Starowieyski's MoMA solo show: 1985 (culture.pl) or 1986 (English Wikipedia).
- Świerzy's birth: "c. 1931" on English Wikipedia; 9 September 1931 on culture.pl and Polish
  Wikipedia.
- Momayez's death: 25 October 2005 (English Wikipedia lead) or 26 November 2005, that is 5 Azar
  1384 (Persian Wikipedia). The English article itself also says "25 November".
- Shiva's birth: 23 January 1941 (English) or 4 Bahman 1319, that is 24 January (Persian).
- Madiha Omar's *Arabic Calligraphy...*: 1949 (her article) or 1950 (the hurufiyya article).
- Cieślewicz's place of death: Paris (culture.pl) or Antony, a Paris suburb (Polish Wikipedia).
- Iranian solar-year dates (e.g. 1364) span two Gregorian years. The earlier one is used.
