# American modernism, 1934–1990s

How European modernism crossed the Atlantic in the 1930s, was naturalised by American business
and the American sense of humour, and turned into two exports of its own: the *idea-led* design of
the New York School and the corporate identity *system*. Then the counter-currents — Push Pin's
raid on history and the expressive typography that phototype made possible — and the Swiss-trained
systems modernism of Unimark and the Vignellis that answered them.

The slice runs from Brodovitch's arrival at *Harper's Bazaar* (1934) to the early 1990s, when
Pentagram's New York office and Paula Scher carried the tradition into the postmodern decades.

A note on reading the dates: years for logos are the year of introduction unless stated; where
sources disagree, the disagreement is flagged in the text.

## American Modernism {#american-modernism}

*The émigré phase, c. 1934–1960. New York, Chicago, Philadelphia, Los Angeles.*

**Context.** In the early 1930s American commercial art was still illustration-led: painted
magazine covers in the Wyeth–Pyle tradition, centred serif headlines, the realist sell. Two forces
broke it. The first was people. The Nazi seizure of power (1933) and the closing of the Bauhaus that
year scattered the European avant-garde, and a striking share of it landed in the United States:
Alexey Brodovitch (via Paris, 1930), Herbert Matter (1936), László Moholy-Nagy (New Bauhaus,
Chicago, 1937), Herbert Bayer and Walter Gropius (1937–38), Will Burtin (1938–39), Ladislav Sutnar
(1939).
The second was patronage. A handful of businessmen — Walter Paepcke of the Container Corporation of
America above all — decided that avant-garde form could make a company look modern, intelligent
and trustworthy. MoMA's *Bauhaus 1919–1928* exhibition (1938) gave the imports institutional
standing. American-born designers who had only seen this work in *Gebrauchsgraphik* — Paul Rand,
Lester Beall, Alvin Lustig — absorbed it at second hand and, crucially, without the ideology.
Technology: fast offset and gravure colour printing, cheap photostats, and the 35 mm camera, which
made candid, cropped, motion-blurred photography available to magazine pages.

**Principles.**

1. **Form without the manifesto.** The European vocabulary — asymmetry, sans-serif type, photomontage,
   primary colour, white space — is adopted as a *toolkit for clarity and impact*, not as a social
   programme. Example: Lester Beall's Rural Electrification Administration posters (1937), which use
   flat red-white-blue stripes and arrows borrowed from Constructivism to sell electric light to
   farmers.
2. **The photograph as raw material.** Photographs are cropped hard, bled off the page, scaled
   against each other and set in dialogue with type, rather than framed as pictures. Example:
   Brodovitch's spreads for *Harper's Bazaar* (from 1934), where the shape of a dancer's body sets
   the shape of the text block.
3. **White space is active.** Emptiness is the frame that makes the one element on the page loud.
   Example: Brodovitch's *Portfolio* (1950–51), whose spreads run whole pages with a single image
   and a line of type.
4. **Information as architecture.** Large bodies of facts are organised by systems of signs, tabs,
   grids and colour so a user can *navigate*, not just read. Example: Ladislav Sutnar and Knud
   Lönberg-Holm, *Catalog Design* (1944) for Sweet's Catalog Service.
5. **Enlightened patronage.** A company buys art, not just ads, and lets the artist sign it.
   Example: the Container Corporation of America's *Great Ideas of Western Man* campaign (from
   1950), commissioning Bayer, Matter, Rand and others to illustrate a quotation each.

**Key works.** Beall, REA posters (1937; photomontage series 1939). Brodovitch, *Harper's Bazaar*
(1934–58), *Ballet* (1945), *Portfolio* (1950–51). Rand, *Direction* covers (1938–45). Matter,
Knoll advertising (1946–66). Sutnar & Lönberg-Holm, *Catalog Design* (1944). Burtin, *Gunner's
Information File* (1944). Lustig, New Directions jackets (1945–55).

**Visual vocabulary.** Photomontage and photogram; bleed photographs with dramatic cropping;
asymmetric layouts with large areas of flat white or flat colour; sans-serif (Futura, Kabel,
Akzidenz) mixed freely with Bodoni and Caslon; primary colours plus black; arrows, dots and bars as
directional devices; collage and torn paper; symbolic objects (a hand, an eye, a barbed wire)
standing in for a concept.

**Lineage.** From Bauhaus, the New Typography, Constructivism, De Stijl, Dada collage, and the
German Sachplakat (Rand learned the object poster from Lucian Bernhard's generation). Fed the
New York School, corporate identity and the West Coast midcentury idiom.

**Quotes.** Carmel Snow, editor of *Harper's Bazaar*, on first seeing Brodovitch's work in 1934:
"I saw a fresh, new conception of layout technique that struck me like a revelation: pages that
'bled' beautifully cropped photographs, typography and design that were bold and arresting."
(*The World of Carmel Snow*, 1962, p. 90.)

**Remix today.** The émigrés' real trick was *translation*: taking a vocabulary invented for
revolution and making it serve a department store without losing its edge. If you work with a
borrowed visual language — Swiss, brutalist, Y2K — strip it to the tools that make things
*clearer and louder*, and drop the costume.

### Alexey Brodovitch {#alexey-brodovitch}

*1898–1971. Russian-born American art director, educator. Art director of* Harper's Bazaar
*1934–1958.*

**Context.** A White Army officer who fled the Revolution, Brodovitch learned design in 1920s Paris
painting sets for the Ballets Russes and doing layouts for *Arts et Métiers Graphiques*. In 1930 he
moved to Philadelphia to run the advertising design department of the Pennsylvania Museum School,
where his Design Laboratory (1933) became the most influential studio course in America — Richard
Avedon, Irving Penn, Diane Arbus and Garry Winogrand all passed through it. In 1934 Carmel Snow
hired him for *Harper's Bazaar*.

**Principles.**
- **Cinematic pacing.** A magazine is a sequence, not a stack of pages: spreads rhyme, accelerate
  and pause like edited film. Example: the *Harper's Bazaar* fashion portfolios of the late 1930s
  and 1940s, running Munkácsi's and later Avedon's location photographs across consecutive spreads.
- **Type takes the shape of the picture.** Text columns are cut to echo forms in the photograph — a
  leg, a skirt, a diagonal. Example: *Harper's Bazaar* layouts of the 1930s–40s.
- **Shock value on every page.** He demanded each page surprise, and told students to bring him
  something they had not seen before. Example: *Portfolio* (1950–51), three issues with no
  advertising, die-cuts and gatefolds.

**Key works.** *Harper's Bazaar* (art director, 1934–58); *Ballet* (photobook, 1945);
*Portfolio* (1950–51, with Frank Zachary and George Rosenthal); *Observations* (with Richard Avedon
and Truman Capote, 1959).

**Visual vocabulary.** Large white margins; Bodoni and Didone headlines in light weights;
photographs bled, cropped off-centre, sometimes blurred; repeated images in film-strip sequences;
second colour used flat and bold.

**Lineage.** Constructivist and Surrealist Paris → American editorial design. Taught or influenced
Avedon, Penn, Arbus, Bob Gill, Henry Wolf, Otto Storch, Art Kane, and through *Bazaar* the whole
idea of the art director as editor.

**Quotes.** From his New School course description for the Design Laboratory: "The aim of the
course is to help the student to discover his individuality, crystallize his taste, and develop
his feeling for the contemporary trend by stimulating his sense of invention and perfecting his
technical ability."

**Remix today.** Treat a scroll, a deck or a product launch as an edit: decide where the reader
breathes, where the cut is, which image is allowed to be enormous. Pacing is a layout tool.

### Herbert Matter {#herbert-matter}

*1907–1984. Swiss-born American photographer and designer.*

**Context.** Trained in Geneva and in Paris under Fernand Léger and Amédée Ozenfant, and at
Cassandre's studio, Matter returned to Zurich in 1932 to make photomontage posters for the Swiss
National Tourist Office. He emigrated in 1936, worked for Brodovitch at *Harper's Bazaar*, then
became Knoll's design consultant (1946–66) and taught photography at Yale (1952–76).

**Principles.**
- **Scale collision.** A huge close-up face beside a tiny skier makes the poster deep before you
  read a word. Example: *Pontresina Engadin* poster (1935).
- **The product as abstract sculpture.** Furniture photographed as form, shadow and line, with
  almost no copy. Example: Knoll advertisements for Saarinen and Bertoia chairs (1946–66).
- **One sign for a whole system.** Example: the New Haven Railroad identity (1954), an "NH"
  monogram applied to everything from locomotives to timetables.

**Key works.** Swiss tourism posters (1934–35); Knoll advertising (1946–66); New Haven Railroad
identity (1954); film *Works of Calder* (1950); design consultant to the Guggenheim (1958–68).

**Visual vocabulary.** Black-and-white photomontage with flat colour planes; extreme scale shifts;
cut-out photographic figures; small sans-serif type.

**Lineage.** Swiss photomontage and Cassandre → Knoll's corporate modernism → Paula Scher's 1985
Swatch parody of his Pontresina poster.

**Remix today.** Matter shows that a brand can be built almost entirely out of *how you photograph
the product*. A consistent photographic stance can do the work of a logo.

### Lester Beall {#lester-beall}

*1903–1969. American graphic designer. Chicago, then New York (1935) and Connecticut.*

**Context.** Self-taught in modernism from European magazines, Beall was one of the first
American-born designers to work fully in the new idiom. His government posters of the late 1930s
are the moment modernism became an American public language.

**Principles.**
- **Graphic signs over illustration.** Arrows, stripes and flat flags do the persuading. Example:
  REA posters *Light*, *Radio* and *Running Water* (1937).
- **Photomontage for the common good.** Documentary photographs of real farm families, cut out and
  set against flat colour. Example: REA photomontage poster of two children on a fence (1939).
- **The identity manual.** Beall was early to codify a mark's use in a standards book. Example:
  International Paper identity and manual (1960).

**Key works.** Rural Electrification Administration posters (1937, 1939); covers for *PM* and
*Collier's* (1930s–40s); International Paper logo (1960).

**Visual vocabulary.** Red, white and blue stripes; heavy arrows; slab and grotesque wood type;
silhouetted photographs; strong diagonals.

**Lineage.** New Typography and Constructivism, learned second-hand → corporate identity
(International Paper is one of the longest-lived American marks).

**Remix today.** A brief that is about a social good can carry hard-edged, abstract form if the
signs are blunt enough to read at a glance. Beall's arrows are a lesson in emotional geometry.

### Ladislav Sutnar {#ladislav-sutnar}

*1897–1976. Czech-born American designer, pioneer of information design.*

**Context.** A leading figure of the Czech avant-garde (director of the State School of Graphic
Arts in Prague from 1932), Sutnar came to New York in 1939 to design the Czechoslovak pavilion at
the World's Fair and was stranded by the German occupation. From 1941 to 1960 he was art director of
Sweet's Catalog Service, where, with Knud Lönberg-Holm, he invented much of what we now call
information architecture.

**Principles.**
- **Visual flow.** A document should be designed as a path: the eye is led by size, colour, position
  and signs from the general to the specific. Example: *Catalog Design Progress* (1950).
- **Function, not decoration.** Every graphic element — a bar, a tab, a numeral in a circle — has a
  navigational job. Example: Sweet's catalogue system (1941–60).
- **Punctuation as structure.** Brackets, rules and numerals organise information. He is credited
  with putting area codes in parentheses in the Bell System's telephone directories (late 1950s–
  early 1960s), a credit never made official.

**Key works.** *Catalog Design* (with Lönberg-Holm, 1944); *Catalog Design Progress* (1950);
*Package Design: The Force of Visual Selling* (1953); *Visual Design in Action* (1961).

**Visual vocabulary.** Modular grids; black, white and one or two primary colours; heavy sans-serif
numerals; circles, squares and arrows as wayfinding signs; diagonal emphasis.

**Lineage.** Czech avant-garde, De Stijl, Constructivism → information design, the Bell telephone
book, and by long descent interface navigation.

**Remix today.** Before the web existed, Sutnar designed *navigation*. His books are still the best
argument that a dense product — a dashboard, a docs site — should be designed as a route through
information rather than a page of it.

### Will Burtin {#will-burtin}

*1908–1972. German-born American designer. Cologne, then New York from 1938–39.*

**Context.** Burtin refused to work for the Nazi propaganda ministry (his wife Hilde was Jewish) and
emigrated (Meggs gives 1938; Wikipedia 1939, when he began teaching at Pratt). Drafted in 1943, he designed gunnery manuals for the Army Air Forces, then was released
from the Army to become art director of *Fortune* (1945–49). From 1949 he worked for the pharmaceutical
company Upjohn, art directing its magazine *Scope* and designing walk-through science exhibitions.

**Principles.**
- **Make the invisible visible.** Scientific processes are given form at human scale. Example:
  *The Cell* (1958), a 24-foot walk-in model of a red blood cell for Upjohn.
- **Teach under pressure.** Instruction must work for a frightened 19-year-old in a moving
  aircraft. Example: *Gunner's Information File: Flexible Gunnery* (1944), diagrams of fields of fire.
- **Integration.** Art, science and technology are one design problem, not three. Example: *The
  Brain* (1960), a model with flashing light pathways.

**Key works.** *Gunner's Information File* (1944); *Fortune* (1945–49); *Scope* (1949–71); *The
Cell* (1958); *The Brain* (1960); Kodak Pavilion, New York World's Fair (1964).

**Visual vocabulary.** Diagrammatic illustration; transparent overlays; scale models; schematic
colour coding; clean grotesque type.

**Lineage.** German New Typography → American scientific visualisation and exhibition design;
an ancestor of data visualisation.

**Remix today.** When explaining a complex system — a model, a pipeline, a body — build something
the audience can stand inside, even if it's only an interactive diagram. Scale is an explanation.

### Alvin Lustig {#alvin-lustig}

*1915–1955. American book, graphic and interior designer. Los Angeles, then New York.*

**Context.** Lustig studied briefly with Frank Lloyd Wright and began designing book jackets in Los
Angeles in 1937. His relationship with James Laughlin's New Directions made the paperback jacket a
site of modern art. Albers invited him to Black Mountain (1945) and Yale (1951), where he helped
shape the first graduate graphic design programme. Diabetes blinded him by 1954; he dictated designs
to assistants (Ivan Chermayeff among them) until his death in December 1955 (sources differ on the
4th or 5th).

**Principles.**
- **Symbol, not illustration.** A jacket evokes the spirit of the text with an abstract sign rather
  than depicting a scene. Example: New Directions New Classics jackets (1945–55).
- **The designer as total designer.** Lustig designed books, interiors, furniture and type, and
  argued the designer should be a generalist. Example: the Lustig Chair for Paramount Furniture
  (1949).
- **Modern art as vernacular.** Lessons from Miró, Klee and Surrealist biomorphism reach a mass
  audience on a cheap cover. Example: jacket for Tennessee Williams's *A Streetcar Named Desire*
  (New Directions, 1947).

**Key works.** New Classics jackets for New Directions (more than seventy, 1945–55); *A Streetcar
Named Desire* jacket (1947); Lustig Chair (1949); *Look* magazine visual research (1944).

**Visual vocabulary.** Biomorphic shapes and hand-drawn marks; photograms; a few flat colours;
small, carefully set type (often extended Futura or a light grotesque); title and author integrated
into the image.

**Lineage.** Bauhaus, Surrealism and the paintings of Miró and Klee → New Directions → his students
and assistants, including Chermayeff and Elaine Lustig Cohen; modern paperback design.

**Remix today.** For any cover — book, podcast, album, essay header — find the *mood-symbol*, not
the scene. Lustig's jackets are still reissued because an abstract sign ages slower than a picture.

## New York School {#new-york-school}

*American graphic design's mature mid-century style, c. 1945–1970. Term from Meggs.*

**Context.** After the war, the second generation — mostly American-born, many children of Jewish
immigrants, trained at Pratt, Cooper Union and the Art Students League — took the émigrés' toolkit
and made it argumentative, witty and fast. The setting was the booming New York magazine and
advertising business, and in 1949 Doyle Dane Bernbach paired art directors with copywriters as
equals, launching the "Creative Revolution". The New York Art Directors Club and AIGA became the
arenas. Where Swiss designers aimed at objectivity, New Yorkers aimed at the *idea* — a visual pun,
a paradox, a joke that makes the message stick.

**Principles.**
1. **The idea is the design.** A single concept, visual or verbal, carries the communication; style
   serves it. Example: Paul Rand's IBM *Eye-Bee-M* rebus poster (1981).
2. **Wit and the visual pun.** Juxtaposition and double meaning make the viewer complete the
   message. Example: Doyle Dane Bernbach's "Think Small" Volkswagen ad (1959, art director Helmut
   Krone), a tiny car in a sea of white.
3. **Word and image as one.** Copy and picture are conceived together, often by an art director and
   copywriter in the same room. Example: George Lois's *Esquire* covers (1962–72).
4. **The handmade inside the modern.** Collage, children's drawing, brush lettering and torn paper
   loosen the grid. Example: Saul Bass's *Man with the Golden Arm* poster (1955), cut paper and
   hand-lettered type.
5. **Simplify to the symbol.** Reduce a complex product to one memorable sign. Example: William
   Golden's CBS eye (1951).

**Key works.** Rand, *Thoughts on Design* (1947); Golden, CBS eye (1951); Bass, *The Man with the
Golden Arm* (1955); DDB, "Think Small" (1959); Lois, *Esquire* covers (1962–72); Lou Dorfsman's CBS
advertising (1950s–60s).

**Visual vocabulary.** Asymmetric, airy compositions; one dominant image; collage and torn paper;
childlike or brush drawing next to clean sans-serif or Bodoni; a strong flat colour (often red);
photography used for argument, not glamour; lots of white.

**Lineage.** American modernism and European Dada/Surrealist collage → corporate identity, the
Creative Revolution in advertising, Push Pin (as its illustrative wing), and the British "Fletcher/
Forbes/Gill" wit that became Pentagram.

**Quotes.** Paul Rand: "ideas do not need to be esoteric to be original or exciting." (*A
Designer's Art*, 1985.)

**Remix today.** In a time of systems and templates, the New York School is the reminder that a
system without an idea is wallpaper. Ask what the single, compressible concept is — the thing someone
would describe to a friend — before choosing a single typeface.

### Paul Rand {#paul-rand}

*1914–1996. American graphic designer, educator. Born Peretz Rosenbaum, Brooklyn.*

**Context.** Self-taught from *Gebrauchsgraphik*, Rand was art directing *Esquire*'s fashion pages at
23 and designing *Direction* covers for free in exchange for total freedom (1938–45). His book
*Thoughts on Design* (1947) gave the American profession its theory. From the mid-1950s he turned to
corporate identity, with IBM (1956) as consultant to Eliot Noyes's design programme. He taught at
Yale 1956–69 and 1974–85.

**Principles.**
- **The idea.** Rand's first principle: a design starts from a concept that reconciles the client's
  problem with a visual form; without it, everything else is decoration. Example: *Eye-Bee-M* poster
  (1981), the corporate name restated as a rebus — eye, bee, striped M.
- **Play.** Rand treated design as a game with rules — wit, surprise, the visual pun — and wrote
  that the "play instinct" is what makes a problem solvable and memorable (his essay "Design and the
  Play Instinct", in Kepes's *Education of Vision*, 1965). Example: his children's books with Ann
  Rand, *I Know a Lot of Things* (1956).
- **Form and content.** Content is what is said; form is how. Good design is their fusion, and the
  designer's job is to make the commonplace strange. Example: the December 1940 *Direction* cover,
  barbed wire wrapping the magazine as both gift and crucifix.
- **Simplicity and restraint (the mutilation test).** A mark must survive blurring, reduction and
  misuse. Example: Westinghouse (1960), a circle, three dots and a "W" that read at any size; the
  eight-stripe IBM (1972).

**Key works.** *Direction* covers (1938–45); *Thoughts on Design* (1947); IBM logo (1956, striped
1972); Westinghouse (1960); UPS shield and parcel (1961); ABC (1962); *Eye-Bee-M* poster (1981);
*A Designer's Art* (1985); NeXT (1986).

**Visual vocabulary.** Collage and torn paper; childlike drawing; a few flat colours (red, black,
blue); geometric marks (circles, slab serifs, stripes); Bodoni and City Medium; playful spatial
tension in otherwise spare layouts.

**Lineage.** Cassandre, Moholy-Nagy, Klee, Le Corbusier, Dewey's *Art as Experience* → the American
corporate identity profession and every Yale-trained designer of the 1960s–80s.

**Quotes.** From *A Designer's Art* (1985): a logo "cannot survive unless it is designed with the
utmost simplicity and restraint"; and "The problem of the artist is to defamiliarize the ordinary."

**Remix today.** Rand's IBM stripes and UPS parcel are reminders that a corporate mark can be
*playful* and still serious. Run your own logo through his mutilation test: blur it, shrink it, fax
it, render it as a favicon. Then ask if it still has a joke in it.

### Saul Bass {#saul-bass}

*1920–1996. American graphic designer and filmmaker. Los Angeles.*

**Context.** Bronx-born and trained by György Kepes at Brooklyn College, Bass moved to Los Angeles
and made film advertising his territory. Otto Preminger let him design both the poster and the
titles for *Carmen Jones* (1954), and then *The Man with the Golden Arm* (1955), whose cut-paper arm
changed what a title sequence could be. With Elaine Bass (married 1961) he made titles for
Hitchcock, Kubrick and later Scorsese; his firm designed some of America's longest-lived logos.

**Principles.**
- **Symbolize and summarize.** Reduce a whole film or company to one graphic sign. Example: the
  jagged arm of *The Man with the Golden Arm* (1955), one image used on poster, titles and ads.
- **Titles as prologue.** The credit sequence sets mood and theme before the first scene. Example:
  *Vertigo* (1958), spirals emerging from an eye.
- **Making the ordinary extraordinary.** Familiar things seen anew. Example: *Walk on the Wild Side*
  titles (1962), a cat turned predator.
- **Marks that outlive companies.** Simple, symbolic logos built for decades. Example: the Bell
  System bell (1969); United Airlines tulip (1974).

**Key works.** *The Man with the Golden Arm* (1955); *Vertigo* (1958); *Anatomy of a Murder* (1959);
*North by Northwest* titles (1959); *Psycho* titles (1960); *Why Man Creates* (1968); Bell System
(1969); United Airlines (1974); AT&T globe (1983; some sources date it 1982); *Goodfellas* titles
(1990).

**Visual vocabulary.** Cut-paper silhouettes with ragged edges; hand-drawn, slightly off-kilter
lettering; flat orange, red and black; a single fragmented figure; kinetic type.

**Lineage.** Kepes and Bauhaus → New York School → motion graphics, title design (Kyle Cooper,
*Mad Men*'s titles), and the "mid-century" poster revival.

**Quotes.** "Design is thinking made visible." (In Pat Kirkham and Jennifer Bass, *Saul Bass: A
Life in Film & Design*, 2011.) Bass called his aim "making the ordinary extraordinary" (*Bass on
Titles*, 1977).

**Remix today.** For any motion system — onboarding, app launch, a talk opener — Bass's rule holds:
one graphic metaphor, cut by hand, carried across poster, motion and logo. Consistency of *idea*
across media beats consistency of layout.

### Bradbury Thompson {#bradbury-thompson}

*1911–1995. American graphic designer, art director, educator.*

**Context.** From Topeka, Kansas, Thompson came to New York in 1938 and for 24 years designed
*Westvaco Inspirations for Printers* (1938–62), a paper-company promotion he turned into a
laboratory. He art-directed *Mademoiselle* (1945–59), taught at Yale (1956–95), and designed over
120 US postage stamps as design coordinator for the Citizens' Stamp Advisory Committee (1969–78).

**Principles.**
- **Process as image.** Printing's own materials — the four-colour separations, letterpress
  plates, halftone dots — become the illustration. Example: *Westvaco Inspirations* spreads (1940s–
  50s), overprinting process colours to build images.
- **Old and new together.** Antique engravings sit beside modernist type and colour. Example:
  *Westvaco Inspirations*, which borrowed museum plates and 19th-century cuts.
- **Reform the alphabet.** Question even the two-case system. Example: *Alphabet 26* (1950), a
  unicase alphabet with one form per letter.
- **Read as spoken.** Break lines by phrase, not by measure. Example: the *Washburn College Bible*
  (1979), set in 14-point Sabon flush-left with lines broken by cadence.

**Key works.** *Westvaco Inspirations* (1938–62); *Mademoiselle* (1945–59); *Alphabet 26* (1950);
US postage stamps (1969–78); *Washburn College Bible* (1979); *The Art of Graphic Design* (1988).

**Visual vocabulary.** Overprinted transparent process colours; huge single letters as forms;
old engravings collaged with modern type; strict but playful grids; Caslon and Baskerville next to
Futura.

**Lineage.** New Typography and the American printing tradition → New York School → expressive
typography; anticipates the layering of 1980s postmodern design.

**Remix today.** Thompson's method — make the *production process* visible — maps directly onto
digital work: show the RGB channels, the vector handles, the grid. And his Bible is a model for
any reading interface: break text where the voice breaks.

### Cipe Pineles {#cipe-pineles}

*1908–1991. Austrian-born American art director and illustrator.*

**Context.** Born in Vienna and raised in Brooklyn, Pineles joined Condé Nast in 1932 as an
assistant to M. F. Agha, became art director of *Glamour* (1942), and was the first woman to hold
the art directorship of a major American mass-market magazine. At *Seventeen* (1947–50), *Charm*
(1950–59) and *Mademoiselle* (from 1959) she defined magazines aimed at young and working women. She
was the first woman member of the New York Art Directors Club (Wikipedia gives 1943; other accounts
give 1948), and she was married first to William Golden (CBS) and then Will Burtin.

**Principles.**
- **Fine art in mass media.** She commissioned serious painters to illustrate fiction. Example:
  *Seventeen* (1947–50), which ran illustrations by Ben Shahn, Ad Reinhardt and Andy Warhol.
- **Design for the reader's real life.** *Charm*, "the magazine for women who work", photographed
  fashion in offices and on streets rather than in fantasy settings. Example: *Charm* (1950–59).
- **The hand as voice.** Her own painted, hand-lettered illustration made a warm counterweight to
  photographic glamour. Example: *Leave Me Alone with the Recipes*, her illustrated cookbook
  (made in the 1940s, published 2017).

**Key works.** *Glamour* (from 1942); *Seventeen* (1947–50); *Charm* (1950–59); *Mademoiselle*
(from 1959); Lincoln Center graphics (1960s); Parsons publication design (from 1963).

**Visual vocabulary.** Painterly and naïve illustration; hand lettering mixed with classical
typography; informal photography; warm colour.

**Lineage.** Agha's Condé Nast modernism → a more humane American editorial design; a model for
women art directors after her.

**Remix today.** Pineles designed for a specific, newly visible audience and commissioned artists
who were not "illustrators". For any editorial product, ask who it is newly for, and which outside
artists could speak to them.

### George Lois {#george-lois}

*1931–2022. American art director and advertising man.*

**Context.** A Bronx-born Greek-American, Lois worked under Bill Bernbach at DDB, co-founded Papert
Koenig Lois (1960) and became the loudest voice of the Creative Revolution. His *Esquire* covers
(1962–72; some sources say 1973) are among the most celebrated magazine covers ever made; MoMA
showed 32 of them in 2008.

**Principles.**
- **The Big Idea.** One provocative, instantly graspable concept beats a hundred well-made layouts.
  Example: Muhammad Ali as Saint Sebastian, *Esquire*, April 1968.
- **Image as editorial.** A cover is an argument, not a decoration. Example: Andy Warhol drowning
  in a Campbell's soup can, *Esquire*, May 1969.
- **Say it in the audience's voice.** Example: "I Want My MTV" campaign (1982).

**Key works.** *Esquire* covers (1962–72); "I Want My MTV" (1982); *What's the Big Idea?* (book,
1991).

**Visual vocabulary.** A single staged photograph or composite, minimal or no type on the image,
cover lines pushed to the margins; shock, satire and celebrity.

**Lineage.** DDB's art director–copywriter team → the idea-first magazine cover, Tibor Kalman's
*Colors*, and contemporary viral covers.

**Remix today.** Lois covers are the ancestors of the thumbnail and the share card. In a feed, a
staged, single-idea image still outperforms the polished layout.

## Midcentury Modern (West Coast and the Eames era) {#midcentury-modern}

*c. 1941–1975. Los Angeles; Zeeland, Michigan (Herman Miller); New York (Knoll).*

**Context.** Wartime research into moulded plywood, fibreglass and aluminium gave designers new
materials, and the postwar housing boom gave them a market. Furniture companies — Herman Miller
under George Nelson (design director from 1945) and Knoll — became total-design patrons, needing
graphics, showrooms, films and exhibitions as well as chairs. Los Angeles, with the Case Study House
programme (from 1945), offered a sunnier, more experimental modernism than New York, and a Hollywood
industry for designers like Bass. The Eames Office (1943–1988) became its laboratory.

**Principles.**
1. **The material leads the form.** New processes produce new shapes and the design shows how it was
   made. Example: Charles and Ray Eames, LCW moulded-plywood chair (1946).
2. **Design within constraints.** The designer's skill is recognising and loving the constraints.
   Example: the Eames House (1949), built from off-the-shelf industrial parts.
3. **One design discipline across media.** The same office makes chairs, toys, films, exhibitions
   and graphics. Example: Eames, *Mathematica: A World of Numbers... and Beyond* exhibition (1961).
4. **Play as method.** Toys, games and humour as serious design research. Example: Eames, *House of
   Cards* (1952).
5. **Explain with film and exhibition.** Complex ideas taught through multi-screen film and
   walk-through shows. Example: *Glimpses of the USA* (1959), seven screens at the American National
   Exhibition in Moscow.

**Key works.** Eames LCW (1946), Eames House (1949), Lounge Chair (1956), *Powers of Ten* (1977);
Herbert Matter's Knoll ads (1946–66); Lustig's Los Angeles work (1937–44); Louis Danziger's work
for Dreyfus and LACMA (1956–71).

**Visual vocabulary.** Primary colours with natural wood; organic curves and splayed legs;
collections of found objects photographed in grids; playful sans-serif and stencil lettering;
multi-image slide sequences.

**Lineage.** Bauhaus and Cranbrook (where the Eameses met, 1940) → Scandinavian and Italian design
dialogues, Apple's industrial design, the "mid-century modern" revival. Influenced by and parallel to
functionalist product design in Europe.

**Quotes.** Charles Eames, asked whether design admits constraint: "Design depends largely on
constraints." (*Design Q&A*, 1972, from interviews for the 1969 exhibition *Qu'est-ce que le design?*
at the Musée des Arts Décoratifs, Paris.)

**Remix today.** The Eames Office is the original multidisciplinary product studio: furniture,
film, data and toys under one roof. Its lesson for product teams is that the *explanation* of a thing
— the film, the exhibit, the demo — is part of the design.

### Charles and Ray Eames {#charles-and-ray-eames}

*Charles Eames 1907–1978; Ray Eames 1912–1988. American designers. The Eames Office, Venice, Los
Angeles, 1943–1988.* (Entry dates are the office's.)

**Context.** Charles, an architect, and Ray Kaiser, a painter trained under Hans Hofmann, met at
Cranbrook and married in 1941. Their wartime moulded-plywood leg splints for the US Navy (1942)
developed the technique behind their first furniture. Herman Miller produced their designs from the
late 1940s. Through the 1950s–70s the office made more than 100 films and exhibitions, often for
IBM.

**Principles.**
- **Learning by doing.** Prototype endlessly; the model is the argument. Example: the plywood
  experiments that led to the LCW (1946).
- **The best for the most for the least.** Industrial production in the service of good design for
  ordinary people. Example: Eames Molded Fiberglass Armchair (1950).
- **Powers of scale.** Understanding comes from seeing a system at every scale. Example: *Powers of
  Ten* (1977; first version 1968).
- **Delight in the collection.** Found objects, toys and folk art as sources of form. Example: *House
  of Cards* (1952).

**Key works.** LCW chair (1946); Eames House (1949); *House of Cards* (1952); Lounge Chair and
Ottoman (1956); *Glimpses of the USA* (1959); *Mathematica* (1961); IBM Pavilion, New York World's
Fair (1964); *Design Q&A* (1972); *Powers of Ten* (1977).

**Visual vocabulary.** Primary colours on white; plywood, wire, fibreglass; multi-screen image
grids; lists and collections; a cheerful, informal sans-serif voice.

**Lineage.** Cranbrook, Saarinen, Bauhaus → Herman Miller, IBM's corporate design programme, and
the modern explanatory film and data-driven exhibition.

**Quotes.** Charles Eames: "One could describe Design as a plan for arranging elements to accomplish
a particular purpose." (*Design Q&A*, 1972.)

**Remix today.** *Powers of Ten* is the original zoomable interface and still the best model for
explaining scale in data. Build your explainers as journeys through scale.

### Louis Danziger {#louis-danziger}

*Born 1923. American graphic designer and educator. Los Angeles.* (Wikipedia lists him as living;
no death date found at time of writing.)

**Context.** Danziger studied under Alvin Lustig at the Art Center School and with Brodovitch at the
New School, opened a practice in Los Angeles in 1949, and became the West Coast's most rigorous
modernist. He largely retired from studio work in 1972 to teach — at Chouinard, CalArts, Art
Center and Harvard — and taught design history to generations. AIGA Medal 1998.

**Principles.**
- **Constructivist form for American advertising.** Diagonals, photography and type as structure,
  applied to ordinary products. Example: house campaign for Dreyfus Advertising Agency (1956).
- **Clarity as intelligence.** Ads that respect the reader enough to say one thing. Example: print
  ad for the Container Corporation of America (1958).
- **The museum catalogue as argument.** Example: catalogue for *Art and Technology*, Los Angeles
  County Museum of Art (1971).

**Key works.** Flax Artist's Materials identity (1952); Dreyfus campaign (1956); CCA ad (1958);
LACMA *New York School* poster and catalogue (1965); *Art and Technology* catalogue (1971).

**Visual vocabulary.** Asymmetric photo-typographic layouts; sans-serif headlines; diagonals; dense
small text blocks; flat primary accents.

**Lineage.** Lustig, Brodovitch, Constructivism → Los Angeles modernism; his students at CalArts
fed the 1980s West Coast new wave.

**Remix today.** Danziger is proof that a designer's longest-lasting work can be teaching history. If
your team keeps reinventing the same solutions, teach them where the solutions came from.

## Corporate Identity {#corporate-identity}

*The American corporate identity programme, c. 1951–1990s. New York, Chicago, Los Angeles.*

**Context.** Postwar American companies became national and multinational, operating trucks,
stations, packaging, annual reports and — after 1950 — television. A single, consistent visual
system became a management tool. Thomas Watson Jr. hired Eliot Noyes as consultant director of
design at IBM in 1956, with Rand on graphics and the Eameses on films and exhibits: the first total
corporate design programme in America, modelled partly on Olivetti. CBS's eye (William Golden, 1951)
proved a symbol could carry a brand on the new medium. By the 1960s firms like Chermayeff & Geismar,
Lippincott & Margulies, Saul Bass & Associates and Unimark were redesigning the visual face of
corporate America.

**Principles.**
1. **The mark as irreducible symbol.** A simple, often abstract mark can stand for a vast, complex
   company. Example: Chermayeff & Geismar's Chase Manhattan octagon (1960), one of the first purely
   abstract corporate symbols.
2. **The standards manual.** Rules for the mark's use — colours, typefaces, spacing, applications —
   are written down so thousands of people can apply it consistently. Example: Saul Bass's Bell
   System identity manual (1969).
3. **Total design.** Graphics, products, architecture and environment speak one language. Example:
   IBM's design programme under Noyes and Rand (from 1956).
4. **Longevity over fashion.** A mark designed for decades, not a campaign. Example: Mobil (1964),
   with its single red "o".
5. **Name, mark, voice.** The identity often changes the name itself — Esso to Exxon (1972), "Bell"
   to the bell — so mark and name are designed together.

**Key works.** Golden, CBS eye (1951); Rand, IBM (1956/1972); Rand, Westinghouse (1960); Chermayeff &
Geismar, Chase (1960) and Mobil (1964); Rand, UPS (1961) and ABC (1962); Unimark/Vignelli, American
Airlines (1967); Bass, Bell System (1969); Danne & Blackburn, NASA "worm" (1975).

**Visual vocabulary.** Geometric marks built from circles, squares and stripes; heavy sans-serif or
modified slab wordmarks; one or two flat corporate colours; generous clear space; extensive
application grids for vehicles, signs and stationery.

**Lineage.** Peter Behrens's AEG work (1907), Olivetti, the New Typography and Swiss Style → American
systems modernism, global branding consultancies, and ultimately design systems.

**Quotes.** Paul Rand on logos: "cannot survive unless it is designed with the utmost simplicity and
restraint." (*A Designer's Art*, 1985.)

**Remix today.** A modern design system is a corporate identity manual with components. The
mid-century lesson worth keeping is ruthlessness: a few elements, defined absolutely, so that
everything else can be free.

### Chermayeff & Geismar {#chermayeff-geismar}

*Founded 1957, New York, as Brownjohn, Chermayeff & Geismar; Chermayeff & Geismar from 1959–60;
Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv since 2013. Active.*

**Context.** Two Yale graduates, Ivan Chermayeff and Tom Geismar, founded the firm with Robert
Brownjohn, a protégé of Moholy-Nagy and Serge Chermayeff at the New Bauhaus. Brownjohn left for
London in 1959. The firm became the archetype of the American identity consultancy: more than 100
marks for corporations, broadcasters and cultural institutions, plus exhibitions (US pavilions at
the 1967 and 1970 World's Fairs).

**Principles.**
- **Memorable, appropriate, simple.** A mark must be all three; the abstract is allowed when the
  company can't be pictured. Example: Chase octagon (1960).
- **Type as the mark.** A wordmark with one distinctive gesture. Example: Mobil (1964), with the red
  "o".
- **Refresh, don't replace.** Evolve heritage marks rather than discard them. Example: the NBC
  peacock (1986), simplifying the earlier peacock to six feathers.

**Key works.** *Watching Words Move* (1959); Chase (1960); Mobil (1964); Xerox (1968); PBS (1984);
NBC (1986); Smithsonian (1998).

**Visual vocabulary.** Geometric abstract symbols; custom sans-serif wordmarks; one bright colour;
collage posters for cultural clients.

**Lineage.** Lustig, Rand, New Bauhaus → contemporary logo design (Sagi Haviv's partnership keeps
the practice going).

**Remix today.** Their marks pass the test that matters for app icons and favicons: they read at
16 pixels. If yours doesn't, simplify the idea, not just the drawing.

### Ivan Chermayeff {#ivan-chermayeff}

*1932–2017. British-born American designer and collagist.* (Death given as 2 or 3 December 2017.)

**Context.** Son of the architect Serge Chermayeff, Ivan grew up in Britain and the US, studied at
Harvard, the Institute of Design in Chicago and Yale, and apprenticed with the nearly blind Alvin
Lustig. Alongside his identity work he made hundreds of collage posters and children's books.

**Principles.**
- **Collage as found wit.** Scraps of everyday material assembled into a portrait or a scene.
  Example: his posters for Mobil-sponsored arts programming on public television (1970s–80s).
- **Words that move.** Typography should act out its meaning. Example: *Watching Words Move* (with
  Brownjohn and Geismar, 1959).
- **Teach shapes first.** Example: *First Shapes* (1991), a children's book of basic forms.

**Key works.** *Watching Words Move* (1959); Mobil identity (with Geismar, 1964); *First Shapes*
(1991); collage posters for Mobil, Pan Am and the Museum of Modern Art.

**Visual vocabulary.** Torn and cut paper, gloves, envelopes and labels; bright primaries; minimal
type.

**Lineage.** Lustig, Matisse's cut-outs, Rand's collage → the playful branch of identity design.

**Remix today.** Chermayeff's posters show that a strict identity can coexist with loose,
handmade campaign work. Separate the mark (fixed) from the expression (free).

### Pentagram {#pentagram}

*Founded 1972, London; New York office 1978. Active.*

**Context.** Pentagram grew out of Fletcher/Forbes/Gill (1962) and Crosby/Fletcher/Forbes (1965).
In 1972 Alan Fletcher, Theo Crosby, Colin Forbes, Kenneth Grange and Mervyn Kurlansky formed a
partnership of equals across graphics, product and architecture. Forbes opened the New York office
in 1978; Michael Bierut and Paula Scher joined it in 1990 and 1991, making it the most important
heir of the New York School. It is included here for that New York office.

**Principles.**
- **A partnership of designers, not an agency.** Each partner leads their own clients; profits are
  shared equally. Example: Pentagram's founding structure (1972).
- **The idea, again.** Wit and concept from the Fletcher/Forbes/Gill tradition, carried into
  identity. Example: Paula Scher's Public Theater identity (1994).
- **Identity as voice, not just mark.** Typography and tone carry a brand as much as a logo.
  Example: Michael Bierut's Saks Fifth Avenue identity (2007), a script mark cut into 64 tiles.

**Key works.** Founding (1972); New York office (1978); Public Theater (1994); Citi (1998); Saks
Fifth Avenue (2007).

**Visual vocabulary.** No house style by design; across partners, a shared preference for
typographic ideas, wit and clarity.

**Lineage.** New York School wit via Fletcher/Forbes/Gill, plus corporate identity rigour →
contemporary independent studios.

**Quotes.** Colin Forbes on choosing partners, in his essay "Transition": "We spend our working
lives together; we should like each other."

**Remix today.** Pentagram's structure — small autonomous teams under shared standards and shared
profit — is still one of the most copied models for a design organisation.

## American Systems Modernism {#us-systems-modernism}

*Swiss-influenced US systems design, c. 1965–1985. Chicago, New York, Washington.*

**Context.** By the mid-1960s the Swiss International Typographic Style, with Helvetica (1957)
newly available in the US, offered a rationalist alternative to New York's wit. Unimark
International (founded 1965 in Chicago) industrialised it: a design firm organised like a
corporation, selling grids, typefaces and manuals rather than artistic personality. Public
institutions followed. The New York City Transit Authority commissioned the subway signage system
(manual 1970) and the Vignelli diagram (1972); after a May 1972 memorandum from President Nixon,
the National Endowment for the Arts ran the Federal Design Improvement Program, whose Federal
Graphics Improvement Program (from 1974) brought modernist identities to NASA, the Department of
Labor and others; the Department of Transportation commissioned AIGA's 34 Symbol Signs (1974), and
the National Park Service adopted Vignelli's Unigrid (1977).

**Principles.**
1. **The grid as operating system.** Every piece is built on a modular grid so that many hands
   produce consistent results. Example: National Park Service Unigrid brochures (1977).
2. **A limited typeface set.** Few faces, used with discipline. Example: Unimark's near-exclusive use
   of Helvetica in the NYCTA Graphics Standards Manual (1970).
3. **Information over expression.** The designer is a problem-solver, not an artist. Example: AIGA
   / DOT Symbol Signs (1974), 34 pictograms drawn by Cook and Shanosky from a survey of existing
   systems.
4. **Abstraction for legibility.** Diagram rather than map; geometry rather than geography.
   Example: Vignelli's New York City Subway diagram (1972).
5. **Design as public service.** Government should look as good as the best corporations. Example:
   Danne & Blackburn's NASA logotype (1975) and graphics standards manual (1976).

**Key works.** Unimark, NYCTA *Graphics Standards Manual* (1970); Vignelli, NYC subway diagram
(1972); AIGA/DOT Symbol Signs (1974); Danne & Blackburn, NASA (1975); NPS Unigrid (1977).

**Visual vocabulary.** Helvetica Medium white on black or dark grey; strict modular grids; colour-
coded lines and bullets; pictograms with uniform stroke weight; generous margins; bands of black at
the top of formats.

**Lineage.** Swiss Style and Ulm, via Italian design (the Vignellis, Bob Noorda's Milan Metro, 1964)
→ wayfinding, government design (US Web Design System, 18F), and design systems.

**Quotes.** Massimo Vignelli: "Design without discipline is anarchy, an exercise of
irresponsibility." (*The Vignelli Canon*, 2010.)

**Remix today.** This is the direct ancestor of every design system and wayfinding standard. Its
underrated lesson: the manual is a *political* document — it lets a public institution behave
consistently across thousands of contractors.

### Unimark International {#unimark-international}

*1965–1977. International design firm headquartered in Chicago.*

**Context.** Founded by Ralph Eckerstrom (formerly design director of the Container Corporation),
Massimo and Lella Vignelli, Bob Noorda, James Fogelman, Wally Gutches and Larry Klein, with Jay
Doblin joining almost at once and Herbert Bayer on the board. At its height it had offices in
Chicago, New York, Milan, London and elsewhere and was described as the largest design firm in the
world. It downsized in 1972 and filed for bankruptcy in 1977.

**Principles.**
- **Design as a business discipline.** Not the designer-as-artist but standardised method. Example:
  the firm's corporate structure and its manuals for Ford, JCPenney and others.
- **Helvetica and the grid as defaults.** Example: NYCTA *Graphics Standards Manual* (1970),
  Vignelli and Noorda.
- **Identity systems across touchpoints.** Example: American Airlines identity (1967).

**Key works.** *Dot Zero* magazine (1966–68); Knoll graphics (from 1966); American Airlines (1967);
NYCTA *Graphics Standards Manual* (1970).

**Visual vocabulary.** Helvetica in two or three weights; black bands; strict grids; primary-colour
codes.

**Lineage.** CCA's patronage, Swiss Style, Italian modernism → corporate identity firms, wayfinding.

**Remix today.** Unimark shows both sides of systematisation: it scaled good design, then diluted
it as marketing took over — the reason Vignelli resigned in 1971. Guard the craft as the system
grows.

### Massimo Vignelli {#massimo-vignelli}

*1931–2014. Italian-born designer. Milan, then New York from 1966.*

**Context.** Vignelli trained in architecture in Milan and Venice and started as a draughtsman for
the Castiglioni brothers at 16, where he learned that a designer should work "from the spoon to the
city". He came to New York to co-found Unimark's office, left in 1971, and with Lella founded
Vignelli Associates. He published *The Vignelli Canon* as a free e-book in 2009 (printed edition
2010), and the archive is at RIT's Vignelli Center for Design Studies.

**Principles (the Vignelli Canon).**
- **Semantics, syntactics, pragmatics.** First find the meaning of the project (semantics), then
  give it a consistent grammar of grid, type and colour (syntactics), then make sure it is
  understood (pragmatics). Example: the NYCTA signage (1970), coded by line and destination.
- **Discipline and appropriateness.** Self-imposed rules applied to every detail; the solution must
  be appropriate to the problem rather than to a style. Example: the NPS Unigrid (1977).
- **Ambiguity, used sparingly.** A design may carry a plurality of meanings, but contradiction
  usually means loss of control. Example: the American Airlines mark (1967), where the paired "AA"
  and the eagle between them read as letters and emblem at once.
- **A limited typeface set.** Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded and Helvetica (he would add a few:
  Futura, Univers, Optima, Caslon, Baskerville); "it is not the type but what you do with it".
  Example: the 1972 subway diagram, Helvetica throughout.

**Key works.** Heller stacking dinnerware (with Lella, 1964); American Airlines (1967); NYCTA
*Graphics Standards Manual* (with Bob Noorda, 1970); New York City Subway diagram (1972);
Bloomingdale's identity (1972); NPS Unigrid (1977); *The Vignelli Canon* (2010).

**Visual vocabulary.** Helvetica and Bodoni; black, white and one strong red; thick black rules and
bands; modular grids with big margins; 45° and 90° lines.

**Lineage.** Castiglioni and Italian rationalism, Swiss Style → Unimark, US systems design, every
transit map that has followed.

**Quotes.** From *The Vignelli Canon* (2010): "We love complexities but hate complications!"; "I
don't believe that when you write dog the type should bark!"; "Design is one — it is not many
different ones."

**Remix today.** The Canon is short, free and still the clearest statement of a design-system
philosophy. Its "tangibles" (paper sizes, grids, margins, type) map to tokens; its "intangibles"
(semantics, discipline, appropriateness) are the governance most systems lack.

### Lella Vignelli {#lella-vignelli}

*1934–2016. Italian-born architect and designer. Milan, then New York.*

**Context.** Born Elena Valle in Udine into a family of architects, she qualified as an architect,
held a fellowship at MIT (1958), and worked in Skidmore, Owings & Merrill's interiors department in
Chicago (1959). She was a founder of Unimark and head of its interiors department, then co-founder,
Executive Vice President and later CEO of Vignelli Associates (1971) and president of Vignelli
Designs (1978). Her role was long under-credited; the RIT archive and recent exhibitions have
corrected that.

**Principles.**
- **Design is one (in three dimensions).** The same discipline governs a plate, a chair and a
  showroom. Example: Heller stacking melamine dinnerware (1964, Compasso d'Oro).
- **Sprezzatura.** Rigour that looks effortless. Example: the Handkerchief chair for Knoll (1983;
  some sources say 1985), a moulded shell that reads as a dropped cloth.
- **The office as a design.** Running the practice — clients, money, people — is part of the work.
  Example: Vignelli Associates (from 1971).

**Key works.** Heller dinnerware (1964); Knoll graphics review (from 1965–66); Handkerchief chair
(1983); interiors for Vignelli Associates projects (1970s–90s).

**Visual vocabulary.** Primary geometric volumes; stacking forms; a black-white-red palette;
materials shown honestly.

**Lineage.** Italian postwar architecture → American interiors and product design; a model for the
designer-executive.

**Remix today.** Lella's career is a reminder that the person who makes the studio work *is* a
designer of it. Credit the operator.

## Push Pin Studios {#push-pin}

*New York, 1954–1980s (the studio continues as The Pushpin Group).*

**Context.** By the mid-1950s, Swiss and New York modernism had set into a correct, pared-down norm.
A group of Cooper Union graduates — Seymour Chwast, Edward Sorel and Reynold Ruffins, soon joined by
Milton Glaser on his return from Italy — formed Push Pin Studios (1954; some accounts give 1955) and
went the other way: back into history, Victoriana, Art Nouveau, Art Deco, comic books and woodcuts.
Their mailer, *The Push Pin Graphic* (from 1957), became an international influence; by the 1970
Louvre exhibition at the Musée des Arts Décoratifs, the "Push Pin style" had reached Europe.

**Principles.**
1. **History as a toolbox.** Any past style can be revived, reinterpreted and combined. Example:
   Milton Glaser's Bob Dylan poster (1966), Art Nouveau curves and a Duchamp silhouette.
2. **Illustration is design.** The illustrator-designer integrates drawing, type and idea into one
   object. Example: Seymour Chwast, *End Bad Breath* anti-war poster (1967).
3. **Wit over neutrality.** Humour and irony replace objectivity. Example: *The Push Pin Graphic*
   themed issues (from 1957).
4. **Display type as personality.** Custom phototype display faces for every mood. Example: Chwast's
   Artone (1964) and Glaser's Baby Teeth, released through Photo-Lettering.
5. **The self-published lab.** An in-house magazine as a playground and portfolio. Example: *The Push
   Pin Graphic* (1957–80).

**Key works.** *The Push Pin Graphic* (1957–80); Glaser, Dylan poster (1966); Chwast, *End Bad
Breath* (1967); Glaser and Clay Felker, *New York* magazine (1968); Glaser, I♥NY (1977).

**Visual vocabulary.** Thick, bulbous outline drawing with flat colour fills; Victorian and Art
Nouveau ornament; woodcut textures; decorative display type from phototype libraries; saturated
pastel and psychedelic palettes.

**Lineage.** Victorian print, Art Nouveau, Art Deco, comics → psychedelic posters (a parallel
current), postmodern eclecticism, the illustrator-designer of today.

**Quotes.** Milton Glaser: "Less is not necessarily more." (Ten Things I Have Learned, AIGA talk,
London, 2001.)

**Remix today.** When everything defaults to the same neutral grotesk, Push Pin's permission to
raid history is a competitive advantage — as long as the reference serves an idea rather than
replacing one.

### Milton Glaser {#milton-glaser}

*1929–2020. American graphic designer and illustrator.*

**Context.** A Bronx-born Cooper Union graduate who studied etching with Giorgio Morandi in Bologna,
Glaser co-led Push Pin with Chwast for twenty years, co-founded *New York* magazine (1968), opened
Milton Glaser, Inc. (1974), and with Walter Bernard formed the publication firm WBMG (1983). He died
on his 91st birthday.

**Principles.**
- **Just enough is more.** Neither modernist reduction nor ornament by reflex; enough to make the idea
  live. Example: I♥NY (1977), three letters and a heart in American Typewriter.
- **Style is not to be trusted.** Style is a means; any style can serve. Example: the Dylan poster
  (1966) versus the DC Comics "bullet" (1977).
- **Design for civic good.** Example: I♥NY, commissioned to rescue New York's image in crisis.

**Key works.** Bob Dylan poster (1966; often dated 1967 from the album's release); *New York*
magazine (1968); Glaser Stencil (1970); I♥NY (1977); DC Comics bullet (1977).

**Visual vocabulary.** Silhouettes with decorative fills; geometric stencil type; flat bright colour;
rebus and pictogram; illustrative eclecticism.

**Lineage.** Morandi, Art Nouveau, Duchamp, Push Pin → editorial design, civic branding.

**Quotes.** From "Ten Things I Have Learned" (AIGA, London, 2001): "Less is not necessarily more";
"Just enough is more"; "Style is not to be trusted."

**Remix today.** I♥NY is the template for every rebus brand and emoji-as-word. Its deeper lesson is
civic: a mark can change how people feel about a place.

### Seymour Chwast {#seymour-chwast}

*Born 1931. American illustrator, designer and typeface designer.*

**Context.** Taught design at Abraham Lincoln High School by Leon Friend, Chwast graduated from
Cooper Union in 1951, was fired from *Esquire* with Sorel in 1954 and co-founded Push Pin. He still
runs The Pushpin Group. AIGA Medal 1985.

**Principles.**
- **Protest through humour.** The anti-war poster as a cartoon. Example: *End Bad Breath* (1967).
- **The vernacular as source.** Woodcut, comics and naïve drawing. Example: *The Push Pin Graphic*
  (from 1957).
- **Type that draws.** Example: Artone (1964) and Blimp (1970) display faces for Photo-Lettering.

**Key works.** *The Push Pin Graphic* (1957–80); Artone (1964); *End Bad Breath* (1967); jacket for
Pauline Kael's *Kiss Kiss Bang Bang* (1968); Blimp (1970).

**Visual vocabulary.** Thick wobbly line; flat colour; comic-book and woodcut motifs; hand-drawn
display type.

**Lineage.** Comics, German Expressionist woodcut, Victoriana → American illustration-led design.

**Remix today.** Chwast proves a political image can be funny without being trivial. A cartoon
can carry a heavier message than a photograph.

## Expressive Typography (US) {#expressive-typography-us}

*American "typographics", c. 1958–1985. New York.*

**Context.** Photocomposition freed display type from metal. Photo-Lettering Inc. (founded 1936 by
Edward Rondthaler) and the photo-display machines of the 1950s–60s let letters be set touching,
overlapping, condensed, expanded and enlarged to any size without loss. Advertising art directors —
Herb Lubalin, Lou Dorfsman, Gene Federico — used this to make words into images. The International
Typeface Corporation (founded 1970 by Aaron Burns, Lubalin and Rondthaler) and its magazine *U&lc*
(1973) turned the style into a commercial type library.

**Principles.**
1. **Typography as image.** The word becomes the picture; meaning is expressed through letterform.
   Example: Lubalin's *Mother & Child* logotype (1965; also dated 1966), the ampersand cradling "child"
   inside the "O".
2. **Tight, touching type.** Letters kerned until they kiss, made possible by phototype. Example:
   *Avant Garde* magazine logotype (1968).
3. **Ligatures and alternates as expression.** Example: ITC Avant Garde Gothic (1970), with its
   expressive ligatures.
4. **Editorial type as voice.** Headlines scale and break to dramatise copy. Example: *Fact* magazine
   (1964–67).
5. **Type as architecture.** Lettering at environmental scale. Example: Lou Dorfsman's
   *Gastrotypographicalassemblage* (1966), a 35-foot type wall for the CBS cafeteria.

**Key works.** Lubalin, *Eros* (1962), *Mother & Child* (1965), *Avant Garde* (1968), ITC Avant
Garde (1970), *U&lc* (1973); Dorfsman, CBS *Gastrotypographicalassemblage* (1966).

**Visual vocabulary.** Tightly kerned display type; huge ampersands and swashes; Victorian and
geometric faces mixed; words stacked into shapes; black and white with one accent.

**Lineage.** Dada and Futurist typography, the American advertising tradition, Push Pin's display
faces → 1980s postmodern typography (Scher's eclecticism), digital type foundries, Emigre.

**Remix today.** Variable fonts and OpenType alternates make Lubalin's tool chain native to the web.
Use them the way he did: for a single word that carries a whole idea.

### Herb Lubalin {#herb-lubalin}

*1918–1981. American graphic designer and typographer.*

**Context.** Cooper Union graduate (1939), colour-blind, Lubalin spent 19 years at Sudler & Hennessey
(1945–64), where he and John J. Graham drew the original NBC peacock (1957), before founding Herb
Lubalin, Inc. (1964). His three magazines for Ralph Ginzburg — *Eros*, *Fact*, *Avant Garde* — are
landmarks of editorial design.

**Principles.**
- **Typographics.** Type is not set but *designed* into an image of its meaning. Example: *Mother &
  Child* (1965).
- **The logotype as editorial.** A masthead can be a manifesto. Example: *Avant Garde* (1968).
- **Promote type by showing it off.** Example: *U&lc* (1973), a type catalogue disguised as a
  tabloid magazine.

**Key works.** *Saturday Evening Post* masthead (1961); *Eros* (1962–63); *Fact* (1964); *Mother &
Child* (1965); *Avant Garde* (1968–71); ITC Avant Garde Gothic (with Tom Carnase, 1970); *U&lc*
(1973).

**Visual vocabulary.** Geometric sans with ligatures; Victorian swash faces; overlapping letters;
large-format page with dramatic photography.

**Lineage.** New York School advertising → ITC → 1970s and 1980s display typography; Heller has
called *U&lc* the first *Emigre*.

**Remix today.** Lubalin's ligature logotypes show how much identity can be built from letters
alone. For a wordmark, look for the letter pairs that can hold hands.

### Paula Scher {#paula-scher}

*Born 1948. American graphic designer and painter. Partner at Pentagram since 1991.*

**Context.** Tyler School of Art (BFA 1970). Scher joined CBS Records in 1972 and in the following
decade designed hundreds of album covers for CBS and, briefly, Atlantic (the exact sequence varies
by source). Short budgets after 1979 pushed her toward type-only covers built from Russian
Constructivist and Art Deco vocabulary and wood type. She co-founded Koppel & Scher (1984) and joined
Pentagram in 1991, where her Public Theater identity (1994) changed American cultural branding.

**Principles.**
- **Historical vocabulary, not historical copy.** Borrow the grammar of Constructivism or Deco and
  speak new things with it. Example: *The Best of Jazz* poster for CBS Records (1979).
- **Parody as homage.** Example: Swatch poster (1985), a reprise of Herbert Matter's 1935
  Pontresina poster.
- **Type as the street.** Loud, mixed wood type that feels like a city. Example: the Public Theater
  identity (1994) and *Bring in 'da Noise, Bring in 'da Funk* posters (1995).
- **Serious, not solemn.** The title of her 2008 TED talk sums up a career of playful rigour.

**Key works.** *The Best of Jazz* (1979); Swatch (1985); Public Theater (1994); *Bring in 'da Noise*
(1995); Citi (1998; introduced 1999); map paintings (from the late 1990s).

**Visual vocabulary.** Mixed-weight wood and grotesque type set at many angles and sizes; black,
red and yellow; type filling the entire format; photographic cut-outs.

**Lineage.** Constructivism, Art Deco, Lubalin, Push Pin (she was married to Chwast) → contemporary
cultural identities and typographic posters.

**Remix today.** The Public Theater identity proves an institutional brand can be loud, typographic
and flexible, built from a type attitude rather than a fixed lockup. That is how many cultural
brands work today.
