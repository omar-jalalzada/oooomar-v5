# Expression & Rupture: from psychedelia to the neo-modern revival (c. 1965–2010s)

This slice covers the half-century in which graphic design stopped agreeing with itself. Between
the San Francisco dance-hall poster of 1966 and the Amsterdam and Zurich studios of the 2000s, the
discipline split into arguments: about legibility, about who the designer works for, about whether
modernism was a method or merely a corporate look. Almost every movement here is a reaction to
the International Typographic Style (`swiss-style`) and to corporate modernism, and the last one
reacts in turn against the reactions.

Two forces run through all of it. The first is **technology lowering the cost of making type**:
Letraset (1961), phototypesetting and the International Typeface Corporation (1970), the
photocopier, then the Macintosh (1984), PostScript (1985), Fontographer (1986), QuarkXPress (1987)
and Photoshop (1990). Each made a new kind of mess cheap. The second is **the designer claiming a
voice**: from Ken Garland's First Things First (1964) to Tibor Kalman's provocations, the designer
stops being an invisible servant of the client's message and becomes an author.

All movements in this slice sit in the `expression` lineage.

## Psychedelic Poster {#psychedelic}

### Context

San Francisco, 1965–1968. Promoters Chet Helms (the Family Dog, at the Avalon Ballroom) and Bill
Graham (the Fillmore) ran weekly dances and needed weekly posters. A small group of artists, "the
Big Five" of Wes Wilson, Victor Moscoso, Stanley Mouse, Alton Kelley and Rick Griffin, answered
with posters that tried to put the intensity of the light shows and LSD into print. They reacted
against the clean, rational corporate modernism of the period and reached back past it, to Art
Nouveau, the Vienna Secession and Victorian ornament. The technology was humble: offset
lithography in a few flat colours, hand lettering, and a market of fans who tore posters down as
fast as they went up. By 1967 they were reprinted and sold nationally; in September 1967 a Wilson
Grateful Dead poster made the cover of *Life* for its feature on "the national poster craze." In
New York, Peter Max carried the look into mass merchandise.

### Principles

1. **Legibility as a delay.** Lettering swells to fill the shape it sits in until the words become
   a texture you must decode. The time spent decoding is the point: it holds a passer-by.
   *Example:* Wes Wilson, Bill Graham Presents poster "Fly 8 Miles High with Me," 1966.
2. **Vibrating complements.** Equal-value complementary colours (red on green, orange on blue)
   placed edge to edge make the boundary shimmer so a flat print appears to move. *Example:*
   Victor Moscoso, Neon Rose #2 for the Matrix, 1967.
3. **Borrowed ornament, new body.** Secession lettering and Mucha-like figures are lifted wholesale
   and re-inked in fluorescent colour for a new public. *Example:* Wes Wilson, Family Dog poster
   "Sin Dance," 1966.
4. **The poster as merchandise.** Event bills become wall art and the style becomes licensable.
   *Example:* Peter Max, *Poster Book*, 1970.

### Key works

- Wes Wilson, *Are We Next?*, anti-war poster, 1965
- Wes Wilson, Trips Festival poster, 1966
- Wes Wilson, Family Dog "The Quick and the Dead," 1966
- Victor Moscoso, Neon Rose series for the Matrix, 1967
- Victor Moscoso, contributions to *Zap Comix* #2, 1968
- Peter Max, *Life* cover, 5 September 1969

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** hand-drawn, never set; letters bloat, melt and lock together to fill a silhouette (a
  flame, a figure, a circle). Counters close up. Words wrap around images.
- **Colour:** saturated complementary pairs at equal value; later fluorescent inks. Colour is used
  to destabilise, not to separate.
- **Grid:** none. Composition is centralised and symmetrical, often a single dense medallion.
- **Imagery:** Art Nouveau women, Native American and Eastern motifs, collage of Victorian
  engravings and photographs (Moscoso was the first of the group to use photo collage).
- **Composition:** all-over; no white space; the whole sheet is pattern.

### Lineage

From `art-nouveau` and `vienna-secession` (Alfred Roller's block lettering was Wilson's source),
with Op Art's optical effects and the commercial illustration revival of `push-pin`. It fed the
underground comix of *Zap* (1968), British psychedelic sleeve design (Barney Bubbles's Hawkwind
work), and, much later, the hand-lettering revival of the 2000s and the gig-poster scene.

### Quotes

- "It was a time of enlightenment. In the '60s, we used to think of Utopia as something that was
  really going to happen." Wes Wilson, quoted in the Wikipedia biography "Wes Wilson."

### Remix today

The psychedelic poster is the clearest proof that *difficulty can be a hook*. A 2026 designer can
borrow the move without the clichés: make a word fill a shape so completely that the reader has
to slow down, or set two equal-value colours against each other on a screen where they will
genuinely flicker. On a feed where everything is designed to be read in 200 milliseconds, a
poster that asks for five seconds stands out.

### Wes Wilson {#wes-wilson}

**American poster artist, 1937–2020.** Robert Wesley Wilson ran a small printing business in San
Francisco, published his first poster, the self-printed anti-war *Are We Next?*, in 1965, and by
early 1966 was designing for both Chet Helms and Bill Graham. He worked exclusively for Graham
until a contract dispute in 1967, then moved to a farm in Missouri, where he painted until his
death.

**Principles.** *Fill the shape:* letters stretch until they occupy a silhouette entirely
("Fly 8 Miles High with Me," 1966). *Stop the passer-by:* he wanted lettering that was
"something people had to stop to figure out" (Family Dog "The Quick and the Dead," 1966). *Learn
from Vienna:* he took Alfred Roller's Secession block lettering, admiring how it filled space,
and resculpted it into his own style (Fillmore posters, 1966).

**Key works.** *Are We Next?*, 1965; Trips Festival, 1966; "Fly 8 Miles High with Me," 1966;
poster for the Beatles' last concert at Candlestick Park, 1966; "Grateful Dead: Wilson's Last
Stand," 1967.

**Visual vocabulary.** Flame-like, melting block lettering; complementary reds and greens;
earthy, sensual female figures; dense symmetrical compositions.

**Lineage.** Vienna Secession lettering into the San Francisco poster; widely imitated by every
psychedelic poster artist who followed.

**Remix today.** Wilson's real invention is a *lettering system*, not a picture: a rule for
deforming letters to fill any container. That is directly programmable today as a variable font
or a shader that fits type to a shape.

### Victor Moscoso {#victor-moscoso}

**Spanish-American poster and comix artist, born 1936.** Born in Galicia and raised in Brooklyn,
Moscoso studied at Cooper Union and at Yale, where Josef Albers taught him, before moving to San
Francisco in 1959. He was the first of the rock poster artists with formal academic training and
the first to use photographic collage. His Neon Rose posters for the Matrix club and his Family
Dog posters for the Avalon brought him international attention in the Summer of Love; from 1968
he drew for *Zap Comix* until its last issue in 2014.

**Principles.** *Colour as motion:* Albers's lesson that adjacent colours alter each other,
pushed to full saturation so the poster flickers (Neon Rose #2, 1967). *Invert the academy:*
academic colour and lettering skill used to make posters harder to read, not easier (Family Dog
"Sphinx Dance," 1967). *Sequence and loop:* his comics repeat frames on a fixed eight-panel grid
so a story can be entered anywhere; "KSAN Comics" (1971) was designed as a loop for San Francisco
buses.

**Key works.** Neon Rose series, 1967; "Sphinx Dance," 1967; *Zap Comix* #2 contributions, 1968;
cover of Herbie Hancock's *Head Hunters*, 1973.

**Visual vocabulary.** Vibrating complementary pairs; photographic collage; bloated lettering;
in comics, rigid repeating grids and morphing figures.

**Lineage.** Albers and Yale colour theory into psychedelia; a bridge from the poster to
underground comix.

**Remix today.** Moscoso shows how a *rigorous* colour education can produce wild results. Take
an Albers exercise, keep the method, swap the intent from harmony to agitation.

### Peter Max {#peter-max}

**German-American artist, 1937–2026.** Born in Berlin, raised in Shanghai, Haifa and Paris before
settling in Brooklyn, Max opened the Daly & Max studio in 1962 and developed what he called his
"Cosmic '60s" style: Day-Glo flowers, planets, rainbows and stars. He designed flyers for the
Central Park Be-In of 1967 and was on the cover of *Life* on 5 September 1969. He then licensed the
look across clocks, clothing and homewares. His repeated claims to have worked on *Yellow
Submarine* (1968) have been denied by its production team; that film's look belongs to Heinz
Edelmann. Max died on 14 September 2026.

**Principles.** *The full spectrum:* whole-spectrum gradients carry optimism directly
(*Poster Book*, 1970). *Style as licence:* one recognisable manner applied to every product,
making the artist the brand (US Expo '74 "Preserve the Environment" stamp, 1974).

**Key works.** Be-In flyers, 1967; *Life* cover, 1969; *Poster Book*, 1970; Expo '74 stamp,
1974.

**Visual vocabulary.** Flat, outlined cartoon figures; rainbow gradients; cosmic motifs; Fauvist
colour.

**Lineage.** Pop art and Push Pin illustration crossed with psychedelia; a precursor of the
artist-as-licensed-brand model.

**Remix today.** Max is a cautionary model as much as an inspiration: a style can scale to every
surface, but a look that is everywhere quickly reads as wallpaper.

## Punk and Post-punk Graphics {#punk}

### Context

London, Manchester and Sheffield, 1976–1986. Punk rejected the elaborate gatefold sleeves of
progressive rock and the professional polish of the music business. Its graphics came from
fanzines, photocopiers, scissors, glue and Letraset, and from a political lineage: Jamie Reid's
Suburban Press (1970) and the Situationist idea of *détournement*, turning the spectacle's own
images against it. Within two years a second wave arrived. Independent labels (Stiff, Factory,
Fetish, 4AD) gave designers unusual freedom, and designers like Barney Bubbles and Peter Saville
answered punk's mess with deliberate, intelligent quotation of modernist and art-historical
sources. Brody's magazine work for *The Face* (from 1981) brought this sensibility to the
newsstand.

### Principles

1. **Ransom-note lettering.** Letters cut from newspaper headlines and glued at mismatched sizes
   make the message look illicit and anonymous. *Example:* Jamie Reid, "God Save the Queen"
   sleeve, 1977.
2. **Détournement of icons.** An official image is defaced just enough to invert its meaning.
   *Example:* Reid's treatment of the Cecil Beaton portrait of Elizabeth II for "God Save the
   Queen," 1977.
3. **The sleeve as a puzzle.** Errors, misregistration, die-cuts and hidden codes reward the fan
   who looks closely. *Example:* Barney Bubbles, Elvis Costello *This Year's Model*, printed
   deliberately miscropped, 1978.
4. **Modernism as a found object.** Tschichold, scientific plots and old paintings are quoted with
   detachment and the band's name is small or absent. *Example:* Peter Saville, Joy Division
   *Unknown Pleasures*, 1979.

### Key works

- Jamie Reid, *Never Mind the Bollocks, Here's the Sex Pistols*, 1977
- Barney Bubbles, Elvis Costello *Armed Forces*, 1979
- Peter Saville, Joy Division *Unknown Pleasures*, 1979
- Peter Saville, New Order "Blue Monday," 1983
- Neville Brody, art direction of *The Face*, 1981–1986

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** cut-up headline letters, typewriter, stencil, Letraset, hand scrawl; in the post-punk
  strand, small, precise sans serif or none at all.
- **Colour:** fluorescent pink and yellow on black-and-white photocopy (Reid); flat, muted or
  single-colour fields (Saville); Pop and Constructivist primaries (Bubbles, Brody).
- **Grid:** abandoned in punk; reasserted, very visibly, in post-punk.
- **Imagery:** defaced photographs, collage, appropriated paintings and diagrams.
- **Composition:** collage and tilt in punk; centred, frontal, empty in Saville.

### Lineage

From `dada` collage and photomontage, the Situationists, `constructivism` (Bubbles and Brody),
and Tschichold's `new-typography` (Saville), with psychedelic sleeve culture in the background.
It fed grunge typography, the style press of the 1980s, and the record-sleeve design culture that
4AD and the Designers Republic continued.

### Quotes

- "Malcolm had a copy of Herbert Spencer's *Pioneers of Modern Typography*. The one chapter that
  he hadn't reinterpreted in his own work was the cool, disciplined 'New Typography' of Tschichold
  and its subtlety appealed to me. I found a parallel in it for the New Wave that was evolving out
  of Punk." Peter Saville (quoted in the Wikipedia article "Peter Saville (graphic designer)").

### Remix today

Punk's lasting lesson is that *the cheapest tool available is a style*. A phone scanner, a
receipt printer or a screenshot can do now what the photocopier did then. The post-punk lesson
is sharper: quoting modernism with detachment can be more subversive than smashing it. Reid
shows how to make one altered image carry a whole argument.

### Jamie Reid {#jamie-reid}

**English artist, 1947–2023.** Reid studied at Wimbledon and Croydon art schools, where he met
Malcolm McLaren during a student sit-in. He founded the radical magazine Suburban Press in 1970
and developed his ransom-note lettering there; McLaren then brought him in to design for the Sex
Pistols. His "God Save the Queen" sleeve was called "the single most iconic image of the punk
era" by Sean O'Hagan in *The Observer*. He later lived in Liverpool and worked on direct-action
campaigns.

**Principles.** *Ransom note:* headline letters cut and pasted at random sizes (*Never Mind the
Bollocks*, 1977). *Deface the official image:* a sanctioned portrait altered just enough to turn
it against itself ("God Save the Queen," 1977). *Graphics as agitation:* the sleeve continues the
political pamphlet (Suburban Press, 1970).

**Key works.** Suburban Press, from 1970; "Anarchy in the U.K.," 1976; "God Save the Queen,"
1977; *Never Mind the Bollocks*, 1977; "Pretty Vacant," 1977.

**Visual vocabulary.** Day-Glo yellow and pink over black; newspaper cut-out letters; defaced
photography; Situationist slogans.

**Lineage.** Situationist détournement and Dada collage into punk; picked up by every protest
graphic since, including Barnbrook's work.

**Remix today.** Reid's method is portable: take the most official image available and alter it
with the smallest gesture that reverses its meaning.

### Barney Bubbles {#barney-bubbles}

**English graphic designer, 1942–1983.** Born Colin Fulcher, he trained in display and packaging
at Twickenham, then worked at Michael Tucker + Associates and at the Conran Group. He ran light
shows in London's underground scene (the source of his name), designed Hawkwind's sleeves in the
early 1970s, and from 1977 was art director at Stiff Records, then at Radar and F-Beat with Jake
Riviera. He redesigned the *NME* masthead in 1979 and directed videos including the Specials'
"Ghost Town" (1981). After 1973 he increasingly left his work uncredited.

**Principles.** *History as a palette:* Constructivism, Kandinsky, Pop and 1950s packaging
quoted freely (Nick Lowe *Labour of Lust* "Hamer & sickle" logo, 1979). *Errors on purpose:*
deliberate wrong photographs and erratum stickers (The Damned, *Damned Damned Damned*, 1977). *The
sleeve as a kit:* fold-outs and build-it objects (*Glastonbury Fayre* triple-LP with cut-out
pyramid, 1972). *Anonymity:* the band and the idea stay in front.

**Key works.** Hawkwind, *In Search of Space*, 1971; *Glastonbury Fayre*, 1972; Elvis Costello,
*This Year's Model*, 1978; *Armed Forces*, 1979, with die-cut "DON'T JOIN" postcards; *NME*
masthead, 1979.

**Visual vocabulary.** Swiss-trained typography used playfully; Constructivist geometry;
symbols and riddles; deliberate misregistration.

**Lineage.** Swiss typography and psychedelic sleeve culture into punk and new wave; a direct
influence on Saville and Malcolm Garrett.

**Quotes.** On Michael Tucker's studio: "very Swiss; very hard; unjustified, very grey; and he
taught me everything about typography." Barney Bubbles, interview in *The Face*, November 1981.
Peter Saville on Bubbles: "The work of Barney Bubbles expresses post-modern principles: that
there is the past, the present and the possible; that culture and the history of culture are a
fluid palette of semiotic expression and everything is available to articulate a point of view."

**Remix today.** Bubbles is the model for a designer who treats the whole of design history as
material without becoming a pastiche artist: every borrowing is chosen because it *says*
something about this record.

### Peter Saville {#peter-saville}

**English art director, born 1955.** Saville studied graphic design at Manchester Polytechnic
(1975–1978) and co-founded Factory Records with Tony Wilson and Alan Erasmus in 1978, designing
the first Factory poster, FAC 1. His sleeves for Joy Division and New Order are some of the most
reproduced images in British design. He later worked at Pentagram, ran his own studios, worked for
fashion houses from Jil Sander to Burberry, co-founded SHOWstudio with Nick Knight in 2000 and was
Creative Director of the City of Manchester from 2004.

**Principles.** *The cool answer to punk:* Tschichold's New Typography as the visual parallel to
post-punk (FAC 1, 1978). *Appropriate and recontextualise:* a pulsar data plot or a Fantin-Latour
still life lifted whole (*Power, Corruption & Lies*, 1983, with a colour-coded alphabet). *Withhold
the name:* coded or absent titles; "Blue Monday" (1983) is a die-cut sleeve modelled on a floppy
disk.

**Key works.** FAC 1, 1978; *Unknown Pleasures*, 1979; *Closer*, 1980; "Blue Monday," 1983;
*Power, Corruption & Lies*, 1983.

**Visual vocabulary.** Large empty fields; small, precise type or none; appropriated scientific
and art-historical images; expensive, unusual print finishes.

**Lineage.** Tschichold and Swiss modernism via Herbert Spencer's *Pioneers of Modern Typography*;
a direct ancestor of 1990s and 2000s minimalist brand and fashion design.

**Remix today.** Saville proves that withholding can be stronger than announcing. An identity
that trusts its audience to know, rather than labelling everything, builds a cult.

### Neville Brody {#neville-brody}

**English designer and typographer, born 1957.** Brody studied graphics at the London College of
Printing from 1976, as punk arrived; his first-year thesis compared Dada and Pop. He designed for
Fetish Records and Cabaret Voltaire, was art director of *The Face* (1981–1986) and *Arena*
(1987–1990), co-founded FontWorks and FontShop's FontFont library with Erik Spiekermann in 1990,
and started the experimental type magazine *FUSE* with Jon Wozencroft in 1991. The 1988 Thames &
Hudson book *The Graphic Language of Neville Brody* became a best-seller and a V&A exhibition
followed.

**Principles.** *Draw the headline:* a custom, constructivist-influenced alphabet per magazine
(Industria, drawn for *The Face* in 1984 and released by Linotype in 1989). *The page as a
poster:* rotated, scaled, image-like type (*The Face*, 1981–1986). *Type as experiment:* a
publication that ships new, often unusable fonts with essays (*FUSE* 1, 1991).

**Key works.** *The Face*, 1981–1986; Cabaret Voltaire *Micro-Phonies*, 1984; *Arena*, 1987;
*FUSE* 1, 1991; Times Modern for *The Times*, 2006.

**Visual vocabulary.** Geometric, hand-drawn sans serif headlines; Constructivist diagonals and
symbols; heavy rules; type as illustration.

**Lineage.** Dada, Constructivism and Pop through punk; fed directly into the postmodern
typography of the 1990s and the FontFont generation.

**Remix today.** Brody's habit of drawing a bespoke alphabet for each publication is easier than
ever with variable-font tools. The bigger lesson of *FUSE* is to keep a research publication
beside the commercial work.

## New Wave Typography {#new-wave}

### Context

Basel, from 1968, then the United States. At the Basel School of Design, the Swiss style had
become an orthodoxy taught as a set of rules. Wolfgang Weingart, a German typesetter who began
teaching typography at the school in 1968, kept the Swiss material (sans serif, asymmetry, the
grid) and questioned every rule applied to it: what happens to letterspacing, to line, to
hierarchy, when you push it? He worked in hot metal and then in film, layering halftone screens
and typesetting film. A lecture tour of the United States in the early 1970s, and the Yale Summer
Program in Brissago where he taught from 1974, brought American students to Basel. April Greiman,
Dan Friedman and Willi Kunz took the approach home, where it became "New Wave," and in Los Angeles
Greiman fused it with photography, video and finally the Macintosh. The term "Swiss Punk" stuck
because the timing coincided with punk, though Weingart's work predates it.

### Principles

1. **Break the grid from inside.** Start from Swiss order, then stair-step, letterspace, reverse
   and overlap type so the structure becomes visible by being violated. *Example:* Weingart,
   Kunsthalle Basel "Kunstkredit 76-77" poster, 1977.
2. **Film as a layer.** Halftone screens, typesetting film and photographs are sandwiched and
   re-photographed; texture and transparency become typographic material. *Example:* the same
   Kunstkredit poster, 1977.
3. **Type in deep space.** Letters, colour swatches and photographs float at different apparent
   depths rather than on a flat page. *Example:* April Greiman and Jayme Odgers, CalArts poster,
   1978.
4. **Readability over legibility.** Instead of maximising the recognition of letters, ask whether
   the whole composition draws a reader in. *Example:* Dan Friedman's typography teaching at Yale
   and SUNY Purchase, 1969–1975.

### Key works

- Wolfgang Weingart, Kunsthalle Basel "Kunstkredit 76-77" poster, 1977
- April Greiman and Jayme Odgers, CalArts poster, 1978
- April Greiman and Jayme Odgers, LA Summer Games runner poster, 1982
- April Greiman, *Design Quarterly* 133, "Does It Make Sense?", 1986

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** sans serif still dominant (Akzidenz, Univers), but with wide and inconsistent
  letterspacing, varying weights within a word, stepped lines, type at angles.
- **Colour:** in Basel, mostly black and one or two colours; in California, pastel and primary
  colour, gradients, video colour.
- **Grid:** present but deliberately disrupted; centred, ragged-left and ragged-right settings
  in one piece.
- **Imagery:** halftone dot enlarged into pattern, layered photographic film, later video
  frames.
- **Composition:** layered, diagonal, kinetic; textures overlapping text.

### Lineage

From `swiss-style` (it is a mutation from inside it, which is why historians still argue whether
it was a break or a continuation), with debts to `dada` and `constructivism`. It fed directly into
American postmodern typography at Cranbrook and CalArts, into Emigre, and into the layered digital
layouts of the 1990s.

### Quotes

- "I took 'Swiss Typography' as my starting point, but then I blew it apart, never forcing any
  style upon my students. I never intended to create a 'style'. It just happened that the
  students picked up—and misinterpreted—a so-called 'Weingart style' and spread it around."
  Wolfgang Weingart (quoted in the Wikipedia article "Wolfgang Weingart").

### Remix today

New Wave is the best model for *how to rebel against a system you respect*. Keep the house
typeface and the grid, then pick one rule (spacing, alignment, hierarchy) and test it to failure.
Design systems teams can use this as a deliberate exercise: the variations reveal what the
system's rules are actually for.

### Wolfgang Weingart {#wolfgang-weingart}

**German typographer and educator, 1941–2021.** Weingart studied applied graphic arts at the Merz
Academy in Stuttgart and did a three-year apprenticeship in hot-metal hand composition. He met
Emil Ruder and Armin Hofmann in Basel in 1963, moved there in 1964, and in 1968 was invited to
teach typography at the school's new advanced class. He taught at the Yale Summer Program in
Brissago from 1974 to 1996, sat on the editorial board of *Typografische Monatsblätter* from 1970
to 1988, and received the AIGA Medal in 2013.

**Principles.** *Start from Swiss, then question it:* keep the base, test every rule
(*Typografische Monatsblätter* covers, early 1970s). *Typography as craft experiment:* spacing,
stepped lines and reversed type explored physically, in metal and film (Kunstkredit poster,
1977). *Teach, don't impose:* he denied creating a style; his students made one.

**Key works.** *Typografische Monatsblätter* covers, early 1970s; Kunsthalle Basel "Kunstkredit
76-77," 1977; *Weingart: Typography*, his retrospective book, 2000.

**Visual vocabulary.** Letterspaced, stair-stepped sans serif; layered film and halftone;
black, grey and one colour.

**Lineage.** Ruder and Hofmann's Basel into New Wave; teacher of Greiman and Friedman.

**Quotes.** "When I began teaching in 1968, classical, so-called 'Swiss typography' (dating from
the 1950s), was still commonly practiced by designers throughout Switzerland and at our school.
Its conservative design dogma and strict limitations stifled my playful, inquisitive,
experimental temperament and I reacted strongly against it. Yet at the same time I recognized too
many good qualities in Swiss typography to renounce it altogether." Wolfgang Weingart (quoted in
the Wikipedia article "Wolfgang Weingart").

**Remix today.** Weingart's sentence "too many good qualities … to renounce it altogether" is the
right attitude toward any inherited system, including today's design tokens.

### April Greiman {#april-greiman}

**American designer, born 1948.** Greiman studied at the Kansas City Art Institute and then at
Basel (1970–1971) under Hofmann and Weingart. She moved to Los Angeles in 1976, collaborated with
designer-photographer Jayme Odgers, and in 1982 became director of the design programme at CalArts,
where she had it renamed Visual Communications. After seeing an early Macintosh demonstration at
the first TED conference in February 1984 she bought one, and made it a medium rather than a
production tool. She calls herself a "transmedia artist."

**Principles.** *Objects in space:* type, swatches, lines and photographs floating in layered
depth (CalArts poster, 1978). *Use the crude tool:* low-resolution video capture and Mac bitmaps
as expressive material (*Design Quarterly* 133, 1986, a nearly three-by-six-foot fold-out poster
of her own naked body, digitised with MacVision). *Hybrid imagery:* photography, video and type
fused into one image (Pacific Wave poster, Museo Fortuny, 1987).

**Key works.** CalArts poster, 1978; LA Summer Games runner poster, 1982; *Design Quarterly* 133,
"Does It Make Sense?", 1986; *Hybrid Imagery*, 1990; Women's Suffrage stamp, 1995.

**Visual vocabulary.** Floating diagonal type; pastel and primary colour with gradients;
pixelated video stills; overlapping translucent planes.

**Lineage.** Basel New Wave into California; a bridge to Emigre and digital design.

**Quotes.** On *Does It Make Sense?*: "the sense it has for me is that it's new and yet old,...
it's a magazine, which is an artwork, which is an object, which is... crazy." April Greiman
(quoted in the Wikipedia article "April Greiman").

**Remix today.** Greiman embraced the new tool's crudeness before it could be made respectable.
The equivalent today is using generative and real-time tools for their artefacts rather than
hiding them.

### Dan Friedman {#dan-friedman}

**American graphic and furniture designer, 1945–1995.** Friedman studied at Carnegie Tech, then at
Ulm and at Basel under Weingart and Hofmann. He taught at Yale (1969–1973) and SUNY Purchase,
worked at Anspach Grossman Portugal and at Pentagram (1979–1984), and made Day-Glo furniture from
found objects. He was close to Keith Haring and the downtown art scene. He died of AIDS in 1995;
the Art Institute of Chicago held the retrospective "Dan Friedman: Stay Radical" in 2023.

**Principles.** *Readability, not legibility:* the whole piece must invite reading (typography
teaching at Yale, 1969–1973). *Radical modernism:* keep modernism's optimism, drop its puritanism
(*Dan Friedman: Radical Modernism*, 1994). *No border between disciplines:* identity, furniture
and art books as one practice (a book for Keith Haring, 1982).

**Key works.** Book for Keith Haring, 1982; *Post Human* catalogue with Jeffrey Deitch, 1992;
*Dan Friedman: Radical Modernism*, 1994.

**Visual vocabulary.** Basel layering in his teaching; later, bright colour, kitsch objects and
pattern close to Memphis.

**Lineage.** Ulm and Basel into American New Wave; a bridge to Memphis-like postmodern objects.

**Remix today.** "Radical modernism" is a useful phrase for anyone who wants modernism's social
ambition without its visual austerity.

## Memphis {#memphis}

### Context

Milan, 1980–1988. Ettore Sottsass had spent two decades at Olivetti making office machines
friendly and colourful, and in the 1960s and 1970s worked alongside the Radical design groups
(Archizoom, Superstudio, Global Tools). In late 1980 he gathered younger designers, among them
Michele De Lucchi, Martine Bedin, Nathalie Du Pasquier, George Sowden and Peter Shire, and the
group named itself after a Bob Dylan song playing during the meeting. Its first collection
appeared in Milan in 1981 and caused a sensation. Memphis attacked functionalist "good taste"
with cheap plastic laminate, terrazzo, toy-like geometry and clashing colour. Sottsass left in
1985 and the group dissolved by 1988, but the look went everywhere: TV sets, mall architecture,
skate graphics, the *Miami Vice* era, and much later the "Corporate Memphis" illustration style of
the late 2010s. Memphis is a product and furniture movement, but its influence on graphic design
was immediate: pattern, flat colour and geometric ornament as surface.

### Principles

1. **Cheap material, luxury price.** Diner-kitchen laminate on gallery furniture, so status and
   taste are put in question. *Example:* Sottsass, Carlton bookcase, 1981.
2. **Pattern as surface, not ornament.** A squiggle or speckle covers a form flatly and
   arbitrarily, unrelated to structure. *Example:* Sottsass's Bacterio laminate pattern, derived
   from a temple surface in Madurai, used on Memphis pieces from 1981.
3. **Object as character.** A bookcase is composed like a totem with arms and a head; personality
   before function. *Example:* Carlton bookcase, 1981.
4. **Clash on purpose.** Colours and shapes that should not sit together are stacked. *Example:*
   Sottsass, Casablanca sideboard, 1981.

### Key works

- Ettore Sottsass and Perry A. King, Valentine typewriter (Olivetti), 1969 (the precursor)
- Ettore Sottsass, Carlton bookcase, 1981
- Ettore Sottsass, Casablanca sideboard, 1981
- Ettore Sottsass, Murmansk fruit bowl, 1982

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** rarely central in the objects, but Memphis graphics favour geometric display faces
  and type used as pattern.
- **Colour:** acid pastels (mint, salmon, lemon) against black and primary red, yellow, blue.
- **Grid:** none; asymmetrical, additive composition.
- **Imagery:** squiggles, speckles, zig-zags, terrazzo, triangles, circles.
- **Composition:** totemic stacking; shapes balanced against each other like toy blocks.

### Lineage

From `art-deco`, Pop art and 1950s kitsch, Italian Radical design, and the colour of `psychedelic`
culture. It fed postmodern graphic pattern of the 1980s, Dan Friedman's furniture, and, at a long
distance, vaporwave and "Corporate Memphis" illustration.

### Quotes

- "I didn't want to do any more consumerist products, because it was clear that the consumerist
  attitude was quite dangerous." Ettore Sottsass, on his late-1960s work (quoted in the Wikipedia
  article "Ettore Sottsass").
- "Sottsass meant it as a joke! It's joke junk." Terence Conran (quoted in the Wikipedia article
  "Memphis Group").

### Remix today

Memphis teaches that *surface can be a separate design layer* from structure. A product or brand
in 2026 can run a pattern system independent of its layout grid. It is also a warning: "Corporate
Memphis" shows how a rebellious style becomes the neutral default of tech marketing once its
critique is removed.

### Ettore Sottsass {#ettore-sottsass}

**Italian architect and designer, 1917–2007.** Born in Innsbruck and raised in Turin, Sottsass
opened his studio in Milan in 1947 and became Olivetti's design consultant in the late 1950s,
working on the Elea 9003 mainframe (Compasso d'Oro, 1959) and the Valentine portable typewriter
with Perry A. King (1969). He made "Superbox" laminated cabinets for Poltronova in the 1960s,
founded Memphis in December 1980 and his consultancy Sottsass Associati the same year, and left
Memphis in 1985.

**Principles.** *Bring the office into pop culture:* a red plastic typewriter marketed like a
fashion item (Valentine, 1969). *Refuse the consumerist product:* objects as sculptural
statements (Superbox cabinets, 1966). *Radical, funny, outrageous:* his own description of
Memphis's aim (Carlton bookcase, 1981).

**Key works.** Elea 9003, 1959; Valentine, 1969; Carlton bookcase, 1981; Casablanca sideboard,
1981; Murmansk fruit bowl, 1982.

**Visual vocabulary.** Laminates with printed patterns; primary and acid colour; totemic,
architectural furniture.

**Lineage.** Italian rationalism (his father's MIAR circle) and Radical design into Memphis.

**Remix today.** Sottsass's Valentine is a template for making a serious tool feel personal: the
same move Apple's coloured iMacs made in 1998.

## Postmodern Typography {#postmodern-typography}

### Context

United States and Britain, about 1978–1995. Two schools defined American postmodern graphic
design: Cranbrook Academy of Art outside Detroit, where Katherine and Michael McCoy ran the
graduate design programme from 1971 to 1995, and CalArts near Los Angeles, where Greiman, Lorraine
Wild and later Ed Fella taught. At Cranbrook the influences were first Robert Venturi's *Learning
from Las Vegas* (1972) and the commercial vernacular, then Weingart's experiments, then semiotics,
post-structuralism and deconstruction. A landmark was a 1978 issue of the journal *Visible
Language*, "French Currents of the Letter," on French literary theory, designed by Cranbrook
students. In New York, Tibor Kalman's M&Co adopted the vernacular as a protest against corporate
style, and in Britain, Vaughan Oliver gave the 4AD label a dreamlike, ornate visual world. Rick
Poynor's later book *No More Rules* (2003) is the standard account. The technology moved from
photostat and Letraset to the Macintosh in the middle of the period.

### Principles

1. **Reading and seeing at once.** Text is set to be both read and looked at; the reader assembles
   meaning from layered words and images. *Example:* Katherine McCoy, Cranbrook Graduate Program
   in Design poster, 1989.
2. **Learn from the vernacular.** Hand-lettered signs, clip art, Letraset and trade printing are
   as legitimate a source as Swiss modernism. *Example:* Ed Fella, Detroit Focus Gallery poster
   "Morris Brose: A Sustained Vision," 1987.
3. **The designer as author.** The designer adds a voice to the content rather than serving it
   invisibly. *Example:* Tibor Kalman and M&Co, typographic video for Talking Heads' "(Nothing
   But) Flowers," 1988.
4. **Theory as a design tool.** Ideas about how texts mean are tested directly in layout and
   documented as a programme. *Example:* Katherine and Michael McCoy, *Cranbrook Design: The New
   Discourse*, 1991.

### Key works

- M&Co with Talking Heads, *Remain in Light* cover, 1980
- Ed Fella, Detroit Focus Gallery posters, from 1987
- Katherine McCoy, Cranbrook Graduate Program in Design poster, 1989
- Vaughan Oliver, Pixies *Doolittle*, 1989
- Neville Brody and Jon Wozencroft, *FUSE* 1, 1991

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** many faces at once; vernacular and historical display type mixed with sans serif;
  hand-altered letters; text broken into fragments.
- **Colour:** often restrained in Cranbrook posters (two or three colours, black and red);
  rich and photographic at 4AD.
- **Grid:** multiple competing grids or none; text blocks overlap images.
- **Imagery:** photographs layered with diagrams, clip art, found ephemera.
- **Composition:** dense, multi-layered, several possible reading paths.

### Lineage

From `new-wave` (Weingart's Basel), the `swiss-style` it argued with, `dada`, the illustration
eclecticism of `push-pin`, and Venturi's architectural postmodernism. It fed Emigre, grunge
typography and the "designer as author" debates of the 1990s.

### Quotes

- No verified movement-level quotation is reproduced here; see Tibor Kalman and Vaughan Oliver
  below. Michael Rock's essay "The Designer as Author" (1996) is the standard critical frame for
  the authorship idea.

### Remix today

The durable idea is *layered reading*: a page that offers several routes in rather than one
hierarchy. That fits interfaces surprisingly well (a dashboard, a map, a long-scroll story),
where users really do enter from different points. Fella's lesson is to take your local
vernacular seriously as a source.

### Katherine McCoy {#katherine-mccoy}

**American designer and educator, born 1945.** McCoy studied industrial design at Michigan State,
then worked at Unimark International among Vignelli and Bayer, at Chrysler, and with Muriel Cooper
on MIT Press projects. In 1971 she and Michael McCoy became co-chairs of design at Cranbrook, where
she led graphic design for 24 years. Students included Lorraine Wild, Ed Fella, Nancy Skolos, P.
Scott Makela and Andrew Blauvelt. She later taught at IIT's Institute of Design and received the
AIGA Medal in 1999.

**Principles.** *Layered reading:* several routes into one poster (Cranbrook Graduate Program in
Design poster, 1989, where a communication-theory diagram overlays a photograph of the campus and
text runs in competing directions). *Studio as laboratory:* minimal assignments, weekly critiques,
real client projects as a counterweight (the Cranbrook programme, 1971–1995). *Read deeply, then
design:* Venturi, Wurman and post-structuralism as working material (*Cranbrook Design: The New
Discourse*, 1991).

**Key works.** Cranbrook Graduate Program in Design poster, 1989; *Cranbrook Design: The New
Discourse*, 1991.

**Visual vocabulary.** Swiss-trained structure overlaid with diagrams, photography and
fragmented text; restrained colour.

**Lineage.** Unimark modernism and Weingart into Cranbrook postmodernism; teacher of a generation
of American design educators.

**Remix today.** McCoy's programme is a model for running a design team as a research studio:
low structure, strong critique, and a real client to keep experiments honest.

### Ed Fella {#ed-fella}

**American designer and artist, born 1938.** Fella trained in commercial art at Detroit's Cass
Technical High School and worked as a commercial artist from 1957 to 1987, drawing automotive and
healthcare advertising. In his spare time he made experimental flyers for the Detroit Focus
gallery. He earned an MFA at Cranbrook in 1987 and was then hired by Lorraine Wild to teach at
CalArts, where he taught until 2013. Emigre called him the "graphic godfather," and he received
the AIGA Medal in 2007.

**Principles.** *Use every tool at once:* found type, Letraset, stencil, scribble, clip art and
the photostat on one sheet (Detroit Focus poster, "Morris Brose: A Sustained Vision," 1987). *Make
every letter different:* a sans serif redrawn by hand so no character repeats (OutWest typeface,
Emigre, 1993). *After the fact:* posters made for those who attended, not to sell an event
beforehand, so the work escapes persuasion.

**Key works.** Detroit Focus Gallery posters, from 1987; OutWest, 1993; *Letters on America*,
his book of vernacular lettering photographs, 2000.

**Visual vocabulary.** Hand-drawn, wobbly, inconsistent letters; dense collage of trade
ephemera; tiny type next to huge type.

**Lineage.** Commercial lettering and Dada "words in freedom" into Cranbrook; fed Barry Deck's
Template Gothic and Jeffery Keedy at CalArts.

**Remix today.** Fella shows the value of a personal practice running beside paid work. Thirty
years of commercial skill is what made his "wrong" letters so controlled.

### Tibor Kalman {#tibor-kalman}

**Hungarian-American designer and editor, 1949–1999.** Kalman fled Hungary in 1956, dropped out of
NYU, and learned design running the in-house team at what became Barnes & Noble. In 1979 he
founded M&Co with Maira Kalman, Carol Bokuniewicz and Liz Trovato. Clients included Talking Heads,
the Whitney and Restaurant Florent. He was art director of *Artforum* and creative director of
*Interview*, then co-founded Benetton's *Colors* with Oliviero Toscani in 1991, moved to Rome to
edit it, and left it in 1995 when he fell ill. He received the AIGA Medal posthumously in 1999.

**Principles.** *Vernacular against corporate:* cheap signage and diner graphics as a protest
against corporate International Style (Restaurant Florent graphics, from 1985). *Good design needs
a good client:* design should serve ethical ends and public awareness (*Colors*, 1991–1995).
*Provoke with the obvious:* one doctored image (the Queen recast as a Black woman) instead of an
essay (*Colors* issue on race, 1993).

**Key works.** Talking Heads *Remain in Light*, 1980 (concept by Tina Weymouth and Chris Frantz,
realised with MIT computers and M&Co); "(Nothing But) Flowers" typographic video, 1988, directed
with Sandy MacLeod; *Colors*, from 1991; *Tibor Kalman: Perverse Optimist*, 1999.

**Visual vocabulary.** Deliberately ordinary, "undesigned" type; multilingual text in *Colors*;
documentary photography and manipulated images.

**Lineage.** A self-taught outsider to modernism; mentor to Stefan Sagmeister; signatory of First
Things First 2000.

**Quotes.** "Designers, stay away from corporations that want you to lie for them." Tibor Kalman
(used by Jonathan Barnbrook as the text of a 2001 billboard in Las Vegas).

**Remix today.** Kalman's question still applies: is the work making people more aware, or less?
His vernacular method (find the honest, cheap visual language around you) is the antidote to
over-polished branding.

### Vaughan Oliver {#vaughan-oliver}

**British graphic designer, 1957–2019.** Oliver studied graphic design at Newcastle Polytechnic
and moved to London in 1979, where he met 4AD's founder Ivo Watts-Russell. With photographer
Nigel Grierson as 23 Envelope, and from 1988 as v23 with Chris Bigg, he designed almost every 4AD
release from 1982 to 1998: Cocteau Twins, This Mortal Coil, Throwing Muses, the Breeders and the
Pixies' whole catalogue.

**Principles.** *Label as one atmosphere:* different bands share one sensibility, so the label
becomes the brand (4AD sleeves, 1982–1998). *Continuity with a fresh approach:* each release new
but in series (Pixies, *Surfer Rosa*, 1988, and *Doolittle*, 1989). *Texture and the uncanny:*
close-up, blurred, surreal photography felt before understood (This Mortal Coil, *Filigree &
Shadow*, 1986).

**Key works.** This Mortal Coil, *It'll End in Tears*, 1984; *Lonely is an Eyesore* compilation,
1987; *Surfer Rosa*, 1988; *Doolittle*, 1989; the Pixies' *Minotaur* box set, 2009.

**Visual vocabulary.** Ornate, sometimes calligraphic or condensed type; soft-focus and
macro photography; rich, dark colour; luxurious print finishes.

**Lineage.** Surrealism, Dalí and Pop art via record sleeves; a parallel, more romantic strand of
post-punk; featured in *Emigre* #9.

**Quotes.** "There was no real culture, my parents were not really interested in anything
unusual – everything I was getting was through record sleeves. It was a democratic way of
discovering art." Vaughan Oliver, 2014 (quoted in the Wikipedia article "Vaughan Oliver").

**Remix today.** Oliver is the model for a *house style that isn't a template*: a mood, a set of
materials and a photographic sensibility that can stretch across very different artists or
products.

## Emigre and Early Digital Type {#emigre-digital-type}

### Context

Berkeley, 1984–2005. The Macintosh launched on 24 January 1984 with a 72-dpi screen and bitmap
fonts. The same year, three Dutchmen in San Francisco, Rudy VanderLans, Marc Susan and Menno
Meyjes, started *Emigre*, a magazine for émigré artists. VanderLans's partner Zuzana Licko began
drawing bitmap typefaces on the Mac; *Emigre* #3 was set entirely in them, readers asked to buy
them, and Emigre Fonts began (dated 1985 by the foundry, with commercial licences from 1986).
PostScript (1985) and Fontographer (1986) then made outline fonts possible without a foundry's
equipment. By issue 9 the magazine had turned to graphic design itself, and from about 1989 to
2005 it was the main forum for, and against, digital experimentation, publishing Cranbrook,
CalArts and British designers alongside critics. It ended with issue 69, *The End*, in 2005.

### Principles

1. **Design for the machine's grain.** Draw the letter from the pixel grid instead of forcing
   metal-age faces into it. *Example:* Zuzana Licko, Oakland (later Lo-Res), used throughout
   *Emigre* #3, 1985.
2. **You read best what you read most.** Legibility is learned habit, not an intrinsic property
   of forms. *Example:* Licko, interview in *Emigre* #15, 1990.
3. **The magazine as specimen.** The publication shows its foundry's fonts in use, and the fonts
   fund the magazine. *Example:* *Emigre* with Emigre Fonts, from 1985.
4. **Distortion as a typeface.** Accident and imperfection encoded in a font file so anyone can
   set "damaged" type. *Example:* Barry Deck, Template Gothic, drawn 1989, released by Emigre
   1991.

### Key works

- *Emigre* #1, 1984
- Zuzana Licko, Oakland, Emperor and Modula, 1985; Matrix, 1986
- Barry Deck, Template Gothic, 1991 release
- Rudy VanderLans and Zuzana Licko, *Emigre: Graphic Design into the Digital Realm*, 1993
- Zuzana Licko, Mrs Eaves and Filosofia, 1996

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** bitmap faces with visible pixel steps; modular, geometric outline faces derived from
  them (Matrix, Modula); then distressed and hand-made digital faces by Deck, Keedy, Makela,
  Fella; finally refined revivals.
- **Colour:** mostly black and white in a large tabloid format (285 × 425 mm, issues 1–32).
- **Grid:** variable; each issue, often each spread, designed afresh, frequently by guests.
- **Imagery:** low-resolution scans, photography by VanderLans, diagrams.
- **Composition:** layered text columns, overlapping, sometimes running across folds; later,
  text-heavy critical essays in a letter-size format (from issue 33, 1995).

### Lineage

From Dutch modernist training (`dutch-modernism`: VanderLans studied at the Royal Academy in The
Hague under Gerrit Noordzij's type programme and apprenticed at Wim Crouwel's Total Design), from
`new-wave` and from `postmodern-typography`. It fed grunge typography, the independent digital
foundry model (FontFont, Lineto, and today's small foundries), and the web's bitmap and pixel
aesthetics.

### Quotes

- "You read best what you read most." Zuzana Licko, *Emigre* #15, 1990.
- "…it was physically impossible to adapt 8-point Goudy Old Style to 72 dots to the inch."
  Zuzana Licko, in *Emigre: Graphic Design into the Digital Realm*, 1993.
- Massimo Vignelli called Emigre's work "garbage" and "an aberration of culture" in a 1991
  interview in *Print*. (Vignelli later designed the poster announcing Licko's Filosofia.)

### Remix today

Licko's first principle is the most transferable idea in this slice: *design from the medium's
real constraint, not against it*. In 2026 the constraints are different (variable fonts, tiny
watch screens, e-ink, LED signage, AI image models with their own artefacts), but the method is
the same. The Emigre business model, a publication that showcases and funds its own products, is
also very modern.

### Rudy VanderLans {#rudy-vanderlans}

**Dutch-American designer, editor and photographer, born 1955.** VanderLans graduated from the
Royal Academy of Art in The Hague in 1979, worked at Total Design, Form Vijf and Tel Design, and
moved to California in 1981 to study photography at Berkeley, where he met Zuzana Licko. He worked
at the *San Francisco Chronicle* art department (1983–1985) while starting *Emigre*, which he
published, edited and designed from 1984 to 2005. He has published eleven photography books about
California.

**Principles.** *The magazine as forum:* argument published alongside work, guest editors and
opponents included (*Emigre*, 1984–2005). *Specimen as design work:* the type specimen as an
object that makes you want the face (Emigre Fonts specimens, from 1986). *Change format with
purpose:* tabloid to letter to CD booklet to book as content moved from image to text (*Emigre*
#33, 1995).

**Key works.** *Emigre* #1, 1984; *Emigre: Graphic Design into the Digital Realm*, 1993; *Emigre*
#69, *The End*, 2005.

**Visual vocabulary.** Large format, layered columns, bitmap type, his own photographs of the
California landscape.

**Lineage.** Noordzij and Crouwel's Netherlands into Berkeley; signatory of First Things First
2000.

**Remix today.** VanderLans shows that a design publication can be the most influential thing a
designer makes. A newsletter or site that argues, not just showcases, still works.

### Zuzana Licko {#zuzana-licko}

**Slovak-born American type designer, born 1961.** Licko came to the United States from Bratislava
aged seven. Her father, a biomathematician, introduced her to computers; her first font was a
Greek alphabet for his pen plotter. She studied graphic communications at Berkeley and, with
VanderLans, founded Emigre Fonts. She has designed at least three dozen families and received the
AIGA Medal with VanderLans in 1997.

**Principles.** *Start from the constraint:* letters designed for 72 dpi screens and dot-matrix
printers (Oakland/Lo-Res, 1985). *Legibility is habit:* "You read best what you read most"
(*Emigre* #15, 1990). *Revival as reinterpretation:* a historical model reworked with a low
x-height and abundant ligatures (Mrs Eaves, after Baskerville, 1996).

**Key works.** Oakland (Lo-Res), Emperor and Modula, 1985; Matrix, 1986; Mrs Eaves, 1996;
Filosofia, after Bodoni, 1996.

**Visual vocabulary.** Square pixel steps; modular geometric construction; later, elegant
ligature-rich revivals.

**Lineage.** Her bitmap faces are a starting point for screen typography; Oakland (as Lo-Res) is in
MoMA's collection.

**Remix today.** Licko's career runs from radical constraint to refined revival without changing
method. Each face is "a snapshot in time" of its technology, as she put it in 2016. That is a good
brief for any type designer working with new rendering tech.

## Grunge Typography {#grunge-deconstruction}

### Context

About 1989–2001, centred on California and Britain. QuarkXPress (1987) and Photoshop (1990) made
layering, transparency, distortion and scanned texture available to any designer with a Mac.
Fonts made at Cranbrook, CalArts and in Britain, often sold by Emigre, encoded imperfection.
David Carson, a former professional surfer with little formal training, took this into surf and
music magazines: *Beach Culture* (1989–1991) and *Ray Gun* (1992–1995 under Carson). The press
called it "grunge," after the music it often covered, and critics borrowed "deconstruction" from
Cranbrook theory, though most practitioners worked by intuition, not theory. In Britain, the
Designers Republic in Sheffield and Why Not Associates and Tomato in London ran parallel strands:
a parody of corporate language, and a kinetic typography moving into film and screens. The
moment peaked with *The End of Print* (1995), and by 1999 the First Things First 2000 manifesto
was asking what all this style was for.

### Principles

1. **Intuition over system.** Each spread composed by eye and mood; consistency lies in attitude,
   not measurements. *Example:* David Carson, *Ray Gun*, 1992–1995.
2. **Text as image.** Body copy cropped, overlapped, set in dingbats or run off the page when its
   texture says more than its words. *Example:* Carson's Bryan Ferry interview set entirely in
   Zapf Dingbats in *Ray Gun*.
3. **Corporate language turned on itself.** Logos, slogans and manuals parodied into a fake
   corporation's identity. *Example:* The Designers Republic, Wipeout packaging and identity, 1995.
4. **Type with a grudge.** Fonts carry political names and loaded historical forms. *Example:*
   Jonathan Barnbrook, Bastard, 1990.

### Key works

- Jonathan Barnbrook, Bastard typeface, 1990
- David Carson, *Ray Gun*, from 1992
- David Carson, *The End of Print*, 1995
- The Designers Republic, Wipeout (PlayStation), 1995
- Stefan Sagmeister, AIGA Detroit lecture poster, 1999

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** distressed, degraded and photocopied fonts (Template Gothic, Dead History, Barnbrook's
  faces); several sizes and faces per line; letters cropped by the page edge; extreme leading and
  tracking either way.
- **Colour:** muddy photographic colour and overprinting in Carson; hard techno colour
  (fluorescent, silver, Japanese-anime palettes) in the Designers Republic.
- **Grid:** absent or shattered; columns at angles, gutters ignored.
- **Imagery:** blurred, cropped, scanned and layered photography; textures from paper, rust and
  photocopies.
- **Composition:** dense layering at different opacities; image and text as a single field.

### Lineage

From `postmodern-typography`, `emigre-digital-type`, `punk` collage and `dada`. It fed web design
of the late 1990s (Flash-era motion graphics, in which Tomato and Why Not were influential), music
and skate branding, and provoked the neo-modernist revival, which defined itself against it.

### Quotes

- "It's the basic decisions—images, cropping and appropriate font and design choices—that make
  design work, not having the ability to overlap or play with opacity." David Carson (quoted in
  the Wikipedia article "David Carson (graphic designer)").

### Remix today

Grunge looks dated mainly where it was purely a software effect. What lasts is the idea that the
*texture of a layout* can carry meaning for its audience, and that a publication can have one
visual voice without a fixed grid. On screens, motion and interaction now do what layering did on
paper: a scroll-driven page that disassembles and reassembles its type is a grunge idea with
better tools.

### David Carson {#david-carson}

**American art director, born 1955.** Born in Corpus Christi, Texas, Carson studied sociology at
San Diego State, took a short graphic design course with a workshop in Switzerland, and worked
at *Transworld Skateboarding* before becoming art director of *Beach Culture* in 1989. It ran only
six issues but made his reputation. He art-directed *Ray Gun* from its launch in 1992 for about
three years, then founded David Carson Design, working for Nike, Pepsi and Levi's. He received
the AIGA Medal in 2014.

**Principles.** *Don't mistake legibility for communication:* a hard-to-read page can communicate
through texture (*Ray Gun*, 1992–1995). *Basic decisions make the work:* image choice, cropping
and type matter more than effects (*Beach Culture*, 1989–1991). *Each page its own answer:* no
template, every spread resolved from scratch (*The End of Print*, 1995).

**Key works.** *Beach Culture*, 1989; *Ray Gun*, 1992; *The End of Print*, 1995; Nine Inch Nails,
*The Fragile* packaging, 1999.

**Visual vocabulary.** Overlapping columns, cropped and inverted text, distressed fonts, blurred
photography, huge drop caps.

**Lineage.** Self-taught; influenced by Emigre-era fonts; spread grunge typography to the
mainstream.

**Remix today.** Carson's real skill was editorial intuition about his readers. The lesson is
less "break the grid" than "design for the reader you actually have."

### Jonathan Barnbrook {#jonathan-barnbrook}

**British designer, born 1966.** Barnbrook trained at Saint Martin's and the Royal College of Art.
His typefaces carry provocative names: Bastard (1990), Exocet, False Idol, Infidel, Moron,
Newspeak, Shock & Awe, and Manson, released by Emigre and later renamed Mason. He worked with Damien Hirst
(1997–2003), art-directed two editions of *Adbusters*, signed First Things First 2000, and
designed David Bowie's *Heathen* (2002), *Reality* (2003), *The Next Day* (2013) and *Blackstar*
(2016). The Design Museum showed his work as "Friendly Fire" in 2007.

**Principles.** *Design as a weapon:* graphic work as political response, often parodying
advertising (billboard of Kalman's line "Designers, stay away from corporations that want you to
lie for them," AIGA conference, Las Vegas, 2001). *The loaded typeface:* name and form carry an
argument (Bastard, a blackletter, 1990). *Type as the cover:* a bespoke face does the image's work
(Bowie's *Heathen*, set in his Priori, 2002).

**Key works.** Bastard, 1990; Manson/Mason, 1992; *Heathen*, 2002; *Blackstar*, 2016.

**Visual vocabulary.** Blackletter and inscriptional forms; corporate-parody layouts; dense
ornament and symbols; black, white and red.

**Lineage.** Punk détournement and the RCA's typographic experiment; a link between grunge-era type
and political design.

**Quotes.** He describes a major influence on his work as "an inner anger which is a response to
all the unfairness that is in this world," and his ambition as using "design as a weapon for
social change." (Quoted in the Wikipedia article "Jonathan Barnbrook.")

**Remix today.** Barnbrook's typefaces show that a font can be an opinion. Naming and shaping type
politically is still rare, and still powerful.

### Stefan Sagmeister {#stefan-sagmeister}

**Austrian designer, born 1962, based in New York.** Sagmeister studied at the University of
Applied Arts in Vienna and, on a Fulbright, at Pratt. He worked for Leo Burnett in Hong Kong and
for M&Co before founding Sagmeister Inc. in 1993, designing for Lou Reed, David Byrne, Talking
Heads and the Rolling Stones. He partnered with Jessica Walsh from 2011 to 2019. He won Grammys for
the Talking Heads box set *Once in a Lifetime* (2005) and for Byrne and Eno's *Everything That
Happens Will Happen Today* (2010).

**Principles.** *The hand over the font:* lettering cut, built or written physically, so the
labour and the risk show (AIGA Detroit lecture poster, 1999, its text cut into his own skin and
photographed). *Hide and reveal:* packaging whose image changes when taken apart (Lou Reed, *Set
the Twilight Reeling*, 1996, in a dark-tinted case). *Design about your own life:* personal maxims
turned into public typography (*Things I Have Learned in My Life So Far*, 2008).

**Key works.** *Set the Twilight Reeling*, 1996; AIGA Detroit poster, 1999; *Things I Have Learned
in My Life So Far*, 2008; *The Happy Show*, 2012.

**Visual vocabulary.** Handwritten and handmade lettering; type built from objects and bodies;
photography of physical typography.

**Lineage.** Kalman's M&Co into a personal, confessional design practice; a bridge from grunge's
intensity to the 2000s craft revival.

**Remix today.** Sagmeister's handmade type is the counter-move to perfect digital output; in an
age of generated images, visible effort and physical risk are again persuasive.

### Why Not Associates {#why-not-associates}

**British design studio, founded 1987.** Founded in London by Royal College of Art graduates
including Andy Altmann and David Ellis, Why Not took the experimental typography of the late 1980s
into commissioned work: exhibition graphics, advertising, title sequences and, increasingly,
public space. Their *Comedy Carpet* in Blackpool, with artist Gordon Young, is a 40 × 200 metre
plaza of more than 160,000 granite letters setting over a thousand jokes and catchphrases; it took
five years and was opened in 2011.

**Principles.** *Type at architectural scale:* typography as floor, readable close up and from
the top of Blackpool Tower (*Comedy Carpet*, 2011). *Experiment for clients:* layered, kinetic type
applied to commissions (graphics for the Royal Academy's *Sensation* exhibition, 1997).

**Key works.** *Sensation* exhibition graphics, 1997; *Comedy Carpet*, 2011.

**Visual vocabulary.** Layered type in motion; music-hall and letterpress showbill typography in
the *Comedy Carpet*; extreme scale contrasts.

**Lineage.** RCA experiment and the grunge-era page into motion and environmental typography,
alongside the London collective Tomato (founded 1991).

**Remix today.** Why Not's trajectory, from layered pages to type you walk on, is a reminder that
experimental typography belongs in physical space as much as on screens.

### The Designers Republic {#the-designers-republic}

**British design studio, Sheffield, founded 1986.** Ian Anderson started tDR to make flyers for a
band he managed; sculptor Nick Phillips joined him. Early work for Age of Chance and Pop Will Eat
Itself (whose identity bastardised the Pepsi logo) was made by hand with photocopiers and craft
knives. In the 1990s tDR became the visual voice of Warp Records (Autechre, Aphex Twin, LFO) and
designed the identity, packaging and manual for Psygnosis's PlayStation game *Wipeout* (1995).
*Emigre* devoted an issue to them in 1994. The company went into voluntary liquidation in January
2009 and was relaunched in smaller form.

**Principles.** *Brand the anti-brand:* corporate logos and slogans ("Work Buy Consume Die," "Buy
nothing, pay now") turned on consumerism, including a fake corporation, Pho-Ku (1990s).
*Maximum-minimalism:* clean technical layouts overloaded with icons, codes and anime until
restraint becomes excess (*Wipeout*, 1995). *North of Nowhere:* Sheffield ("SoYo") pride against
London's Soho.

**Key works.** Pop Will Eat Itself, *Very Metal Noise Pollution*, 1989; *Emigre* issue on tDR,
1994; *Wipeout*, 1995.

**Visual vocabulary.** Pictograms, barcodes and warning labels; Japanese type and anime; bright
techno colour and silver; dense micro-typography.

**Lineage.** Constructivism (Anderson's first reference) and punk into rave-era corporate parody;
an ancestor of today's "techno-brutal" and Y2K revival graphics. Alumni include Michael C. Place
(Build) and Matt Pyke (Universal Everything).

**Remix today.** tDR's trick, adopting the full visual grammar of corporate identity to say
something its client couldn't say directly, is perfect for brand systems that want irony without
losing rigour.

### Ken Garland {#ken-garland}

**British designer, writer and educator, 1929–2021.** Garland is placed here because the 1999
reissue of his manifesto is a defining event of the grunge era, though his own design practice was
clear-cut British modernism. He studied at the Central School of Arts and Crafts (graduating 1954,
with classmates including Alan Fletcher and Colin Forbes), was art editor of *Design* magazine from
1956 to 1962, then founded Ken Garland & Associates (1962), whose clients included Galt Toys and
the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament. He wrote First Things First on 29 November 1963; it was
published in 1964 and reprinted in *The Guardian*. In 1999 *Adbusters* relaunched it as First
Things First 2000, signed by 33 designers and critics including Barnbrook, Boom, Kalman, Licko,
McCoy and VanderLans, and published simultaneously in *Adbusters*, *Emigre* 51, *Eye* 33, the *AIGA
Journal*, *Blueprint* and *Items*.

**Principles.** *First things first:* design skill belongs first to useful communication (signs,
books, education, science), not consumer selling (First Things First, 1964). *Clear form for a
cause:* plain, bold modernist graphics for campaigns (CND "Aldermaston to London Easter 62"
poster, 1962). *Work is a team effort:* his studio's output was collective, and he later argued
for designer-client partnership over "us and them" ("Last Things Last," *Eye* 83, 2012).

**Key works.** CND Easter march posters, 1962; First Things First, 1964; *Graphics Handbook*,
1966; *Mr Beck's Underground Map*, 1994.

**Visual vocabulary.** Bold sans serif, flat colour, simple diagrammatic forms; documentary
photography in his later books.

**Lineage.** Swiss-influenced British modernism; the manifesto links 1960s critical theory to the
1990s culture-jamming of *Adbusters*.

**Quotes.** "…we have reached a saturation point at which the high pitched scream of consumer
selling is no more than sheer noise. We think that there are other things more worth using our
skill and experience on." Ken Garland et al., First Things First, 1964. The 2000 version
addressed "unprecedented environmental, social and cultural crises."

**Remix today.** The question in First Things First has never been settled, and every designer
working on attention-optimised products in 2026 is answering it, whether they mean to or not.

## Neo-modernist Revival {#new-swiss-revival}

### Context

Amsterdam, Arnhem, Zurich and Lausanne, from the late 1990s. After a decade of grunge and digital
expressionism, a generation trained in the Netherlands and Switzerland returned to grids, grotesque
type, black-and-white and plain construction, not as corporate style but as a critical, even
political, language. Experimental Jetset (founded 1997) read modernism through punk and Provo; in
Zurich, Lineto (founded 1993 by Cornel Windlin and Stephan Müller) became the foundry for a new
Swiss type, publishing Norm's Simple (2001) and Replica (2008) and Laurenz Brunner's Akkurat
(2004). Karel Martens's Werkplaats Typografie in Arnhem trained designers in a research-led,
modernist-rooted practice, and Irma Boom made the book an architectural object. Gary Hustwit's
documentary *Helvetica* (2007), featuring Experimental Jetset and Norm, marked the revival's arrival
as a public taste. By the 2010s, its grotesques (Akkurat, Circular, Unica's digital revival of
2014) had become the default of tech and culture branding.

### Principles

1. **Helvetica as a found material.** A neutral grotesque used without irony and without
   decoration; meaning comes from scale, rhythm and repetition. *Example:* Experimental Jetset,
   John & Paul & Ringo & George T-shirt, 2001.
2. **The responsive identity.** A mark is a system that changes shape to fit its context.
   *Example:* Experimental Jetset, Whitney Museum "Responsive W," 2013.
3. **The book as architecture.** Scale, thickness, edges and binding carry the content.
   *Example:* Irma Boom, *SHV Think Book 1996-1896*, 1996.
4. **Construct the letter.** Typefaces rebuilt from stated rules, a modernist method producing
   deliberately odd results. *Example:* Norm, Simple (Lineto), 2001.

### Key works

- Irma Boom, *SHV Think Book 1996-1896*, 1996
- Experimental Jetset, John & Paul & Ringo & George, 2001
- Norm, Simple, 2001
- Laurenz Brunner, Akkurat (Lineto), 2004
- Irma Boom, *Sheila Hicks: Weaving as Metaphor*, 2006
- Experimental Jetset, Whitney Museum identity, 2013

### Visual vocabulary

- **Type:** Helvetica, Akzidenz and new grotesques (Akkurat, Replica, Simple); large, tightly set
  headlines; often one size and one weight per piece.
- **Colour:** black, white, one primary (Experimental Jetset's red and blue); in Boom's books,
  colour as material (edges, paper, embossing).
- **Grid:** strict and visible, frequently used as the subject of the work.
- **Imagery:** little or none; typography carries the work; when images appear, plainly placed.
- **Composition:** flush-left, asymmetric, generous white space, systemic variation.

### Lineage

From `swiss-style` and `dutch-modernism` (Crouwel, Total Design), with `punk` and post-punk
attitude and the experimental rigour of `new-wave`. It fed the 2010s grotesque-sans branding of
technology companies and museums, and the design-systems thinking of the screen era.

### Quotes

- Irma Boom: "Somebody once said that I'm building books. I really like that expression very
  much." (Quoted in the Wikipedia article "Irma Boom.")

### Remix today

The revival's lesson is that *a style is not its politics*. The same grotesque can be corporate or
critical depending on how it's used. A 2026 designer can take its systemic thinking (the
responsive mark, the rule-based typeface, the book as an object) without inheriting the bland
"tech sans" that its success produced.

### Experimental Jetset {#experimental-jetset}

**Dutch design studio, Amsterdam, founded 1997.** Marieke Stolk (born 1967), Danny van den Dungen
(1971) and Erwin Brinkers (1973) met at the Gerrit Rietveld Academie and named the studio after
Sonic Youth's 1994 album *Experimental Jet Set, Trash and No Star*. They work mainly in printed
matter, museum installations and identities, almost always in Helvetica and a restricted palette.
They guest-edited an issue of *Emigre* and appear in *Helvetica* (2007).

**Principles.** *Restricted means:* typography alone, mostly Helvetica, in black, white, red and
blue (John & Paul & Ringo & George, 2001). *Modernism plus punk:* De Stijl and Provo read through
post-punk; modernism as a way of making, not a corporate look (the studio's name, 1997). *A line
that responds:* an identity as one rule that scales to any format (Whitney "Responsive W," 2013).

**Key works.** John & Paul & Ringo & George, 2001; Whitney Museum identity, 2013; *Statement and
Counter-Statement: Notes on Experimental Jetset*, Roma Publications, 2017.

**Visual vocabulary.** Helvetica; heavy rules; black, white, red, blue; typographic systems with
no imagery.

**Lineage.** De Stijl, Crouwel and Provo, crossed with punk; central to the 2000s neo-modernist
revival.

**Remix today.** The Whitney's Responsive W shows how an identity can be a behaviour rather than a
picture, which is exactly how screen-era brand systems need to work.

### Irma Boom {#irma-boom}

**Dutch book designer, born 1960.** Boom studied at the AKI academy in Enschede and spent five and
a half years at the Dutch Government Publishing and Printing Office, where her experimental Dutch
stamp books of 1987–1988 drew hate mail from collectors. She founded her Amsterdam office in 1991,
has been a critic at Yale since 1992, and has designed more than 500 books. She was the youngest
recipient of Leipzig's Gutenberg Prize (2001). She signed First Things First 2000.

**Principles.** *Build the book:* thickness, edges, scale and binding carry the content, worked out
first as tiny models (*SHV Think Book 1996-1896*, 1996: 2,136 pages, five years in the making).
*Remove the expected:* no page numbers, no index, reverse chronology, a white cover that reveals
its title as it gets dirty (SHV, 1996). *Address other senses:* inkless embossed pages and frayed,
sawn edges (*N°5 Culture Chanel*, 2013; *Sheila Hicks: Weaving as Metaphor*, 2006).

**Key works.** *SHV Think Book*, 1996; *Sheila Hicks: Weaving as Metaphor*, 2006 (gold medal,
"Most Beautiful Book in the World," Leipzig, 2007); *Irma Boom: The Architecture of the Book*,
2010; *N°5 Culture Chanel*, 2013.

**Visual vocabulary.** Extreme formats (fat, tiny, huge); printed edges; embossing and die-cuts;
colour as material; minimal covers.

**Lineage.** Dutch modernist book design (Crouwel, Martens, Dumbar) taken into sculpture.

**Quotes.** "I never talk about clients, by the way. I have 'commissioners'." (Quoted in the
Wikipedia article "Irma Boom.")

**Remix today.** In a screen era, Boom's insistence that a book must be something a PDF cannot is
the strongest argument for physical design: make the object do what the file can't.

### Norm {#norm}

**Swiss design studio, Zurich, founded 1999.** Dimitri Bruni and Manuel Krebs (later joined by
Ludovic Varone) work in typography, books and identities with a strict, rule-based method and a
dry sense of humour. Their Simple typeface, designed independently and adapted for Ruedi Baur's
signage at Cologne Bonn Airport, and Replica, used for VH1's 2012 rebrand, are published by Lineto.
The Museum für Gestaltung Zürich exhibited their typefaces in 2020.

**Principles.** *Rules generate form:* a typeface comes from a stated construction system, so its
oddities belong to the system (Simple, 2001). *Modernism with a wink:* rigorous Swiss method with
slight postmodern references (Replica, 2008).

**Key works.** Simple, 2001; Replica, 2008.

**Visual vocabulary.** Geometric, constructed grotesques with clipped corners; systematic
layouts; black and white.

**Lineage.** Swiss modernism via the Lineto circle (Windlin, Brunner); part of the neo-modern
revival shown in *Helvetica* (2007).

**Remix today.** Norm's approach maps directly onto parametric and variable type design: write the
rule first, accept what it produces.
