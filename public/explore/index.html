Vert BarsCONCEPT 06 · BAR-FIELD RECONSTRUCTION
v6 Hold the tempo at the top and the whole thing goes off Everything v5 does, plus an easter egg. Peg the tempo dial at its ceiling and hold it there for a second and the mark goes full rainbow rave. It has to be held rather than touched: the tempo keeps its value after you let go of a letter, so a trigger that simply fired at the maximum would latch the first time anyone found it and never come off again, which makes it a permanent mode rather than a secret. Charge builds while the dial is pegged and drains the moment it isn't, and ignition is a threshold on that charge rather than the charge itself, so holding is a build in which nothing happens and then it goes. It comes in fast and unwinds slowly, because a drop should land but cutting it dead the instant the tempo dips reads as a bug. The rainbow is a sweep across the word with per-column scatter on top, drifting on musical time so it rolls through the letters at a rate the tempo sets. Picking each column's hue from its own hash was the first attempt and it read as coloured static — a rainbow is a gradient, and the eye wants to see the spectrum travel somewhere. Every hue is then pulled toward a common luminance, because HSL at a fixed lightness is nowhere near equally bright across the wheel: green and yellow carry several times the luminance of blue and violet, so a raw sweep came out as a lime field with occasional other colours in it. Only partially pulled, since full normalisation multiplies blue by nearly six and it blooms into a smear. The hard part was not the rainbow but keeping it. Reinhard tonemapping runs per channel, so anything bright enough drives all three channels toward one and arrives white however saturated it started — turn everything up, as the brief said, and the party bleaches itself out. Turning brightness down instead produced a technically colourful picture that was dim and murky and not remotely a party. The answer was to stop fighting the tonemap and restore the chroma on the far side of it, which is what finally made it read as neon; ahead of that, white is still the enemy, so the redline is the one moment the white core glow gets turned down. Legibility was the other argument with the brief. Taken literally, dialling everything up deletes the logo: the first pass added a third of gap noise, doubled the edge jitter and forced full agitation, and the result was confetti with no readable OMAR anywhere in it. Oversized bloom did as much damage as the geometry, closing up the O and A counters and filling in the M's valley — the mark went illegible from light rather than from shape, which took a while to spot. So the terms that change speed get the big multipliers and the terms that change shape get a fraction of them, and what you see is clearly OMAR clearly losing its mind. Every one of those multipliers is applied on the way into the shader and never written back into the dials, so igniting the party doesn't stomp the settings and the sliders keep saying what you set them to. The sound stacks: at the redline all four grooves play at once, any voice any groove asks for on a step firing together, which takes a bar from seventeen hits to fifty-seven and is what makes it feel faster than the dial can go without touching the tempo — the floor groove's ghost hats already cover all sixteen sixteenths, so the union is double-time. Raising the actual rate would have dragged the visualiser's beat clock and the HUD's bar out of step with it. Borrowed hits sit slightly under the settled groove's own, so the letter you are on stays accented through the wall; bringing them in at full made all four grooves identical and moving between letters stopped changing anything, which meant the party had eaten the thing the letters were for. The kick is the other exception and is pinned to four-on-the-floor rather than joining the union, from every groove including the broken one — half genre, since a rave kick is on the floor and four stacked kick patterns are mud, and half guardrail, because the field flashes on every kick the sequencer books and the union would have put that over three flashes a second. The master filter swings about three octaves open, which drives the soft clipper sitting behind it, and a riser rebuilds every second bar. The chrome stays deadpan through all of it: the HUD, the gauges and the tagline hold their acid yellow and keep soberly reporting numbers while the field behind them loses its mind, which is funnier than joining in and keeps the readouts legible. It acknowledges the party in exactly two places — the tempo gauge relabels itself HOLD while charging and REDLINE once lit, and the corner badge swaps its mode from z-space to redline. The top eighth of the tempo track is hatched in a colour that isn't the instrument's, which is the entire affordance: an easter egg with no affordance is dead code, since nobody maxes a dial they have no reason to max, and this says something is here without saying what. The charge bar beside it exists because pegging the dial and waiting otherwise looks like nothing happening, and a second of no feedback is easily long enough to let go. v5 One letter expands, the word makes room for it An accordion rather than an orbit. The claimed letter expands where it stands and the other three hold the baseline, sliding away from it — left goes further left, right goes further right — far enough to clear the room it needs, plus a widening gap per step so the word breathes apart instead of shuffling over. Nothing goes anywhere, but nothing is still either: each of the others travels in and out of depth on the spot on its own period, with size and depth moving together so it reads as distance rather than arbitrary growth. They float vertically as well, on a second period at an unrelated ratio to the depth so the drift never settles into an obvious loop, and their shape is calmed while it happens: the claimed letter takes the whole of the field's deformation and the other three take about a third of it. At full strength the fbm warp and the travelling gap noise chew a small letter into pieces, so the three that were not the subject ended up doing more waving than the one that was. Only the two deformations that carry time are calmed — the fixed per-column edge jitter stays at full, because it cannot wave and it is the ragged silhouette itself; calming it too flattened the unclaimed letters into plain rectangles. So what is taken away is the waving and what is left, and increased, is the movement: depth, float and parallax are all position rather than shape. The calming fades in with the claim, so the resting word is untouched. Because they cross in depth the four layers are composited in depth order rather than summed, since adding makes an overlap read as brighter, the exact opposite of behind. The group is re-centred and fitted from its real projected boxes, so claiming an outer letter doesn't walk the word off one side and a narrow window tightens the gaps rather than shoving letters off screen. The pointer tests against each letter's slot in the settled word rather than where it currently is, since hit-testing the live box would move a letter out from under the cursor and oscillate forever. At rest the word is completely still and carries no chrome at all — nothing reacts until you are on a specific letter, and the tagline sits settled underneath it. Scanning the tagline out of bars was never an option: at this column density a lowercase letter gets two or three bars, so legibility would need letters wide enough to be a second wordmark. It is real type instead, withdrawing as you engage, because it belongs to the settled presentation layer rather than to the gesture. Set in neutral white it read as browser default text laid over a designed thing, so it now wears the instrument's own yellow — the same colour as the detection frames, the gauges and the corner badge — and hangs on a dimension line rather than sitting on an axis. The line spans the wordmark's exact width, from the left edge of the O to the right edge of the R, ticked at each end and broken in the middle for the words: it measures the mark instead of merely sitting beneath it, which is the geometric tie a centred line never had. It is also the one piece of real type in the sketch that answers the music. The tracking opens on each kick, so the phrase breathes wider and the two rules give and take to make room for it; the rules brighten and dim with the top end, on the same signed deviations the bars use, so they move either side of a resting value rather than only ever getting brighter. Every term is scaled by how much music there is, which means with the sound off it is perfectly still — a settled state should stay a settled state, and it measures as zero movement to three decimal places. The window for all of this is the tail rather than the gesture. Holding the tagline visible while a letter is claimed was tried and abandoned: at a third of its strength over the expanded letter's wall of lit bars it was unreadable mud, the same problem the gauges needed a scrim for. It withdraws entirely instead, and because the chrome falls in a few hundred milliseconds while the sound takes ten seconds to go, letting go leaves the word snapping back to crisp with the tagline as the last thing still moving, breathing the track down to silence. The letterforms themselves were also rebuilt — cut out of a single block rather than assembled from abutting pieces, since a union of signed distance fields that merely touch reports a phantom edge and the bar field antialiases a dark break into every stroke crossing it. This version also has sound, synthesised rather than sampled so the folder stays self-contained. It is a drum record: kick, claps, hats, shaker, toms and ride carry it, with a deep sine sub and short filtered chord stabs sitting underneath rather than on top, and a sidechain that flattens everything tuned on every kick. Each letter owns a whole groove rather than just a chord — the floor at the O, a broken kick with tribal toms at the M, sixteenth hats and doubled claps at the A, and a half-time drop-out at the R — so the drums lift and fall as you move across the word. An earlier pass changed only the harmony between letters, which turned out to be inaudible under a pumping mix; it also had a detuned square arp and a resonant saw bass, which is where a thin piped quality was coming from. Two grooves run at once and an equal-power crossfade walks between them over half a bar, so nothing is ever switched: the hats you were hearing thin out as the toms arrive, the change is booked a couple of sixteenths ahead, and a riser swells into it. The version before that marked a change by cutting the master filter to 600 Hz on the downbeat, which put a hole in the music at the one moment it has to carry you across. The claimed letter also carries a music readout in its HUD — the chord and the groove it's in, or both groove names mid-crossfade, over a live bar of sixteenths drawn as tall as the number of voices landing on each one, so you can watch the hats fill the bar in at the A and drain out of it at the R. Each letter is also a handle onto one control in the mix: press it and drag up for more, down for less, with the letter travelling under the pointer in both axes because a handle that follows your hand is what makes it feel like an object rather than an invisible modifier on a hover. The O sets the pace, the M the dirt, the A the weight of the drums, the R how loud any of it is — so the word as a whole is the mixing desk. One dial each rather than one dial reachable four ways, which gives the four letters separate jobs instead of making them interchangeable. Every letter carries its own gauge beside it, in two states: a dim one that comes up with the hover and exists only to say there is something here to pull, and a lit one that follows the drag — ticks and a larger number arrive, the hint carets leave. Because the gesture is the same for all four the frame is too, one vertical track and readout in the same place each time; what changes is the glyph beneath it, and that is the part that says what kind of quantity you have hold of. Four quarter-note lights for the tempo, so a rate is shown as a rate. A wave that squares off for the grit, which is the shape of a soft clipper's own transfer curve. Struck bars on a floor for the drums, as tall as the weight you have set and flashing on each kick. A segmented staircase for the volume, the one gauge everybody already knows how to read. Four different widgets would have made one gesture look like four. Each gauge sits on a dark scrim, because thin line work over a wall of lit bars is unreadable at any opacity, and it flips to the other side of the letter when it would otherwise run under the dial panel. A ring also leaves the held letter on every quarter note and fades as it grows — the pulse of the track rather than a readout of any one dial, so it belongs to all four. The ring takes its phase from the audio clock rather than the frame clock, so it cannot drift away from the kick. An earlier pass drove grit off the same grab, sideways; two axes on one gesture was one more thing than it wanted, and the second was hard to avoid nudging while aiming the first. Releasing is treated as the same event as moving away — the letter walks back to its place — because holding it at the drop point meant hit-testing it where it now was rather than in its slot, and the moment those two disagreed the hover flickered between them. Leaving the word altogether is a ten-second fade rather than a stop, with the clock still running underneath it, since the letters are tuned to snap back the instant you leave and following that straight down made the music sound like the record being switched off. Click the pill to start it — a browser will not open an audio context without a real gesture, and pointer movement does not count as one. The strokes themselves are now the visualiser. They always moved; here they move on the music's clock instead of a wall clock — a counter of beats rather than seconds, so the tempo you drag sets the rate of the jitter as well as the rate of the drums, running from 15Hz at the bottom of the dial to 23Hz at the top, with the amount rising alongside it. The amount comes off the mix: an analyser tapped after the master fade splits it four ways and each column answers one band, picked by the column's own hash rather than by its position, because mapping frequency left-to-right would have turned the wordmark into a literal EQ display. The bands are handed over as signed deviations from their own recent average, so a quiet band pulls its columns down exactly as far as a loud one pushes them up; a first pass passed absolute levels and measured out as a third more brightness everywhere rather than as movement, closing up the A's counter. Each band also finds its own floor and ceiling live, because loudness falls off so steeply with frequency that one fixed window pinned the bottom end at full scale for every groove while leaving the hats at nothing — the two bands that most distinguish one groove from another, both saying nothing. The kick is the exception: rather than hunt for it in the spectrum, where a continuous sub sits in the same few bins and keeps the bottom end permanently hot, the sequencer reports the kicks it books, so the field breathes exactly on the drum. That comes out as four hits a bar at the O, six at the broken M and two at the R — the letters visibly breathing at different rates. With the sound off every one of these terms is zero and the field is precisely what it was before any of it existed. v4 Four letters, four depths, hover-triggered detection O, M, A and R become independent layers, each with its own z, drift and roll. At rest they converge on the exact logotype; move the pointer and they separate into depth, with parallax reversing across the focal plane so near and far letters slide opposite ways. Hovering pulls a letter forward, agitates its bars, and flickers in its detection frame with live coordinates and a depth readout. Scaling is deliberately shallow — on a mark this wide, depth has to be carried by tone and parallax rather than size. 12 SEP 2026 → v3 Rebuilt on WebGPU — analytic SDF mark, real bloom chain Same look, rebuilt on vgpu (vgpu.sh). The OMAR mark is now an analytic signed-distance field in WGSL instead of a rasterised bitmap mask, so it stays crisp at any scale and warps without resampling. Bloom is a genuine five-pass target chain — HDR field, luminance threshold, separable gaussian, tone-mapped composite — rather than a canvas blur filter. Runs at 60fps with the dials live. 12 SEP 2026 → v2 OMAR mark as blob field + live dials Uses the real OMAR logotype as the silhouette, rendered in the magenta blob treatment. Offscreen mask sampling, denser columns, dual-pass bloom, and a dial panel for density, bloom, warp, color, and HUD — so the look can be tuned live. 12 SEP 2026 → v1 Silhouette → vertical capsules → detection HUD First pass recreating Oğuz Yağız Kara’s bar-field look: sample an SDF (shield or organic blobs) into columns, draw rounded vertical capsules with noise jitter and bloom, then overlay faux computer-vision boxes and coordinate labels. Two modes — warm shield on white, magenta blobs on dark. 12 SEP 2026 →
